# Entitlement and Site Risk

Last updated: 2026-09-01

Scope: U.S. ground-up development and heavy redevelopment across multifamily, industrial, retail, office, and mixed-use. This is an educational reference for screening land use, environmental, and site-condition risk before and during predevelopment. It is not legal, environmental, engineering, tax, or investment advice. Land use is governed locally under state enabling statutes, so procedures, hearing calendars, notice periods, and fee schedules vary by jurisdiction. Confirm every threshold with local land use counsel, the current municipal code, and licensed environmental and geotechnical professionals. For stabilized-operations assumptions once the asset is delivered, use the multifamily, industrial, office, and retail packs.

---

## Current Context

- Legislatively adopted impact fees are not immune from constitutional scrutiny. In Sheetz v. County of El Dorado (decided 2024-04-12, unanimous), the Supreme Court held the Takings Clause does not distinguish legislative from administrative permit conditions, so the Nollan essential nexus and Dolan rough proportionality tests reach legislative monetary exactions. The Court expressly left open how individualized a condition imposed on a class of properties must be.
- Regulatory cost is a large and growing share of development budgets. NAHB reported (2026-06-09) that regulation adds $131,734 to an average new single-family home, 26.4% of a $499,500 average price, of which $46,795 (9.4%) lands at the land development stage. NAHB and NMHC previously put regulation at 40.6% of total multifamily development cost (survey fielded April 2022, 49 usable responses). Directional, dated, and not universal.
- Community opposition is a measurable schedule item, not just noise. The same 2022 NAHB/NMHC survey attributed an average 5.6% of total development cost and 7.4 months of delay to opposition, separate from regulation itself.
- Federal wetland permitting reset in 2026. The Corps published its final nationwide permit action 2026-01-08; the 2026 NWPs take effect 2026-03-15 and expire 2031-03-15. NWP 39 (commercial and institutional developments) now expressly lists data centers and pharmaceutical manufacturing, while keeping the 1/2-acre loss cap and pre-construction notification requirement.
- California narrowed CEQA for infill housing. AB 130, signed and effective 2025-06-30, created a statutory exemption (Pub. Res. Code 21080.66) for housing-rich infill projects, and SB 131 limits review for near-miss projects to the condition that defeated the exemption. This is California-only; do not generalize it.

## Approval Pathways and What Each One Costs You

| Pathway | What it is | Discretion | Practical risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| By-right / ministerial | Use and form already permitted; staff confirms compliance | None or minimal | Schedule and plan-check cost only |
| Administrative site plan review | Staff or planning board reviews layout, access, landscaping | Conditions can be imposed | Conditions of approval become hard cost; state statutes often set a decision clock |
| Variance | Relief from a dimensional standard on hardship findings | Quasi-judicial | Hardship findings are narrow and appealable |
| Conditional / special use permit | Use allowed if conditions are met | Quasi-judicial | Denial is possible on the record; conditions can be onerous |
| Rezoning / map amendment | Legislative change of the district | Legislative | Highest political risk; no entitlement until appeal and referendum periods run |
| Planned unit development (PUD) | Negotiated flexible district, typically two-stage preliminary then final plan | Legislative or conditional | Negotiation and discretion are structural, per APA PAS Report 545 (2007) |
| Density bonus / incentive program | Extra density in exchange for affordability | Largely mandatory relief with defined denial findings | State-specific; concessions can still be denied on findings |

Statutory clocks exist but are local. New York Town Law 274-a, for example, lets a planning board impose reasonable conditions directly related to a site plan, requires 10 days' notice to the applicant and 5 days' published notice, and requires a decision within 62 days of application or of the close of hearing, extendable only by consent. Read the equivalent provision in the governing state and code; do not assume this one.

There is no defensible national default for months-to-entitlement by approval type. This library does not publish one. Build the schedule from the local code, the hearing calendar, and comparable recent approvals in the same jurisdiction.

## Exactions, Impact Fees, and Inclusionary Requirements

| Item | What to confirm | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Impact fees | Current adopted schedule, basis (per unit, per sf, per trip), timing of payment, escalation | Payment at permit vs at certificate of occupancy changes carry cost materially |
| Linkage fees | Whether commercial or residential square footage triggers an affordable housing or transit linkage payment | Common in high-cost coastal jurisdictions |
| Land dedication / fee in lieu | Whether right-of-way, park, or school land must be dedicated or left unbuilt | NAHB/NMHC 2022: present for 51.0% of respondents, averaging 4.7% of total development cost when present |
| Site-work-stage fees and studies | Fees and required studies charged when site work begins | NAHB/NMHC 2022: reported by 98.0% of respondents, averaging 8.7% of total development cost when present |
| Building permit stage fees | Permit fees plus utility hook-up fees | NAHB/NMHC 2022: 95.9% of respondents, averaging 4.6% |
| Inclusionary requirement | Set-aside percentage, affordability depth, term, on-site vs fee in lieu | NAHB/NMHC 2022: present in 38.8% of projects, averaging 6.9% of cost when present, with those respondents raising rents 7.6% on average |
| Density bonus offset | Whether affordability unlocks density, concessions, waivers, and parking relief | California's State Density Bonus Law (HCD fact sheet, April 2026) grants 20-50% more density for 5% very low, 10% low, or 10% for-sale moderate units, 80% for qualifying 100% affordable projects, and 1 to 5 concessions by tier |

All percentages above are directional survey figures from a small sample and are dated. Actual fee amounts are set locally and must be pulled from the current adopted schedule. After Sheetz, a fee that lacks an essential nexus or is not roughly proportional to the project's impact is challengeable even when adopted by ordinance, though the Court did not decide how individualized a class-wide condition must be.

## Environmental Site Review

| Item | Federal threshold or standard | What it means on a site |
|---|---|---|
| Phase I ESA / All Appropriate Inquiries | 40 CFR Part 312; ASTM E1527-21 (E2247-23 for forestland or rural) | Supports the bona fide prospective purchaser, innocent landowner, and contiguous property owner defenses under CERCLA |
| Phase I shelf life | Conducted or updated within 1 year of acquisition; interviews, records review, visual inspection, and lien search within 180 days | A stale Phase I needs the 180-day components redone, not just a cover letter |
| Phase II subsurface investigation | No single national trigger | Driven by recognized environmental conditions and the environmental professional's judgment; keep scope and cost case-by-case |
| Emerging contaminants | E1527-21 treats PFAS and similar as non-scope considerations | Include where the state regulates them; state programs vary |
| Wetlands and waters | Clean Water Act Section 404; avoid, then minimize, then compensate | USACE issues permits, EPA sets criteria and holds veto authority |
| Nationwide Permit 39 | 1/2 acre non-tidal waters loss cap; 300 linear feet of stream bed; pre-construction notification required | Crossing the cap drops the project into an individual permit with public notice |
| Flood | 44 CFR 60.3: permit for all SFHA development; new residential lowest floor at or above BFE; non-residential may elevate or dry floodproof; floodway encroachment requires no rise | Many communities adopt freeboard above the federal minimum; that is local |
| Endangered species | ESA Section 10 incidental take permit with a Habitat Conservation Plan for non-federal projects | The HCP itself triggers NEPA and public review; duration is project-specific and long |
| Construction stormwater | NPDES permit for 1 acre or more disturbed, or under 1 acre within a larger common plan of development or sale; SWPPP before the Notice of Intent | Most states run their own authorized program, so the operative permit is usually a state general permit |

## Utilities, Offsite Obligations, and Geotechnical

- Will-serve letters are written confirmations from water, sewer, electric, and gas providers that capacity exists and will be committed to the project. Practitioner guidance (Area Development, Q2 2024) treats getting them early, alongside right-of-way acquisition, as core site-risk control. Whether a jurisdiction requires them as a filing prerequisite is local; confirm it.
- A capacity statement is not a reservation. Check for expiration dates, capacity reservation fees, and whether the commitment survives an entitlement extension.
- Offsite obligations (road widening, signals, turn lanes, sewer upsizing, lift stations, dedications) are typically documented in a development agreement or subdivision improvement agreement with bonding or letter-of-credit security. Underwrite them as hard cost with their own schedule, not as contingency.
- Geotechnical investigation is required by IBC Section 1803, with a defined scope covering soil classification, slope stability, bearing capacity, moisture variation, compressibility, liquefaction potential, and expansiveness. The building official may waive it only narrowly, such as where satisfactory adjacent data exists or the structure is unoccupied.
- A soil is expansive under IBC 1803 if it has a plasticity index of 15 or greater (ASTM D4318), more than 10% passing a No. 200 sieve, and more than 10% of particles under 5 micrometers, or alternatively an expansion index above 20 (ASTM D4829).
- Redevelopment sites carry geotechnical exposures a Phase I will not find: undocumented fill, prior foundations and slabs, abandoned utilities, and buried tanks. Budget exploratory work separately.

## Historic, Design, and State Environmental Review

- Section 106 of the National Historic Preservation Act applies to federal undertakings, which include projects receiving federal financial assistance, permits, licenses, or approvals. The process is at 36 CFR Part 800, administered by the ACHP with the State Historic Preservation Office. A purely private project with no federal nexus is outside Section 106 but can still face local landmark and design review.
- Screen for a federal nexus early. Federal funding, a loan guarantee, or a Section 404 individual permit can pull NEPA and Section 106 into a project that otherwise faced only local review.
- New York's SEQRA classifies actions as Type I (presumed more likely to have significant impact, full EAF, mandatory coordinated review), Type II (no further review), or Unlisted (short or full EAF, determination of significance required). NYSDEC's published process contemplates 30 days to designate a lead agency, 20 days for a determination of significance, 60 days to final scope, 45 days for draft EIS adequacy, a minimum 30-day comment period, and findings 30 days after the final EIS. These are the structure of one state's regime, not a national schedule.
- California's CEQA runs on a separate track with its own exemptions, negative declaration, and EIR paths, now supplemented by the AB 130 statutory infill exemption described above. Other states (for example Washington SEPA, Massachusetts MEPA) have their own regimes not covered here.

## Questions to Ask Before Committing Capital to a Site

- Is the intended program by-right under current zoning, or does it require a legislative or quasi-judicial approval?
- Who is the decision maker, what findings must they make, and what is the appeal and referendum window after approval?
- What recorded conditions, prior development agreements, annexation agreements, easements, or deed restrictions bind the site independent of zoning?
- What is the adopted impact fee schedule today, when is each fee payable, and does anything vest it?
- Does the site sit in an SFHA, and if so, is any part in the regulatory floodway?
- What did the Phase I identify, is it still within the 1-year and 180-day AAI windows, and does anything point to a Phase II?
- Are there wetlands, streams, or other waters on site, and does the design stay inside NWP 39's 1/2-acre and 300-linear-foot limits?
- Do we hold current will-serve letters for water, sewer, power, and gas, and do they expire before we expect to pull permits?
- What offsite improvements will be conditioned, who pays, and what security is required?
- Has a geotechnical investigation been done to IBC 1803 scope, and does it address expansive soil, liquefaction, and undocumented fill?
- Is there a federal nexus that triggers NEPA or Section 106?
- What is the organized opposition, and what is the litigation history of this decision maker and this neighborhood?

## Red Flags

- Land is under contract with no entitlement contingency, or with a deposit that goes hard before the discretionary approval and its appeal period.
- The pro forma treats a rezoning or PUD as if it were by-right, with no probability weighting and no alternative program.
- Impact fees are underwritten from a prior year's schedule, or the timing of payment is assumed to be at certificate of occupancy without confirming the code.
- Any part of the buildable area sits in the regulatory floodway, where the no-rise standard applies.
- The wetland delineation is stale, unverified by the Corps, or the design sits close enough to the 1/2-acre NWP 39 cap that a small change forces an individual permit.
- The Phase I is more than a year old at closing, or the 180-day components have lapsed and no update is budgeted.
- A recognized environmental condition is identified and the budget carries no Phase II, no remediation allowance, and no environmental insurance.
- Will-serve letters are missing, expired, or describe adequacy in general terms rather than committing capacity to this project.
- Offsite improvements sit in contingency instead of in the hard cost budget with their own schedule.
- No geotechnical investigation on a redevelopment site with prior structures, or a report that omits expansive soil and liquefaction findings.
- The schedule assumes the shortest statutory interval at every step of a state environmental review with a positive declaration.
- A large infill project underwrites the low per-project litigation rate. Litigation is rare per project but concentrates on large projects: the Rose Foundation-commissioned study (May 2023) found a 1.9% CEQA litigation rate for 2013-2021 and 508 cases filed 2019-2021, while Holland and Knight's Hernandez research (August 2022) reported 47,999 housing units targeted by CEQA suits filed in 2020. Both can be true; a big project belongs to the litigated population.
- Density bonus, concession, waiver, or inclusionary assumptions are imported from California into a state with no comparable statute.
