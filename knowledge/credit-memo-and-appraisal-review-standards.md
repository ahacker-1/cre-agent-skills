# Credit Memo and Appraisal Review Standards

Last updated: 2026-09-01

Scope: U.S. commercial real estate credit approval memoranda and collateral valuation review inside a regulated lender - banks, credit unions, and the credit functions that borrow their discipline. This is an educational credit reference, not legal, accounting, appraisal, or financing advice. Your institution's loan policy, your primary regulator, and your counsel control. Thresholds and supervisory limits below are quoted from federal banking agency sources; confirm the current text before relying on any of them in an examination or approval.

---

## Current Context

- The OCC's Spring 2026 Semiannual Risk Perspective release (2026-05-07) said credit conditions and refinancing risk in certain segments of CRE lending warrant ongoing monitoring, which keeps collateral support and exit assumptions under examiner attention.
- CBRE's H1 2026 U.S. Cap Rate Survey (published 2026-08-12) reported the all-property average cap rate essentially flat, about 60 percent of surveyed professionals expecting no change over the next six months, and the 10-year Treasury peaking at 4.67 percent in the period. Directional only; use it to judge whether an appraisal's rate assumption is stale, not as a benchmark cap rate.
- The CRE appraisal threshold has been $500,000 since the 2018 joint final rule raised it from $250,000; an evaluation may be used below it. Guidance written before 2018 still shows the old number, and the AVM quality control rule effective 2025-10-01 covers consumer principal dwellings, not CRE.
- The 2023 interagency Policy Statement on Prudent CRE Loan Accommodations and Workouts sets the valuation and credit file bar for modifications: updated borrower, sponsor, and guarantor financials, current valuations supporting the workout plan, and a documented credit assessment behind the accrual decision. Borrower-side workout mechanics live in [Workout and Extension Structures](knowledge/workout-and-extension-structures.md).

## Credit Approval Memo Structure

The OCC expects the loan file to hold an approval memorandum that documents the approval and gives approvers enough information for a fully informed credit decision, with executed loan documents consistent with that memo. Use [Underwriting Calculations](knowledge/underwriting-calc.md) for metric definitions and mechanics; this file does not restate formulas.

| Section | What it must answer | Most common omission |
|---|---|---|
| Request and recommendation | Amount, facility type, term, amortization, rate, purpose, recourse, and the ask being approved | The recommendation is buried instead of stated up front with conditions |
| Sources and uses | Total capitalization, hard equity amount, form, source, and timing of the borrower's contribution | Equity that is really a related-party loan, deferred fee, or land appreciation |
| Property, collateral, and market | Location, type, size, condition, occupancy, rent roll, lease rollover, capital needs, parcel and lien position; submarket supply, competing construction, absorption, effective rents net of concessions | Rollover inside the loan term shown but not carried into the cash flow; market data restated from the appraisal without independent challenge |
| Sponsor and guarantor | Global cash flow, liquidity, contingent liabilities, track record, other lender relationships | Guarantor net worth quoted with no liquidity or contingent liability analysis |
| Financial analysis | In-place and stabilized NOI, DSCR, debt yield, LTV, LTC, and the loan-sizing test that binds | Sizing off stabilized NOI only, with no in-place coverage shown |
| Stress and sensitivity | Sensitivity to interest rates, capitalization rates, vacancy, operating expenses, and absorption; refinance or takeout test | A single base case with no downside and no exit test |
| Risk rating | Proposed rating, the rationale, and how it maps to the institution's definitions | Rating asserted without tying it to the rating framework |
| Policy exceptions | Each exception named, quantified, and supported by written justification of the credit factors | Exceptions listed but not justified or not reported upward |
| Structure and conditions | Covenants, reserves, cash management, curtailment, re-margining, guaranties, reporting | Conditions that never make it into the loan documents |

## Documentation and Covenant Checklist

The agencies require documentation practices that let the lender make an informed decision, identify the purpose and repayment source, assess ability to repay, and keep claims legally enforceable.

| Category | Items the OCC lists as typical for a CRE loan file |
|---|---|
| Approval and financials | Approval memorandum; signed borrower and guarantor financial statements; property operating statements and rent rolls |
| Title and security | Title insurance policy; recorded mortgage or deed of trust; note; lease assignments; security agreement, with identical property descriptions across mortgage, security agreement, title policy, survey, and tax statement |
| Leases, insurance, valuation | Leases and executed tenant estoppels; insurance with proof of premium covering hazard, liability, and where appropriate loss of rents and flood; the appraisal or evaluation, the lender's own review of it, the engagement letter, and the appraiser's qualifications |
| Property and entity | Survey showing improvements, easements, and encroachments; organizational documents, borrowing resolutions, good standing certificates; proof taxes are paid and the collateral has its own parcel identification numbers; environmental reports as warranted |
| Construction add-ons | Construction loan agreement with draw conditions, budget, curtailments, release prices, and default events; contractor and borrower expertise; title policy updated with each advance; pro forma cash flows; as-is and as-completed or as-stabilized appraisals stating when stabilization is expected; plans, feasibility study, and cost budget; executed contracts; soil reports; foundation survey |

Covenant menu for income-producing CRE loans:

| Covenant | Typical use | Review question |
|---|---|---|
| DSCR | Ongoing performance test on the property or global | Are income and expenses defined in the documents, and does the covenant calculation differ from the underwriting DSCR? |
| Debt yield, LTV, LTC | Leverage, collateral margin, and cost discipline | Is debt yield measured on in-place NOI? What valuation refreshes LTV, and what is the cure - paydown, additional collateral, or re-margining? |
| Guarantor net worth and liquidity | Support for recourse and completion | Is liquidity defined net of contingent liabilities and pledged assets? |
| Reporting | Operating statements, rent rolls, borrower and guarantor financials | Frequency scaled to stability: annual can fit a stabilized, long-leased asset; lease-up or multi-tenant assets can warrant monthly, quarterly, or semiannual |
| Construction-specific | Completion, in-balance, curtailment, presale and preleasing, release prices | Are draws tied to verified improvements and a budget that stays in balance? |

Reporting covenants only work if the information is actually collected and analyzed on time; tracking receipt and analysis is part of sound credit administration. No numeric covenant floor is stated here as a default - the agencies require clear and measurable standards but do not publish CRE DSCR or debt yield minimums. See [Lender Credit Policy Benchmarks](knowledge/lender-credit-policy-benchmarks.md) for institution-level benchmarks.

## Policy Exceptions and Supervisory LTV Limits

The Interagency Guidelines for Real Estate Lending, reproduced in the OCC handbook, cap internal LTV limits at 65 percent for raw land, 75 percent for land development or improved lots, 80 percent for commercial, multifamily, and other nonresidential construction, 85 percent for one- to four-family construction, and 85 percent for improved commercial, multifamily, and other nonresidential property. Owner-occupied one- to four-family and home equity lending carries no set supervisory limit, but a loan at or above 90 percent LTV at origination requires appropriate credit enhancement in the form of mortgage insurance or readily marketable collateral. Loans at these levels are not automatically sound. Nonconforming loans belong in the aggregate exception basket. Approval of any exception requires written justification setting out the relevant credit factors, retained in the permanent loan file, with significant exceptions reported to the board. An exception that is named in the memo but not justified, quantified, and reported is an exception the file cannot defend.

## Appraisal or Evaluation: Which Applies

| Situation | Requirement |
|---|---|
| Loan amount $500,000 or less | Evaluation permitted in place of an appraisal |
| Business loan of $1 million or less where income from sale or rental of real estate is not the primary repayment source | Evaluation permitted |
| Existing extension of credit, with no new money beyond reasonable closing costs, or with new money and no obvious and material change in market conditions or the physical property | Evaluation permitted |
| Transaction of $1 million or more, or a nonresidential transaction of $500,000 or more including one- to four-family construction | State certified appraiser required |

An evaluation must identify the location, describe the property and its current and projected use, give market value in actual physical condition, use, and zoning as of its effective date with limiting conditions noted, describe how physical condition was confirmed and the extent of inspection, describe the analysis and supporting information, describe supplemental information used with any analytical method or technological tool, list all data sources, and be identified and signed by the preparer.

## Appraisal Review Standards

Independence is structural, not a signature. The valuation function must be isolated from loan production and collection influence with independent reporting lines; where a small institution cannot fully separate it, it must show prudent safeguards.

| Rule | What it means in practice |
|---|---|
| Communication with the appraiser | Prohibited: a predetermined, expected, or qualifying value, loan amount, or target LTV; a minimum value as a condition of ordering; compensation tied to loan consummation; implying future work depends on the value reached. Permitted: asking the appraiser to consider additional information, support a basis, or correct factual errors |
| Appraisals the lender did not order | A borrower-ordered or borrower-provided appraisal violates the appraisal regulations. One transferred from another institution is usable only if that institution engaged the appraiser, the appraiser had no interest in the property or parties, and the lender determines it is valid, conforming, and appropriate, with a more thorough review |
| Reviewer role | Independent of the transaction, with expertise commensurate with the complexity, property type, and market; depth of review is risk-focused. A reviewer cannot change the value conclusion - a second opinion of value requires a USPAP-compliant appraisal or appraisal review by an appraiser |
| Unresolved deficiencies | If deficiencies cannot be resolved with the appraiser, obtain a compliant appraisal or evaluation before the credit decision |

Minimum appraisal standards under 12 CFR 34.44: conform to USPAP; be written with sufficient information and analysis to support the decision; be subject to appropriate review for USPAP compliance; analyze and report appropriate deductions and discounts for proposed construction or renovation, partially leased buildings, non-market lease terms, and tract developments with unsold units; use the regulatory market value definition; and be performed by a state licensed or certified appraiser. USPAP Standards 3 and 4 govern developing and reporting an appraisal review; the Appraisal Institute's Standards of Valuation Practice mirror this with Standard B for review development and Standard C for reporting.

## Value Premise and Assumption Testing

| Premise | Definition | Where the LTV test uses it |
|---|---|---|
| As-is market value | Actual physical condition, use, and zoning as of the effective date | Raw land or a stabilized property |
| Prospective value as complete | Value at the time development is expected to be completed | Owner-occupied building, or a property to be built that is preleased |
| Prospective value as stabilized | Value at the date stabilized occupancy is projected to be achieved | Existing property not yet stabilized, or a property to be built that is not preleased to stabilized levels |

Prospective values must rest on current and reasonably expected market conditions, with the time frame and conditions stated. Reasonableness questions drawn from the OCC's published CRE appraisal review worksheet:

- Are the units of comparison consistent with the comparables cited, are the adjustments discussed and supported, and is the capitalization or discount rate supportable and reasonable for the class, property type, and market?
- Are current and projected vacancy and absorption rates stated, does the feasibility study or market analysis support the absorption period, and are effective rents net of concessions?
- Are tenant strength, rollover risk, renewal probability, lease terms, and releasing costs analyzed, and were significant departures from past lease rates, expenses, and absorption explained?
- Were omitted valuation approaches explained and the reconciliation reasoned? For troubled properties a discounted cash flow is typically more informative than direct capitalization, and the cost approach is usually inappropriate outside special purpose facilities.
- Were deductions and discounts reported where required, was going concern value allocated among real property, personal property, and intangibles, and does cost exceed or closely approximate value? If the project is not economically feasible, ask why either party is doing the deal.

Validity of an existing appraisal is a facts test, not a calendar test: passage of time, market volatility, financing terms and availability, natural disasters, over- or under-supply of competing space, improvements or deferred maintenance, changes in assumptions such as capitalization rates and lease terms, zoning or technology changes, and environmental contamination. An arbitrary period such as 12 months should not be the decisive criterion. In workouts, a new or updated valuation is warranted on material deterioration in project performance, changes in the market or property type, or variances between actual conditions and the original appraisal assumptions.

## Red Flags

- The memo sizes the loan on stabilized NOI while in-place coverage is never shown or fails.
- Loan production ordered the appraisal, discussed a needed value, or the borrower supplied it. Or the review checks USPAP boxes but never tests the cap rate, absorption, or comparable adjustments against the credit memo's own assumptions.
- A reviewer or credit officer overrides the appraiser's value conclusion in a memo instead of ordering a compliant new valuation.
- An as-stabilized value drives the LTV on an asset with no funded leasing capital, no signed leases, and no stabilization plan, or the value premise was chosen to fit the desired LTV rather than the strategy.
- Policy exceptions are listed without written justification, quantification, or upward reporting.
- Reporting covenants exist but the file has no recent operating statements, rent roll, or guarantor financials, and nobody analyzed what did arrive; or the loan documents do not match the approved structure, missing a covenant, reserve, curtailment, or cash management condition promised in the memo.
- An appraisal is treated as valid because it is under 12 months old, with no assessment of market change; an evaluation is used above the threshold; or a transferred appraisal is accepted without confirming engagement, independence, validity, and conformity.
