# Construction Lending Criteria

Last updated: 2026-09-01

Scope: U.S. ground-up construction, land development, and substantial rehabilitation debt for income-producing commercial and multifamily real estate. This is an educational underwriting reference, not legal, tax, investment, accounting, or financing advice. Regulatory thresholds here apply to U.S. banks and savings institutions; non-bank lenders are not bound by them. Confirm live appetite, proceeds, covenants, and legal terms with active lenders and counsel, and validate current market conditions where rates, spreads, and leasing assumptions are time-sensitive.

---

## Current Context

- FDIC Quarterly Banking Profile for Q2 2026 reported bank construction and development loan balances of $453.5 billion, down 3.4% from a year earlier, with a C&D noncurrent rate of 0.99% and 30-89 day past due of 0.35%. Bank construction books are shrinking even as credit performance holds.
- The Federal Reserve's July 2026 Senior Loan Officer Opinion Survey reported construction and land development standards "basically unchanged on net" over Q2 2026, a moderate net share of banks reporting weaker CLD demand, and a significant net share reporting standards at the tighter end of their historical range.
- CBRE reported on 2026-08-03 that its Lending Momentum Index was 1.0 in Q2 2026, down from a five-year high of 1.5 in Q1 but above 1.3 a year earlier, with average LTV of 59.6% on commercial and 63.3% on multifamily and alternative lenders at 38% of non-agency closings versus banks at 30%; MBA reported on 2026-08-06 that Q2 2026 originations rose 16% year over year, with depository volume up 61%.
- FDIC FIL-64-2023 (2023-12-18) directs banks to manage C&D and CRE portfolios closely, maintain current financial and collateral information, and strengthen workout infrastructure. Expect more reporting and covenant discipline than a stabilized loan carries.

Underwriting implication: construction proceeds are set by the tightest of several tests plus the lender's own balance-sheet position, not by a target LTC.

## Sizing Tests That Control Proceeds

Run all of them. The lowest result is the loan.

| Test | What it measures | Basis and notes |
|---|---|---|
| Loan to cost (LTC) | Loan divided by total project cost | Lender-specific. The OCC Comptroller's Handbook directs banks to set LTC limits in policy alongside LTV so the borrower contributes real equity; it prescribes no level. Treat the cap as quote-driven. |
| LTV as-complete | Loan divided by as-completed appraised value | For banks, the Interagency Guidelines for Real Estate Lending Policies set supervisory limits of 80% for commercial, multifamily, and other nonresidential construction, 85% for 1-4 family residential construction, 75% for land development, and 65% for raw land. |
| LTV as-stabilized | Fully funded loan divided by as-stabilized value | Governs the refinance exit. Stress the exit capitalization rate rather than assuming compression. |
| DSCR at stabilization | Stabilized NOI divided by permanent-loan debt service | Lender-specific for conventional debt. HUD Section 221(d)(4) publishes program minimums (below). |
| Debt yield at stabilization | Stabilized NOI divided by fully funded loan | Cap-rate-independent test that often binds when exit values are uncertain. Threshold is lender-specific. |
| Contributed capital / HVCRE | Borrower capital as a share of as-completed value | 15% minimum for the bank capital exclusion (below). This is a capital-treatment test, not a proceeds test, but it constrains what a bank will offer. |
| Lender concentration | The lender's C&D and CRE exposure to its own capital | OCC Bulletin 2006-46 screens at C&D of 100% or more of total risk-based capital and total CRE of 300% or more with 50% growth over 36 months. Screens, not caps, but they explain declines that have nothing to do with the project. |

Loan sizing formula: loan equals the minimum of the LTC, as-complete LTV, as-stabilized LTV, stabilized DSCR, and stabilized debt yield results.

FHA Section 221(d)(4) new construction and substantial rehabilitation, per HUD Mortgagee Letter 2025-03 (2025-01-08): market rate at 87% LTV/LTC and 1.15 DSCR with a 7% vacancy factor; LIHTC affordable with rent advantage at 90% and 1.11 with 5% vacancy; properties with 90% or greater rental assistance at 90% and 1.11 with 3% vacancy. Loan is the lesser of the requested amount, statutory limits, the DSCR-supportable amount, and the ratio-supportable amount.

## Equity, Contributed Capital, and HVCRE

| Item | Standard | Source basis |
|---|---|---|
| Equity timing and form | Contributed before construction loan disbursements begin, with deferred equity raising completion risk and requiring proof it remains available; examples include cash, marketable securities, land purchased with cash, and initial soft costs paid up front such as architect, engineering, and permit fees | OCC Comptroller's Handbook, CRE Lending |
| What does not count | Deferred developer profit, unearned developer fees, incurred overhead, and interest or holding fees paid or accrued on contributed land; a guarantor's unpledged assets are not a substitute for project equity | OCC Comptroller's Handbook |
| HVCRE risk weight | 150% under the standardized approach for an ADC exposure that does not fit an exclusion | HVCRE final rule, effective 2020-04-01 |
| HVCRE 15% test | Borrower contributes capital of at least 15% of the property's appraised "as completed" value, before the bank advances funds other than nominal lien-securing sums, contractually required to stay in until reclassification, with LTV at or below the applicable supervisory limit | HVCRE final rule |
| Forms of contributed capital | Cash, unencumbered readily marketable assets, development expenses paid out of pocket before first advance, and contributed real property or improvements at appraised value net of liens, provided the property is directly related to the project; borrowed funds count only if not derived from, related to, or encumbering the project or contributed collateral | HVCRE final rule |
| Phased projects | Each financed phase generally needs its own as-completed appraisal or evaluation to be a separate project for the 15% test | HVCRE final rule |
| Reclassification and legacy loans | Reclassification requires substantial completion plus cash flow sufficient to support debt service and expenses under the bank's permanent-financing criteria; ADC loans originated before 2015-01-01 are not HVCRE exposures | HVCRE final rule |

Read the HVCRE 15% test and cash equity at risk separately. Land contributed at appreciated value can satisfy the test while representing little new cash in the deal.

## Interest Reserve and Carry

- Size the interest reserve to fund interest through anticipated completion and lease-up, sale, or occupancy, using stressed assumptions for rate changes, draw timing, and absorption duration. The OCC handbook is explicit that "the presence of an interest reserve may not accurately reflect a borrower's ability to pay." A funded reserve is not coverage.
- If the borrower intends to pay interest from its own funds, confirm the lender could still fund it within its LTC and LTV limits if the borrower stops. During lease-up, apply project cash flow to interest first; once cash flow covers interest, reserve draws should stop. Interest reserves are generally inappropriate for stabilized properties or speculative raw land.
- Refunding or repacking a depleted reserve with new loan proceeds is a credit-deterioration signal, not a routine budget amendment; supporting it appropriately calls for a new appraisal or evaluation and a fresh feasibility review, and FDIC examiners also test guarantor capacity at that point.
- A carry reserve or carry guaranty for operating expenses, taxes, and insurance during lease-up is separate from the interest reserve. Confirm which the term sheet actually provides.

## Guaranties and Recourse Posture

| Guaranty | Covers | Typical release or burn-off |
|---|---|---|
| Completion | Lien-free completion by an outside date substantially per lender-approved plans, capped at the amount needed to complete less undisbursed loan proceeds and balancing reserves | Project complete and the statutory mechanics lien filing period expired; also on repayment in full |
| Carry | Operating and maintenance costs, taxes, insurance, and utilities while cash flow is insufficient | Repayment in full, or the property reaching a stipulated DSCR or debt yield test; often a post-foreclosure tail |
| Repayment | All or a stated portion of principal | Repayment, or reduction and burn-off as the property hits agreed thresholds such as a sustained DSCR with no default |
| Non-recourse carve-out | Fraud, misrepresentation, voluntary or collusive bankruptcy, unapproved liens, waste, prohibited transfers, diversion of funds; some carve-outs trigger full recourse | Effectively performed on repayment; certain acts survive |
| Environmental indemnity | Environmental conditions and claims | Often a sunset one to three years after repayment or transfer, subject to a clean report and no outstanding claim |

Recourse posture is negotiated, not fixed by rule, but the pattern is consistent: banks generally require sponsor guaranties on construction debt, and non-recourse execution is typically available only at lower leverage and higher pricing. Burn-off levels and percentages are deal-specific; the structures and triggers above are supportable, the numbers are not. The OCC handbook expects the bank to monitor and assess burn-off conditions before releasing a guarantor and to verify guarantor liquidity. Track the tests; an unexercised burn-off leaves the guaranty in place.

## In-Balance, Draw Controls, and Retainage

In-balance test, run at every draw: remaining loan commitment plus unfunded borrower equity plus remaining contingency must be at least cost to complete plus remaining interest and carry. If it fails, the loan is out of balance and the borrower funds the gap.

| Control | Standard |
|---|---|
| Draw support | Architect or engineer inspection report with each draw; percentage-complete verification independent of the lending function; disbursements advanced on a prearranged plan and checked against original estimates and prior draws |
| Lien protection | Lien waivers or releases from subcontractors and suppliers before disbursement; title policy updated with each draw, which matters most where mechanics liens can prime the mortgage |
| Retainage | Commercial progress payment plans typically hold back 10% to 20% of each payment |
| Final draw | Holdback released only after all lien waivers, a final inspection confirming completion to specification, and a certificate of occupancy |
| Contingency and fees | Contingency typically 5% to 10% of the overall budget, with overruns from poor projections or management ordinarily borrower-funded; developer fee, distinct from developer profit, typically does not exceed 4% of project cost and is often deferred or paid on percentage of completion |
| Change orders and reporting | Change orders reviewed by qualified staff or a consultant, approved and documented by the lender and any take-out lender, and reflected in the budget; monthly reporting of work completed, costs to date, costs to complete, deadlines, remaining funds, and lease-up rent rolls |
| Contract form | A fixed-price contract mitigates overrun risk; where borrower and contractor are related, the OCC handbook says the contract should be cost plus a fee with a guaranteed maximum price. Payment and performance bonds and title insurance are named mitigants |

Front loading, where the builder overstates the cost of early-stage work, is the failure mode these controls exist to catch; undetected, it leaves insufficient funds to complete on default.

## Extension, Mini-Perm, and Takeout

- Tenor should cover construction plus stabilization or sale, never less than the time required for completion, and extension option length should match expected construction time plus projected absorption. A common structure converts construction financing into a bridge or mini-perm facility for stabilization; the OCC handbook notes bridge loans are usually written for up to three years.
- Extension tests are lender-specific and normally combine completion, a minimum DSCR or debt yield, an occupancy or leasing threshold, no default, and a fee. Confirm each test is measurable and dated, and model the case where one fails.
- Take-out commitments come as standby (backup, priced to discourage use) or forward (rate or spread locked ahead of funding). The OCC handbook cautions such commitments "may mitigate little of the risk" the construction lender assumes, because they require completion and are usually conditioned on lease-up to break even or better at minimum rents. Underwrite the case where the take-out does not fund. FDIC examiners test take-out validity through counsel review, the permanent lender's financial capacity, a tri-party buy and sell agreement executed before the construction loan closes, and an automatic completion-date extension for delays beyond the builder's control.

## Questions to Ask Before Sizing

- Which test binds: LTC, as-complete LTV, as-stabilized LTV, stabilized DSCR, or stabilized debt yield?
- Is the lender a bank subject to supervisory LTV limits and HVCRE treatment, or a non-bank bound by neither, and where does it sit against the 100% construction and 300% total CRE screens?
- What counts as contributed capital here, how much is new cash rather than appreciated land or accrued fees, and is all of it funded before the first advance?
- Does the interest reserve carry the project through realistic absorption, and is there a separate carry reserve or carry guaranty for operating expenses, taxes, and insurance?
- What are the guaranty burn-off tests, who measures them, and on what schedule?
- What is the retainage percentage and what releases it, what contingency remains against cost to complete, and who funds an out-of-balance condition?
- What are the extension conditions and fees, what happens if lease-up misses them, and is the take-out a firm forward, a standby, or an assumption?

## Red Flags

- Loan sized to stabilized value or stabilized NOI while the in-balance test already fails at current cost to complete, or an interest reserve replenished with new loan proceeds rather than borrower or guarantor cash.
- Reserve draws continuing after project cash flow is already sufficient to cover interest.
- Deferred developer profit, unearned fees, or accrued land carry presented as equity, or land contributed at appreciated value satisfying the 15% HVCRE test with little cash at risk.
- A phase loan treated as conforming without a phase-level as-completed appraisal or evaluation, or a loan modification that increases the amount, changes project scope, or releases part of the 15% capital without recognizing it can re-trigger HVCRE treatment.
- Draw requests running ahead of verified percentage complete, a rising change-order count not reflected in the budget, or lien waivers, title updates, and inspection reports missing from the draw package.
- Contingency consumed early while the schedule has slipped, or guaranty burn-off assumed without the lender having tested and documented the conditions.
- Extension test that depends on a leasing pace the market is not delivering, with no fallback capital, or a take-out treated as certain when it is a standby or conditioned on rents the project has not reached.
