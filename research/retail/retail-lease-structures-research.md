# Retail Lease Structures Research

## Purpose

- Supports `knowledge/retail-lease-structures.md`
- Also supports the Retail v1 skills that read leases: retail lease abstraction, co-tenancy and anchor risk, CAM reconciliation and recovery, rent roll and tenant mix, and IC memo work
- Intended users: acquisition, asset management, leasing, and lender diligence teams reading U.S. retail leases, REAs, and recovery provisions

## U.S.-Only Assumptions

- Geography: United States
- Asset types: grocery-anchored, power, strip / unanchored, lifestyle, mall, mixed-use retail, pad, and single-tenant net lease (STNL)
- Deal type: acquisition diligence, refinancing, and asset review; not development entitlement work
- Legal assumptions: lease and REA interpretation, enforceability of co-tenancy, radius, continuous-operation, and liquidated-damages provisions are state-law specific and require counsel. Nothing here is legal, tax, accounting, or financing advice.
- Accounting assumptions: U.S. GAAP under ASC 842, where percentage rent and variable recoveries are variable lease consideration recognized when the underlying threshold or cost is incurred

## Key Findings

### Lease form and recovery structure

- Retail is predominantly net. Holland & Knight (2026-03) frames triple net as the landlord-favorable structure common in retail, with the tenant paying a proportionate share of operating expenses, taxes, and insurance; gross and modified gross exist but usually carry carve-outs (utilities, merchant association dues), and the actual lease language, not the label, controls.
- Kimco's FY2025 10-K states that most leases provide for fixed base rent monthly in advance plus a tenant's allocable share of real estate taxes, insurance, utilities, and common area maintenance, and that "certain of the leases provide for the payment of a fixed-rate reimbursement of these such expenses." That is the fixed-CAM structure appearing inside an otherwise net portfolio.
- Simon's FY2025 10-K is more categorical for malls: "For substantially all of our leases in the U.S. mall portfolio, we receive a fixed payment from the tenant for the CAM component," recognized straight-line under ASC 842; where fixed CAM does not apply, reimbursement reverts to the tenant's proportionate share of allocable operating expenses and CAM capital expenditures.
- Simon also states that a substantial portion of its leases "other than those for anchor stores" require reimbursement of CAM, taxes, and insurance. Anchors are commonly outside the standard recovery pool.
- Kimco notes that while many leases require the landlord to make roof and structural repairs, a number of leases place that responsibility on the tenant, and many leases provide for reimbursement of capital expenditures. Roof/structure allocation is deal-specific and must be read, not assumed.
- Kimco reports that minimum base rental revenues, operating expense reimbursements, and percentage rents together accounted for 98% of total revenues from rental properties for FY2025.

### Lease term conventions

- Brixmor FY2025 10-K: anchor tenants generally have original terms of 10 to 20 years; smaller tenants typically 5 to 10 years; both may or may not have renewal options; leases generally provide for contractual base rent increases over the original and renewal terms.
- Brixmor FY2025 10-K: leases "generally provide for the payment of fixed monthly base rent," with certain leases adding percentage rent above a predetermined gross sales threshold, and reimbursement of common area expenses, utilities, insurance, real estate taxes, and certain maintenance capital.

### Percentage rent mechanics

- Natural breakpoint = minimum rent divided by the percentage rate. Holland & Knight's worked example: $140,000 annual rent at 7% yields a $2,000,000 natural breakpoint. An artificial breakpoint is any negotiated figure set independently of that math, used when the parties want to trade minimum rent against percentage rent.
- Gross sales definitions commonly exclude customer refunds, employee discounts, accommodation sales (stamps, money orders), coin-operated devices, third-party credit card commissions, inter-store merchandise transfers, returns to manufacturers, and sales taxes collected (Holland & Knight).
- Leases typically require contemporaneous sales records, periodic reports, and landlord audit rights (Holland & Knight).
- Definition risk is now a disclosed business risk, not a theoretical one. Simon's FY2025 10-K warns that growth in online shopping "may result in certain tenants underreporting sales," giving examples of curbside pickup and store-fulfilled online orders not being reported and online returns processed in store being netted against reported sales.
- Magnitude check for open-air centers: Brixmor recognized $9.6 million, $9.7 million, and $9.3 million of percentage rent for FY2025, FY2024, and FY2023 against FY2025 rental income of $1,369.5 million - roughly 0.7% of rental income in FY2025. Percentage rent is a material lease-negotiation issue but is not a material income line in a typical grocery-anchored portfolio.
- Simon, by contrast, describes overage rent as a portion of lease income that "directly depend[s] on the reported sales volume" of retail tenants, and flags declines in tenant sales as a direct income risk. Mall and outlet formats carry more percentage-rent sensitivity than open-air.

### CAM, tax, and insurance recovery structure

- Caps are usually negotiated on controllable expenses only, with taxes, insurance, security, and utilities carved out (Cox Castle common-area piece). Landlords resist caps as inconsistent with net-lease economics.
- Cumulative vs non-cumulative matters. Lowndes' worked example at a 5% annual cap: if year 2 expenses rise 3%, a cumulative cap lets the landlord bill up to 7% in year 3 (5% plus the unused 2%); a non-cumulative cap resets and holds year 3 to 5%.
- Administrative and management fees: Cox Castle states landlords typically include third-party management fees plus an administrative fee "often 10-15% of other CAM expenses," and that major tenants may negotiate to an administrative fee alone. Practitioner guidance is that admin fees should apply to controllable costs rather than to taxes, insurance, and utilities passed through at cost.
- Denominator convention varies by format (Cox Castle): regional malls commonly exclude anchor tenants from the denominator; power centers commonly use strict square-footage ratios; ground-leased parcels are often allocated by land area. Cost pooling - allocating an expense only among the tenants it benefits - is a landlord right to look for.
- Capital expenditures are a negotiated middle ground: excluded during early lease years for some items, amortized over useful life for others (Cox Castle).
- Common tenant exclusion asks: ground rent, debt service, casualty repairs, tenant improvements, leasing commissions, and above-market costs (Cox Castle).
- Audit rights: typically once per year, on notice, at the landlord's office, by an independent CPA firm; landlord reimburses the audit cost when an overstatement exceeds a 3% to 5% threshold (Cox Castle). The ICSC REA materials describe the same 3% or 5% variance convention for operator audits at the REA level.
- Occupancy-based distortion is real in the data, not just the lease. Brixmor reported FY2025 total leased occupancy of 95.1% against a 350 basis point spread to billed occupancy, and leased occupancy of 92.2% for spaces under 10,000 square feet. Leased and billed are different denominators and different revenue.

### Co-tenancy

- Two families: opening co-tenancy (tenant need not open, or need not pay full rent, until named anchors or an occupancy threshold are satisfied) and operating/ongoing co-tenancy (already-open tenant gets relief when anchors go dark or occupancy falls). Cox Castle and the California Lawyers Association piece agree on this split.
- Key-tenant definitions typically hinge on named anchors or mini-majors, described by Cox Castle as roughly 15,000 to 30,000 square feet.
- Landlord protections that show up repeatedly: replacement-tenant satisfaction, tenant not in default, proof of a sales decline, cure periods before remedies trigger, remedies as sole and exclusive relief, and a forced election between reduced rent and termination ("fish or cut bait") (Cox Castle; ICSC 2025 Workshop 11).
- Remedy shapes: rent abatement (Cox Castle cites roughly 50% of fixed rent as a common level), alternate/substitute rent, delayed opening, and termination after a period of continued violation, commonly 6 to 12 months or more, with a snap-back to full rent or landlord recapture if the violation runs past about a year.
- Terminating tenants often seek reimbursement of unamortized leasehold improvements, typically limited to the initial term (Cox Castle).
- Enforceability turned in the landlord's favor in California. In JJD-HOV Elk Grove, LLC v. Jo-Ann Stores, LLC (S275843), the Supreme Court of California affirmed the judgment in full on 2024-12-19. The California Lawyers Association analysis describes the lease as pairing fixed minimum rent with substitute rent of the greater of $12,000 or 3.5% of sales when co-tenancy failed, and the court treating that as valid alternative performance by sophisticated commercial parties rather than a penalty under Civil Code 1671 or a forfeiture under 3275. The earlier Grand Prospect Partners v. Ross Dress For Less analysis remains the caution: an abatement bearing no reasonable relationship to anticipated damages can still be attacked, while a negotiated termination right is generally not a forfeiture.
- Drafting takeaway from the same source: structure relief as a second, condition-triggered rent level rather than as a penalty-flavored abatement.

### Go-dark, continuous operation, exclusives, radius

- ICSC 2025 Workshop 11 frames the mechanism directly: a tenant going dark can trigger co-tenancy defaults in other leases, cut traffic, and impair the landlord's ability to lease or finance the property. Before granting any go-dark right, the landlord must test it against every other co-tenancy clause at the center.
- Continuous-operation clauses are enforceable in many jurisdictions when clearly drafted and tied to legitimate economic interests. Courts disfavor specific performance because of supervision burden, and prefer damages, termination, or other default remedies. Where percentage rent is the sole or primary rent, some courts imply a duty to operate.
- Liquidated damages are generally enforced when actual damages are difficult to calculate and the formula is a reasonable estimate. El Centro Mall, LLC v. Payless ShoeSource, Inc., 174 Cal. App. 4th 58 (2009), as described in the ICSC materials, upheld a per-day charge of ten cents per square foot of leased space or $100, whichever is greater.
- Landlord toolkit for dark space: recapture rights after a defined closure period, rent increase or acceleration limited to the closure period, termination after notice and cure, and termination of the dark tenant's exclusive use protection.
- Radius restrictions are typically paired with percentage rent or with a sales-based kick-out right, and exist to stop the tenant from diluting the sales base the landlord bargained for (ICSC 2022 Workshop 8).
- Enforceability test from the same materials: the restriction must be necessary to protect a legitimate business interest and must not be overly broad in location, duration, or scope; a radius "that covers more than a few miles is very likely to be unenforceable." Suburban drafting commonly measures X miles from the shopping center boundary or from the lease lines of the premises, and the measuring point must be stated.
- Radius scope should reach the tenant entity plus affiliates and owners (single-purpose entities can otherwise be circumvented) and should describe the type of business rather than only the trade name; tenants carve out existing locations, non-retail uses, and franchisee/licensee locations they do not control (ICSC 2022; Hollander 2024-08-02).
- Radius remedies: injunction, a fixed increase in minimum rent, counting the violating store's gross receipts toward premises gross sales for percentage rent, negating the kick-out or go-dark right, or default and termination (ICSC 2022; Holland & Knight).
- Exclusive use protections are frequently imposed at the REA level as well as in leases, especially by grocers, home improvement, club, and themed restaurant users, and historic enclosed-mall REAs are described as far more restrictive and poorly adapted to current retail uses (ICSC 2018).

### Kick-outs and early termination

- Kick-outs are typically tenant-side, one-time, exercisable in a short window after a defined number of lease years, conditioned on failure to hit a stated sales threshold; landlords may negotiate a mirror right to recapture underperforming space (Siegfried Rivera).
- Where the landlord funded TI or paid commissions, the exercise price commonly includes the unamortized portion of both (Siegfried Rivera).

### REA / OEA, pads, and ground leases

- An REA (also OEA, ECR, COREA) governs a multi-parcel unified development: easements for access, parking, utilities, and signage plus a governing regime for construction, maintenance, architectural theme, prohibited/restricted/exclusive uses, site-plan control, and signage rights (ICSC 2018).
- Approving-party rights are usually concentrated in the master developer plus the top few anchor parcel owners; outparcel REAs are narrower and typically leave control with the developer (ICSC 2018).
- Common-area maintenance under an REA is handled one of three ways: each owner maintains its own tract, the owners appoint an operator for the whole center, or a hybrid. Where there is an operator, expect an annual budget with owner approval rights, an annual reconciliation statement, and audit rights with a 3% or 5% variance trigger for cost shifting (ICSC 2018).
- Parking ratios are set in the REA, often with higher ratios for restaurant and theater uses and separate ratios for office or residential in mixed-use; whether a tract must be self-parked or may borrow parking is a specific negotiated point (ICSC 2018).
- Terms run long. The ICSC materials describe easements and REA terms in the 50 to 80 year range, note that early mall REAs are now approaching expiration, and observe that parties are beginning to weigh letting outdated REAs expire against extending them.
- Ground-leased assets are common at the portfolio level: Kimco's FY2025 10-K reports interests in 36 consolidated shopping center properties subject to long-term ground leases where a third party owns the land, with Kimco generally responsible for all costs of the building and improvements and reversion of land plus improvements at expiration absent extension. Simon's property schedule lists numerous centers held in "Fee and Ground Lease" with stated expiration years.

### Assignment, sublease, estoppel, SNDA

- Assignment and sublease control is part of the operating-covenant toolkit, not a separate topic: ICSC 2025 recommends retaining recapture on cessation of operations including by subtenants and assignees, restricting transfer unless the transferee meets financial and operational thresholds, and preserving operating covenants against successors and assigns.
- Estoppel certificates are point-in-time confirmations that the named tenant is the tenant, the lease is in full force and effect, no default has occurred, no advance rent has been paid, there is no offset claim, and no bankruptcy is pending; practice is to obtain them at or just before closing, generally no more than 30 days in advance (Williams Mullen).
- An SNDA is a three-party agreement among lender, landlord, and tenant covering subordination of the lease, tenant attornment to a foreclosure successor, and the lender's non-disturbance covenant, often with added lender cure rights, rent-prepayment limits, and restrictions on lease modification (Williams Mullen).

## Benchmark and Formula Decisions

Suitable as repo defaults (all traceable above):

- Natural breakpoint = annual minimum rent / percentage rate. Percentage rent due = (gross sales - breakpoint) x rate, applied against the lease's own gross sales definition and measurement period.
- Anchor original lease terms of 10-20 years and small-shop terms of 5-10 years as the open-air convention to sanity-check a rent roll against (Brixmor FY2025).
- CAM caps default to controllable expenses only; taxes, insurance, and utilities pass through uncapped unless the lease says otherwise.
- Cumulative vs non-cumulative cap treatment must be identified explicitly; the Lowndes 5% example is the reference math.
- CAM administrative fee reference range of 10-15% of other CAM expenses, as a negotiating range and not a market rule.
- Audit-cost-shifting threshold reference of 3-5% overstatement, at both lease and REA level.
- Denominator convention by format: malls commonly exclude anchors, power centers commonly use gross square footage ratios, ground-leased parcels commonly use land area.
- Leased occupancy and billed occupancy are separate figures and must be reported separately; the Brixmor FY2025 350 bp spread is the illustration.
- Radius reasonableness screen: a restriction materially beyond a few miles, or without a stated measuring point, is a flag rather than an assumption of enforceability.
- Estoppel currency: dated no more than about 30 days before closing.

Remain case-by-case only (state explicitly in the knowledge base):

- Any specific co-tenancy occupancy threshold (for example "80% occupancy" or "two of three named anchors"). These are pure negotiation outputs; the sources give no market default.
- Percentage rent rates by category. No retrieved source supports a defensible rate table by merchandise category, so rates stay a read-the-lease item.
- Occupancy cost ratio thresholds by format. Not sourced here; see the pack's tenant sales and occupancy cost knowledge base.
- Alternate-rent levels. Cox Castle's "typically 50% of fixed rent" is directional practitioner commentary, not a benchmark; the JJD-HOV lease used the greater of $12,000 or 3.5% of sales, which is a very different shape.
- Radius mileage. The ICSC sample uses five miles illustratively; enforceable distance is market and state specific.
- Roof, structure, and capital replacement allocation. Kimco's disclosure shows both directions inside one portfolio.

## Conflicting Source Resolution

- Percentage rent materiality: Simon frames overage rent as a real income sensitivity, while Brixmor's own numbers make percentage rent under 1% of rental income. Both are primary filings and both are right for their format. Resolution: treat percentage rent as format-dependent - meaningful in mall, outlet, and some lifestyle and restaurant/pad deals, immaterial to income but still important as a sales-reporting and audit hook in open-air grocery-anchored deals.
- Co-tenancy enforceability: practitioner commentary written before December 2024 leans on Grand Prospect Partners v. Ross Dress For Less and treats abatement-style co-tenancy relief as penalty-exposed. The Supreme Court of California's affirmance in JJD-HOV (2024-12-19, confirmed in the court's own minutes) is the later and higher authority in that state. Resolution: primary court record controls for California; nothing here generalizes to other states, so the knowledge base flags this as a counsel question rather than a rule.
- Fixed CAM prevalence: Kimco describes fixed-rate reimbursement as applying to "certain" leases while Simon describes it as applying to "substantially all" U.S. mall leases. Resolution: prevalence is format-driven; do not assume either default without reading the lease.
- CAM caps as "market": tenant-side explainers describe 3-5% annual caps as common. No primary or quasi-primary source retrieved this session supports that as a market default, and Cox Castle describes caps as landlord-resisted and occasionally negotiated. Resolution: the knowledge base states cap structure as a required read, gives the cumulative/non-cumulative math, and does not publish a default cap percentage.

## Edge Cases and Red Flags

- Anchors outside the recovery pool. If anchors do not pay standard CAM (Simon) or pay a separately negotiated contribution, the small-shop denominator absorbs a disproportionate share and reconciliation math from a summary rent roll will be wrong.
- Fixed CAM plus inflation. Fixed CAM converts a recovery into a straight-line revenue stream; the landlord keeps expense risk. In a rising insurance or tax environment this looks like clean revenue and behaves like a short position.
- Cumulative caps quietly restoring recovery. A cumulative cap can bill above the stated percentage in a catch-up year. Modeling it as a hard annual ceiling understates recovery; modeling an uncapped pass-through overstates it.
- Sales underreporting in an omnichannel format. Curbside, ship-from-store, and in-store returns of online orders can suppress reported sales, which simultaneously depresses percentage rent, understates occupancy cost, and can hand a tenant a kick-out it should not have (Simon FY2025).
- Go-dark chain reaction. One dark anchor can trip opening and ongoing co-tenancy in multiple leases at once. Never price a go-dark consent without reading every other co-tenancy clause at the center (ICSC 2025).
- Co-tenancy remedies with no cure period or no sunset. Without a landlord cure window and without a snap-back or recapture at roughly a year, reduced rent can run indefinitely (Cox Castle).
- REA terms nearing expiration. Older enclosed-mall REAs written for a 50-80 year horizon are now approaching term end; expiration can strip site-plan, use, and maintenance governance and is a diligence item, not a footnote (ICSC 2018).
- Approving-party consents. Redevelopment, pad splits, parking reconfiguration, and use changes may need anchor or parcel-owner consent under the REA even where the lease is silent (ICSC 2018).
- Exclusive use conflicts across documents. Exclusives can live in leases and in the REA; a proposed tenant can clear the rent roll and still violate a recorded REA exclusive.
- Ground-leased pads and anchors. Reversion at expiration, leasehold financeability, lender protections, and rent resets sit outside the operating lease and change residual value (Kimco FY2025; Simon FY2025).
- Missing estoppels and SNDAs. A stale estoppel (older than roughly 30 days) or a refused SNDA is a financing and closing issue, not a cleanup item (Williams Mullen).
- Radius clauses drafted only against the trade name or only against the tenant entity are routinely circumvented by affiliates and new single-purpose entities (ICSC 2022).
- Kick-out exercise math. If the exercise price does not recapture unamortized TI and commissions, the landlord funds a departure it already paid for (Siegfried Rivera).
