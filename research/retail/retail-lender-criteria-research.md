# Retail Lender Criteria Research

## Purpose

- Supports `knowledge/retail-lender-criteria.md`
- Intended users: sponsors, brokers, credit analysts, and lenders testing whether a U.S. retail asset is financeable, and by which lender lane
- Scope is educational decision support for retail acquisitions, refinancings, and asset reviews. It is not legal, tax, investment, accounting, or financing advice.

## U.S.-Only Assumptions

- Geography: United States. Bank supervisory criteria, SBA program rules, CMBS servicing standards, and rating agency criteria cited here are U.S. constructs and do not transfer to other jurisdictions.
- Deal type: grocery-anchored, power, strip / unanchored, lifestyle, mall, mixed-use retail, pad, and single-tenant net lease (STNL) acquisitions, refinancings, and maturity reviews.
- Legal and market assumptions: retail credit is lease-driven and sales-driven. Anchor status, co-tenancy, exclusives, go-dark rights, recovery structure, and tenant sales govern proceeds more than headline leverage does.
- Actual lender quotes, term sheets, and loan documents always control over any directional range in this note.

## Key Findings

### Retail credit is currently the healthier side of the CRE ledger, but it is not uniform

- The OCC's Spring 2026 Semiannual Risk Perspective (2026-05-07) states plainly that "Retail remains a bright spot. The vacancy rate in retail properties remains low, with little space available for lease in most markets."
- CBRE reported (2026-07-29) that U.S. retail availability was unchanged at 4.9% in Q2 2026 with average asking rent of $24.79 PSF, up 2.4% year over year, on four consecutive quarters of positive net absorption.
- Yet Trepp data reported 2026-08-11 shows retail CMBS delinquency at 6.96% in July 2026 - well below office at 11.91% but far above industrial at 1.13%, and close to the 7.86% all-property rate. Legacy mall and outlet exposure, not open-air necessity retail, drives most of that number.
- Implication: sector-level "retail is fine" statements are not underwriting. Format, anchor, and trade area determine credit.

### Capital is flowing back to retail, unevenly by lender lane

- MBA reported (2026-08-06) that Q2 2026 retail originations were up 61% year over year, the largest property-type gain, while CMBS volume was up 68% and depositories up 61%, and life company volume was down 27%.
- The Fed's July 2026 SLOOS (released 2026-08-03) found moderate net shares of banks eased standards for nonfarm nonresidential loans, while also reporting standards remained at relatively tight levels; demand was basically unchanged, stronger at larger banks and weaker at smaller ones.
- FDIC's Q2 2026 Quarterly Banking Profile shows the largest quarterly past-due-and-nonaccrual decline occurred in nonfarm nonresidential CRE, down $2.4 billion or 14 basis points to 1.52%, with a 1.23% noncurrent rate.
- Implication: a retail borrower in 2026 has more executions available than in 2023-2024, but the mix has shifted toward CMBS and banks rather than life companies.

### Bank appetite is shaped by concentration supervision, not just the asset

- The 2006 interagency guidance (OCC Bulletin 2006-46) sets supervisory screening criteria at construction/land/development loans of 100% or more of total risk-based capital, or total CRE loans of 300% or more of total risk-based capital where the CRE book grew 50% or more over the prior 36 months. Exceeding those criteria does not cap lending; it triggers additional supervisory analysis.
- Practical effect on retail deals: a bank's answer can be driven by where it sits against those ratios and by relationship deposits, independent of the center's quality.
- FIL-23-2023 (2023-06-29) tells examiners that prudent modifications for borrowers with demonstrated repayment capacity are not criticized, and that a modified loan is not adversely classified solely because collateral value fell below the balance. That is the regulatory basis for a bank granting a retail extension rather than forcing a maturity default.

### CMBS surveillance thresholds are the most concrete, citable retail credit triggers available

CREFC IRP v8.4 (published 2024-03-13) defines the Servicer Watchlist triggers that CMBS master servicers apply. These are surveillance thresholds rather than origination limits, but they are the clearest published statement of what institutional retail lenders treat as deterioration:

- 1E Fixed rate DSCR trigger: credit item at NCF DSCR below 1.10x (1.20x for healthcare and lodging).
- 1F DSCR versus underwriting: informational item when current NCF DSCR is below 75% of the underwritten NCF DSCR and current DSCR is below 1.40x.
- 1G Floating rate DSCR trigger: credit item when DSCR is below 1.00x and below 90% of in-place NOI as of underwriting.
- 4A Occupancy decrease: below 80% of underwritten occupancy for fixed rate loans, below 90% of in-place tenants as of underwriting for floating rate loans.
- 4C Single tenant, or any tenant above 30% of NRA, expiring within 12 months for loans of $30 million or more, within 6 months for loans under $30 million, or on any notice the tenant will not renew. Fully amortizing CTL loans are excluded where the lease expires co-terminus with the loan.
- 4D Any combination of top three tenants each occupying at least 5% of NRA and cumulatively more than 30% of NRA with expirations within the next 6 months.
- 4E Bankruptcy of licensee, franchisor, or any combination of top three tenants each at least 5% of NRA and cumulatively above 30% of NRA.
- 4F Major tenant lease in default, terminated, or dark, above 30% of NRA.
- 1B/1C/1D operational triggers: non-escrowed taxes 60+ days past due; insurance more than 60 days past due or force-placed; outstanding servicing advances above $10,000 aggregate for 30+ days.
- 5A Pending maturity or ARD with a balloon balance inside 90 days.
- 6A Any other situation indicating increased default risk creating potential material losses, at the lesser of 10% of UPB or $500,000.

The 4C through 4F cluster is effectively the anchor-risk and go-dark test, and 4A is the co-tenancy proxy. A retail asset that would trip these on day one after a plausible anchor loss is not a clean permanent-loan candidate.

### Rating agency practice sets the valuation haircut expectation

- KBRA's North American CMBS Property Evaluation Methodology (2026-01-09) derives KBRA Value by dividing KNCF (sustainable net cash flow) by a long-term, asset-specific KBRA capitalization rate rather than accepting appraised value.
- In a concrete example, KBRA's ratings for BBCMS 2024-C24 (2024-02-15), a pool with 43.0% retail exposure, reported KNCF 10.4% below issuer cash flow and KBRA values 40.0% below third-party appraisals.
- Implication for sponsors: the gap between broker NOI and a rating-agency or credit-committee sustainable NOI is where retail proceeds are lost. Percentage rent, temporary/specialty income, above-market anchor rent, and unreserved capital are the usual sources of that gap.

### Percentage rent and overage rent generally do not create loan proceeds

- Practitioner commentary is blunt on the lender posture: "You can spend the percentage rent we just won't let you borrow against it" (Concordia Realty, 2019-01-26). The same source distinguishes a natural breakpoint (base rent divided by the percentage rate) from a negotiated unnatural breakpoint.
- Kimco's FY2025 10-K describes percentage rent clauses as an inflation mitigant - additional rent calculated as a percentage of tenant gross sales above pre-determined thresholds - rather than as core contractual rent.
- Implication: treat percentage rent as an equity return item and as a tenant-health signal, not as debt-service capacity, unless a specific lender confirms otherwise in writing.

### Co-tenancy is a documented, disclosed risk in the public retail REIT universe

- Kimco's FY2025 10-K risk factors state that "multiple lease terminations by tenants, or a failure by multiple tenants to occupy their premises in a shopping center could result in lease terminations or significant reductions in rent by other tenants in the same shopping centers under the terms of some leases."
- This is exactly the mechanism a lender is sizing against: the anchor loss is the first-order event, and the inline rent reductions or termination rights are the second-order event that a naive rent roll does not show.

### STNL sits in its own financing universe

- PGIM Private Capital's published CTL program offers up to 100% LTV based on the present value of future rental obligations, $25 million to $100+ million per transaction, maturities of 20+ years with customized amortization, and targets investment grade corporate, not-for-profit, and government tenants, with below-investment-grade considered case by case.
- Realty Income's FY2025 10-K shows the credit profile the STNL market runs on: 15,511 properties at 98.9% occupancy, retail at 79.1% of $5.31 billion annualized base rent, 32.2% of ABR from investment grade clients, top 20 clients at 35.8% of ABR, and weighted average remaining lease term of approximately 8.8 years.
- Implication: for STNL the credit question is tenant and lease document first, real estate second - but residual value still matters at loan maturity for anything short of a fully amortizing CTL.

### SBA is an owner-user lane only

- SBA states the 504 program provides up to $5 million (up to $5.5 million for certain projects) and explicitly excludes "speculation or investment in rental real estate."
- CDC practitioner guidance commonly cited: occupy 51% of an existing facility, or 60% immediately for new construction with a path toward 80% within 10 years. Treat those percentages as commonly cited program practice to verify against the current SOP with the CDC, not as a repo-hardened rule.
- Implication: SBA is relevant to a retail user buying its own pad or storefront, and to a pad seller's buyer pool. It is not a lane for investor-owned centers.

## Benchmark and Formula Decisions

Formulas are not restated here. Use [Underwriting Calculations](knowledge/underwriting-calc.md) for DSCR, debt yield, and LTV mechanics and [Risk Scoring](knowledge/risk-scoring.md) for scoring conventions.

Suitable as repo defaults (traceable to sources above):

- CMBS surveillance thresholds from CREFC IRP v8.4 as the default deterioration screen for retail loans: DSCR below 1.10x; DSCR below 75% of underwritten NCF DSCR while under 1.40x; occupancy below 80% of underwritten; any tenant above 30% NRA rolling within 12 months (loans >= $30 million) or 6 months (loans < $30 million); top-three-tenant combination at 5%+ each and 30%+ cumulative rolling within 6 months; major tenant dark, defaulted, or terminated above 30% NRA.
- Bank concentration screening at 100% of total risk-based capital for construction/land/development and 300% for total CRE with 50% three-year growth, as the reason a bank may decline an otherwise clean retail deal.
- SBA 504 is owner-user only and cannot finance investment or rental retail.
- Percentage rent is excluded from debt-service capacity by default and carried as upside plus a tenant-health signal.
- Sustainable NOI, not broker NOI, controls proceeds; expect a lender or rating agency haircut to issuer cash flow (KBRA reported 10.4% in one 43%-retail pool) and a larger haircut to appraised value.

Directional only, must be re-verified per deal and per lender:

- Life company retail parameters of roughly 60%-75% LTV, 1.25x-1.35x DSCR, and 8%-10% debt yield, and general retail parameters of up to 75% LTV with 1.25x-1.35x DSCR, are lender-broker published ranges as of 2026-09-01, not universal underwriting law.
- CTL terms up to 100% LTV with 20+ year maturities reflect one named program (PGIM) for investment grade tenants; other CTL providers differ and below-investment-grade tenants are case by case.
- Any current delinquency, rent, availability, or origination figure in this note is dated market data and must be refreshed before it is used in a live credit conversation.

Intentionally left case-by-case (no repo default):

- Loan-per-square-foot limits by retail format. No cited source publishes durable PSF caps for grocery-anchored, power, strip, lifestyle, mall, pad, or STNL product, and basis varies too widely by market and anchor structure to hard-code.
- Reserve sizing for TI, LC, replacement, anchor rollover, and co-tenancy cure. Lenders size these to the specific rent roll, lease documents, and PCA. State the mechanism, not a number.
- Recourse posture. Bank, debt fund, and life company recourse varies by sponsor, market, and leverage; CMBS and CTL are typically non-recourse with standard carve-outs, but the carve-out schedule is deal-specific.
- Spreads, coupons, and index levels.

## Conflicting Source Resolution

- Sector health vs CMBS distress: the OCC (2026-05-07) calls retail a bright spot with low vacancy, while Trepp (reported 2026-08-11) shows retail CMBS delinquency at 6.96%. Resolution: the OCC is describing the aggregate leasing market, which is dominated by open-air necessity retail; the Trepp figure is concentrated in legacy mall, outlet, and older commodity centers held in securitizations. The knowledge base resolves this by format, not by a single sector verdict.
- Lending standards easing vs standards being tight: the July 2026 SLOOS reports both that banks eased NFNR standards on net and that standards sit at relatively tight levels. Both are true; easing is a direction, tightness is a level. The knowledge base states the direction and the level together.
- Capital source direction: MBA shows life company originations down 27% year over year in Q2 2026 while CMBS is up 68%. This conflicts with the common assumption that life companies are the natural home for grocery-anchored retail. Resolution: life companies remain a strong fit for the best stabilized retail, but 2026 volume evidence says a sponsor should not assume that execution is available, and should run a CMBS and bank track in parallel.
- Published leverage ranges: broker-published life company and retail ranges (60%-75% LTV, 1.25x-1.35x DSCR, 8%-10% debt yield) are practitioner-tier sources with no regulator or standards-body equivalent. Primary and standards-body sources took priority for anything stated as a default; the practitioner ranges are carried explicitly as directional.

## Edge Cases and Red Flags

- Anchor rent far below market. Proceeds look strong on in-place NOI, but the renewal or replacement economics are punitive. Do not hard-code a haircut; model the replacement case.
- Anchor owns its pad or is subject to an REA/OEA the borrower does not control. The center's site plan, parking ratios, and redevelopment rights may sit outside the loan collateral. This needs counsel review, not a default assumption.
- Co-tenancy cascade. A single anchor departure can trigger rent reductions or termination rights across multiple inline leases at once, as Kimco's own risk factors describe. Screen the leases for triggers, cure periods, and remedies before sizing.
- Dark but paying anchor. Rent continues, sales collapse, inline traffic collapses, and co-tenancy clocks may be running. CREFC treats a dark major tenant above 30% NRA as a credit watchlist item; a lender will treat it as a proceeds event.
- Recovery leakage. CAM caps, base-year and gross-up structures, admin fee exclusions, capital recovery exclusions, and anchor pro-rata carve-outs create a permanent gap between billed and recoverable expense. The gap belongs in NOI, not in an expense reimbursement footnote.
- Percentage rent-dependent NOI. If a material share of NOI is overage rent, expect it to be excluded from debt-service capacity.
- Specialty, temporary, and kiosk income. Short-term by nature; expect it to be discounted or excluded.
- Loan maturity inside anchor expiration. CREFC's 4C trigger exists precisely because this is the classic retail refinance failure. Structure a reserve, a signed renewal, or a maturity that clears the expiration.
- Bank concentration decline. A clean center can still be declined because the bank is near its 300% CRE supervisory criterion. That is a lender-portfolio fact, not an asset fact.
- SBA proposed for an investor deal. SBA 504 cannot finance investment or rental real estate. If a broker proposes it for a leased center, that is a structural disqualification, not a negotiation point.
- CTL assumed for a non-investment-grade tenant. CTL proceeds depend on the tenant's rating and on a bondable or near-bondable lease. Both the credit and the lease document have to qualify.
- Maturity default risk masked by a workout. FIL-23-2023 gives banks room to modify prudently; that room does not make the underlying refinance math work.
