# Retail Tenant Sales and Occupancy Cost Research

## Purpose

- Supports `knowledge/retail-tenant-sales-and-occupancy-cost.md`
- Also supports the Retail v1 skills that consume sales-based health metrics: rent roll and tenant mix analysis, co-tenancy and anchor risk, underwriting model building, financing fit, and IC memo writing
- Intended users: acquisition, asset management, and credit teams underwriting U.S. retail centers and single-tenant net lease (STNL) assets who must convert tenant sales reports into occupancy cost ratios, credit views, and renewal probability

## U.S.-Only Assumptions

- Geography: United States, including Puerto Rico where a cited REIT portfolio includes it
- Deal type: retail acquisition, refinance, and asset review across grocery-anchored, power, strip / unanchored, lifestyle, mall, mixed-use, pad, and STNL formats
- Legal assumptions: U.S. Bankruptcy Code (Title 11) governs tenant insolvency outcomes; lease interpretation and enforceability are state-law questions requiring counsel
- Accounting assumptions: tenant-reported sales are unaudited landlord reporting, not GAAP revenue; REIT-disclosed occupancy cost is an issuer-defined, non-GAAP operating statistic
- Market assumptions: cap rates, delinquency rates, and closure counts are time-sensitive and directional only

## Key Findings

**Definition and formula.** Occupancy cost ratio (also called the health ratio) is total occupancy cost at a location divided by that location's gross sales for the same trailing period. Tanger's 10-K defines the cost side as base rent plus CAM, real estate taxes, insurance, and advertising and promotions. Adventures in CRE describes the same construction, adding percentage rent. Morningstar DBRS describes it as base or fixed rent plus percentage-of-sales amounts plus all reimbursable expenses, over sales. The three definitions agree in substance.

**Category is the controlling variable, not a single universal threshold.** Morningstar DBRS criteria set the grocery threshold at roughly 2% to 7% depending on sales volume because grocers operate on thin margins, and set the power-center anchor threshold at roughly 10%, above which the agency evaluates whether the tenant can keep paying rent and operate profitably. Chipotle reported occupancy costs of 5.2% of total revenue in fiscal 2025, up from 5.0% in 2024 - a company-level, not store-level, figure but a useful QSR anchor point. Tanger reported outlet-portfolio occupancy cost at 9.7% of tenant sales in 2025, from 9.5% in 2024, 9.3% in 2023, 8.6% in 2022, and 8.1% in 2021, and describes that level as among the lowest in the retail industry.

**Mall bands by sales tier exist but are old.** The 2003 WealthManagement.com summary of the then-current rating agency mall method gives bands of 9% to 11% for comparable mall shop sales under $250 PSF, 11% to 13% for $250 to $300, 13% to 14% for $300 to $350, and 14% to 16% above $350, computed on aggregate mall shop costs over aggregate sales for tenants under 10,000 SF in occupancy at least 12 months. The shape of that relationship, higher productivity supporting higher occupancy cost, is still the right intuition; the dollar tiers are more than two decades stale in nominal terms and should not be used as current cutoffs.

**Grocery productivity anchor.** Sprouts reported fiscal 2025 net sales of $8.806 billion across 477 stores and 12,992,097 total square feet at period end, with average store size of 27,237 SF. Dividing net sales by ending square footage gives approximately $678 PSF; using the average of beginning and ending square footage gives approximately $701 PSF. Treat roughly $650 to $700 PSF as one specialty-grocer data point, not a category norm - conventional supermarkets, discount grocers, and club formats differ widely.

**Shopping-center format sets the sales-reporting expectation.** ICSC classification gives neighborhood centers a supermarket anchor, 30,000 to 125,000 SF, 30% to 50% anchor GLA, and a roughly 3-mile trade area; community centers 125,000 to 400,000 SF with 2+ anchors; power centers 250,000 to 600,000 SF with 3+ category-dominant anchors at 70% to 90% anchor GLA and a 5 to 10 mile trade area; super-regional malls 800,000+ SF with 3+ anchors and a 5 to 25 mile trade area. Morningstar DBRS notes sales are frequently reported for anchor tenants but less often for in-line tenants outside regional malls, which is why occupancy cost coverage is thin in open-air centers.

**Credit tiering is disclosed and quantifiable in STNL.** Realty Income reported that 32.2% of total portfolio annualized base rent as of December 31, 2025 came from properties leased to investment-grade clients, their subsidiaries, or affiliates; its top 20 clients were 35.8% of ABR with 11 of the 20 investment grade; portfolio WALT was approximately 8.8 years and 97.8% of properties were single-tenant. The Boulder Group reported Q2 2026 STNL retail cap rates at 6.60% (up 5 bps) against 6.82% overall, with McDonald's and Chick-fil-A ground leases asking the lowest cap rates at 4.45%, investment-grade assets representing less than 10% of retail inventory, retail supply up 16.2% quarter over quarter concentrated in non-credit product, and buyers scrutinizing tenant financials and store-level performance.

**Distress signals are observable at the market level.** Trepp data reported by MBA NewsLink put July 2026 retail CMBS delinquency at 6.96%, up 5 bps, against 7.86% overall, 11.91% office, 7.69% multifamily, 5.35% lodging, and 1.13% industrial. Coresight's midyear 2026 outlook frames the year around roughly 7,900 U.S. store closures and 5,500 openings, with the underlying detail subscription-gated. Retail Dive's tracker lists 2026 chapter 11 filings including Saks Global (January 14, 2026, exited June 26, 2026), Pat McGrath Cosmetics (January 22, 2026), Francesca's (February 5, 2026), Eddie Bauer (February 9, 2026, roughly 220 stores at filing), QVC Group (April 16, 2026), and Sleep Number (June 12, 2026, more than 570 U.S. stores), following 2025 filings including Joann (790 stores, liquidated), Forever 21, Hudson's Bay, Rite Aid, At Home, and Claire's.

**Bankruptcy mechanics set the recovery floor.** Under 11 U.S.C. 365(d)(4), a nonresidential real property lease is deemed rejected if not assumed or rejected by the earlier of 120 days after the order for relief or plan confirmation; the court may extend 90 days for cause, and any further extension requires the lessor's prior written consent - the practical 210-day outer limit absent landlord consent. Barclay Damon notes that on deemed rejection the debtor must immediately surrender the premises, that landlords commonly trade consent for arrearage cures and fee reimbursement, and that DIP lender milestones often drive lease decisions ahead of the statutory clock. Section 365(d)(3) requires timely performance of post-petition lease obligations. Section 502(b)(6) caps the rejection damages claim at the greater of one year's rent or 15% of the remaining term's rent not to exceed three years, plus unpaid pre-petition rent; Jones Day reported a March 2026 decision applying the cap even to a solvent debtor, reducing a claim from about $9.7 million to about $5.6 million, and Proskauer covers the rent-approach vs. time-approach split in computing the 15%.

**Assignment in a shopping center carries retail-specific tests.** Section 365(b)(3) requires adequate assurance that the assignee's financial condition and operating performance are similar to the debtor's, that percentage rent will not decline substantially, that assumption or assignment will not breach radius, location, use, or exclusivity provisions in the lease or any related master agreement, and that it will not disrupt tenant mix or balance in the center. This is the statutory hook that makes exclusives, radius clauses, and tenant mix underwriting facts rather than soft preferences.

**Go dark is an economic event before it is a legal one.** Cox, Castle & Nicholson's negotiation checklist covers requiring an initial period of continuous operation, 90 to 180 days' advance notice, no-default conditions, continued rent, continued maintenance and insurance, landlord recapture and termination rights, and landlord recovery of unamortized TI allowance and brokerage commissions. A tenant that goes dark while paying rent still degrades traffic and can trip co-tenancy rights held by other tenants.

**Backfill cost is measurable from open-air comparables.** Kimco reported 1,557 leases in the consolidated operating portfolio in 2025 across roughly 10.8 million SF, split 502 new leases and 1,055 renewals and options, with leasing costs of approximately $149.2 million, or $41.65 PSF, comprising $115.6 million of tenant improvements and $33.6 million of external leasing commissions, and average rent of $22.61 PSF on new leases versus $21.50 PSF on renewals and options. Simon reported average annual initial base minimum rent on new leases of $65.09 PSF in 2025 with average tenant allowance of $63.92 PSF. The renewal-versus-new-lease cost gap is the core of renewal probability economics.

**Portfolio context for 2025 year-end.** Kimco: proportionate portfolio occupancy 96.4%, consolidated operating portfolio 96.6% leased across 458 centers and 79.5 million SF, with 82% of proportionate ABR from grocery-anchored assets. Simon: U.S. Malls and Premium Outlets ending occupancy 96.4% and average base minimum rent $60.97 PSF; The Mills ending occupancy 99.2% and average base minimum rent $41.24 PSF. Tanger: consolidated occupancy 98% and average annual base rent $27.77 PSF, with fiscal 2025 rental revenues of $550.9 million split $437.3 million fixed and $113.6 million variable (percentage rent plus reimbursements).

**Channel context.** The Census Bureau reported Q2 2026 adjusted e-commerce sales of $340.2 billion, 17.1% of total adjusted retail sales of $1,986.5 billion, with e-commerce up 12.2% year over year against 6.7% for total retail sales. Store-reported sales therefore understate a location's economic contribution wherever the retailer fulfills online orders from or returns to that store.

## Benchmark and Formula Decisions

Suitable as repo defaults:

- Occupancy cost ratio = trailing 12-month total occupancy cost at the location (base rent + percentage rent + CAM + real estate taxes + insurance + marketing/promotional fund) divided by trailing 12-month reported gross sales at the location. Traceable to Tanger 10-K, Morningstar DBRS criteria, and Adventures in CRE.
- Sales PSF = trailing 12-month reported gross sales at the location divided by leased square footage of that location, using the same period on both sides.
- Category screening thresholds, stated as screens rather than pass/fail rules: grocery roughly 2% to 7%; power-center or category-killer anchor roughly 10%, with above-10% triggering a tenant-viability review. Traceable to Morningstar DBRS criteria.
- Restaurant reference point: a large national fast-casual operator reported occupancy cost at 5.2% of revenue for fiscal 2025 (Chipotle 10-K). Use as a benchmark for well-performing QSR/fast-casual, not as a limit for all restaurants.
- Outlet and mall in-line reference point: a national outlet REIT reported portfolio occupancy cost at 9.7% of tenant sales for 2025, trending up from 8.1% in 2021 (Tanger 10-K).
- Bankruptcy timelines and caps: 120 days plus one 90-day for-cause extension under 365(d)(4), with further extensions only on the lessor's prior written consent; 502(b)(6) cap at the greater of one year's rent or 15% of remaining term rent capped at three years, plus unpaid pre-petition rent.
- Section 365(b)(3) adequate assurance items as the checklist for underwriting assignment risk in a shopping center.

Case-by-case only, do not hard-code:

- Any single "healthy" occupancy cost percentage applied across categories. Adventures in CRE is explicit that the answer depends on retail type, tenant, how the store compares to the tenant's other stores, and the tenant's importance to the center.
- Sales PSF norms for apparel, jewelry, fitness, medical and service, entertainment, discount, and full-service restaurants. No primary or quasi-primary source retrieved in this session supports category-level sales PSF defaults for those uses.
- The 2003 mall sales-tier bands (9% to 16%) as current cutoffs. Keep the directional relationship; discard the dollar tiers.
- The derived Sprouts figure of roughly $678 to $701 PSF as a grocery norm. It is one specialty grocer, derived by the author from disclosed net sales and square footage, and is presented as such.
- Cap rates, delinquency rates, and store-closure counts. All are dated market observations.

Formulas that belong elsewhere: DSCR, debt yield, NOI build, and risk weighting are already covered in `knowledge/underwriting-calc.md` and `knowledge/risk-scoring.md` and are not restated here.

## Conflicting Source Resolution

- **Category thresholds vs. a single band.** Rating agency criteria (Morningstar DBRS) set category-specific thresholds; the 2003 trade-press summary sets one set of bands for mall shop tenants by sales tier. The rating agency criteria took priority because it is a published lender-side methodology, is far more recent, and is explicitly category-aware. The 2003 bands are retained only as historical context.
- **Occupancy cost components.** Tanger includes advertising and promotions; Morningstar DBRS says "all reimbursable expenses"; Adventures in CRE adds percentage rent. The knowledge base uses the union of these, because a narrower numerator understates the ratio and flatters the tenant. Where a specific lease's charges differ, the lease controls.
- **Bankruptcy lease-decision clock.** Barclay Damon's 2022 piece is framed around a 210-day initial period tied to a temporary amendment that was scheduled to sunset on December 27, 2022. The statute itself, read this session, provides 120 days plus a single 90-day for-cause extension, with further extensions requiring lessor consent. The statute controls; the 210-day figure is the practical outer limit without landlord consent, not the initial period.
- **REIT occupancy cost figures vs. property-level underwriting.** Issuer-disclosed occupancy cost is portfolio-wide, uses issuer definitions, and covers only tenants that report sales. It is a benchmark, not a substitute for the subject property's own tenant sales file.

## Edge Cases and Red Flags

- Tenants with no sales reporting obligation. Most open-air in-line and many net lease tenants never report sales; the ratio simply cannot be computed and should be marked not available rather than estimated.
- Omnichannel attribution. Buy-online-pickup-in-store, ship-from-store, and in-store returns distort reported store sales in both directions; ask how the tenant's report treats them before drawing a health conclusion.
- Partial-year, ramp-up, and remodeled stores. Exclude locations with less than 12 months of reporting, and note remodel or closure downtime.
- Gross vs. net of returns, and exclusions. Leases commonly exclude gift card sales, employee sales, sales taxes, and inter-store transfers from "gross sales"; the exclusion list changes the denominator.
- Percentage-rent-only reporting. Some leases require reporting only to compute percentage rent above a breakpoint, so reports may arrive late, unaudited, or only in months sales exceed the breakpoint.
- Franchisee versus corporate tenants. A branded store operated by a franchisee carries franchisee credit, not brand credit, unless the parent guarantees; verify the actual lease signatory and any guaranty.
- Ground-leased pads and anchors that own their box. Occupancy cost math does not apply the same way when the anchor owns its parcel and is bound by an REA/OEA rather than a lease.
- Solvent-parent assumptions. The March 2026 decision reported by Jones Day shows the 502(b)(6) cap can apply even when the debtor could pay in full; do not underwrite an uncapped landlord claim.
- Anything involving lease interpretation, bankruptcy strategy, tax, or investment suitability requires counsel or the relevant licensed professional. This research is educational decision support only.
