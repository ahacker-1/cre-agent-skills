# Retail Co-Tenancy and Anchor Risk Analyst Research

## Purpose

- Supports `skills/retail/retail-co-tenancy-and-anchor-risk-analyst.md`
- Also supports the co-tenancy, anchor, and REA content in `knowledge/retail-lease-structures.md` and the dark-store and backfill content in `knowledge/retail-tenant-sales-and-occupancy-cost.md`
- Intended users: acquisition, asset management, leasing, and lender diligence teams sizing the risk that an anchor closure or a center-occupancy decline cascades into rent relief, terminations, and re-tenanting cost across a U.S. retail asset

## U.S.-Only Assumptions

- Geography: United States
- Asset types: grocery-anchored, power, strip / unanchored, lifestyle, mall, mixed-use retail, pad, and single-tenant net lease (STNL). Co-tenancy analysis is only meaningful in multi-tenant formats; STNL and pad assets enter this analysis as the anchor or as a parcel bound by an REA, not as the holder of co-tenancy rights.
- Deal type: acquisition diligence, refinancing, and asset review, not development entitlement work
- Legal assumptions: co-tenancy, continuous-operation, radius, and liquidated-damages enforceability is state-law specific. Recorded REA / OEA interpretation is document specific and title dependent. Nothing here is legal, tax, accounting, investment, or financing advice; counsel must review the operative documents.
- Bankruptcy assumptions: U.S. Bankruptcy Code, 11 U.S.C. 365, including the shopping-center-specific adequate assurance provisions

## Key Findings

**Anchor ownership is the first fact, and it is frequently not the landlord.** Macerich's FY2025 10-K reports total anchor GLA of 18,211,000 SF, of which 8,594,000 SF is owned by the anchor and 9,617,000 SF is leased, and states that anchors either own their stores and the land under them or hold long-term leases at rates below those charged to mall and freestanding stores, with owner-anchors entering reciprocal easement agreements with the center owner. Regency's 10-K separately describes "shadow anchors" - retailers who own space just outside or adjacent to the center, who appear to consumers as center tenants and whose vacancy would reduce center traffic without the landlord holding any lease at all. CBL states that replacement difficulty "has been exacerbated if the Anchor space is owned by a third party and we are not able to acquire the space." Brixmor's stated acquisition strategy expressly includes buying non-owned anchor spaces adjacent to its centers, which is the market's practical answer to the control problem.

**Anchor rent contribution and anchor risk contribution are inversely related.** Macerich reports that anchors accounted for approximately 6.9% of total rents for FY2025. Regency's FY2025 leasing table shows new anchor-space leases at $17.46 PSF base rent against $28.67 PSF of tenant allowance and landlord work plus $4.65 PSF of leasing commissions, versus renewals at $15.14 PSF with $0.65 PSF of allowance. The anchor is a small share of income, a large share of traffic, and the most capital-intensive box to re-lease. That asymmetry is the entire case for modeling a cascade rather than a single line-item vacancy.

**The cascade mechanism is disclosed identically across formats.** Regency: if a significant tenant vacates, co-tenancy clauses "in select leases may allow other tenants to modify or terminate their rent payment or other lease obligations," with three named variants - postponed opening, right to close early, and, "more commonly," reduced rent until a certain number of tenants open. Brixmor: "a failure by an anchor tenant to occupy their leased premises could potentially trigger lease termination rights or reductions in rent due from certain other tenants in that shopping center." CBL: co-tenancy and sales-based kick-out provisions allow reduced rent and in certain instances termination "if we fail to maintain certain occupancy levels or retain specified named Anchors," and a tenant bankruptcy that results in lease rejection can itself trigger co-tenancy clauses in other leases. Macerich states that anchor or small-tenant closures may trigger co-tenancy clauses but that it does not anticipate a significant negative revenue impact - which is a management view about magnitude, not a denial of the mechanism.

**An anchor can go dark without breaching anything.** Regency's risk factor states that some anchor tenants "have the right to vacate their space and may prevent us from re-tenanting by continuing to comply and pay rent in accordance with their lease agreement." A dark-but-paying anchor is invisible in a rent roll and fully live in every co-tenancy clause at the center. The ICSC 2025 operating covenants materials make the operational point directly: before granting any tenant the right to go dark, even temporarily, the landlord must first assess the impact on co-tenancy provisions in other leases at the center.

**Co-tenancy triggers are drafted three ways and often combined.** Cox, Castle & Nicholson describes key-tenant definitions built on named anchors, on mini-majors of roughly 15,000 to 30,000 SF, and on occupancy thresholds, with combined requirements such as one of two anchors, four of seven mini-majors, and 75% of shop space or at least 100,000 SF. The JJD-HOV lease is a clean real-world example: three anchor tenants or comparable substitutes, or 60% of gross leasable area occupied excluding the tenant's own space.

**Remedies follow a recognizable ladder, but the numbers are negotiated.** Cox Castle describes rent abatement often at 50% of fixed rent, termination rights that typically require the violation to persist a minimum of six months and often a year, and a return to full rent (or landlord recapture) after about one year if the violation continues and the lease survives. The ICSC 2025 materials list the landlord-side limitations that decide how expensive a clause actually is: a cure period, a forced election between termination and rent reduction, remedies limited to fixed rent reductions rather than percentage rent, carve-outs for casualty, remodeling, and force majeure, landlord rights to propose replacement tenants, and a requirement that the tenant demonstrate measurable damage before invoking relief. None of these are defaults; each is present or absent in the specific lease.

**Substitute rent survives challenge where the landlord has real choice.** In JJD-HOV Elk Grove, LLC v. Jo-Ann Stores, LLC (S275843, decided December 19, 2024), the Supreme Court of California treated a co-tenancy substitute-rent provision as valid alternative performance rather than an unenforceable penalty. The provision required three anchor tenants or 60% GLA occupancy; substitute rent was the greater of 3.5% of gross sales or $12,000 monthly against fixed minimum rent that Phillips Lytle reports at $42,292 monthly, with $638,293 in disputed back rent. The court's dividing line from Grand Prospect was landlord control: JJD owned the shopping center and could recruit tenants, where the Grand Prospect landlord was powerless over a fee-owning co-tenant. This is California law only, and it cuts toward enforceability of well-drafted substitute-rent clauses rather than against them.

**REA / OEA restrictions decide whether a backfill is even legal.** The ICSC REA materials state that approval rights under a center-wide REA are often limited to the master developer and the top few anchor retailers who own their own parcels, that anchor approving parties commonly control permissible building areas, roadways, drives, parking, and access points, and that site plan controls "are quite often the biggest contractual impediment to effecting much needed redevelopment projects at shopping centers," with retailers frequently requiring significant economic concessions to consent to deviations. Prohibited-use lists commonly bar exactly the categories that now backfill dark boxes - fitness, entertainment, bars, office, hotel, and residential uses - and the materials note that antiquated prohibited-use restrictions are causing centers, especially enclosed malls, to decline faster. Exclusive uses survive unless the REA contains a "use it or lose it" clause extinguishing the exclusive when the benefited retailer closes or changes use; without it, dead exclusives linger as clouds on title.

**Bankruptcy is a scheduled event, not an open-ended one.** Under 11 U.S.C. 365(d)(4) the lease must be assumed or rejected by the earlier of 120 days after the order for relief or plan confirmation. Under 365(f)(1) anti-assignment provisions are overridden. But 365(b)(3) gives shopping centers four specific protections on assumption or assignment: adequate assurance of the source of rent and of an assignee whose financial condition and operating performance are similar to the debtor's at lease inception; that percentage rent will not decline substantially; that the assignment is subject to all lease provisions including radius, location, use, and exclusivity, and will not breach any such provision in any other lease, financing agreement, or master agreement relating to the shopping center; and that it will not disrupt tenant mix or balance. Those are the levers a landlord actually holds when an anchor files.

**Replacement-anchor probability is real but category-specific and box-specific.** Sprouts opened 37, 33, and 30 new stores in fiscal 2025, 2024, and 2023 and plans more than 40 openings in fiscal 2026. Burlington ended its fiscal year at 1,212 stores, plans approximately 110 net new stores in the year ending January 30, 2027, and discloses acquiring leases through bankruptcy proceedings that produced $35.5 million and $15.7 million of pre-opening costs in the two most recent fiscal years - a direct, filed statement that backfilling bankrupt retailers' boxes is a core growth channel. Brixmor's disclosure supplies the pricing side: the weighted average expiring ABR PSF of anchor expirations through 2028 is $11.37 against $17.84 PSF on new anchor leases signed during 2025. Against that, CBL discloses the opposite tail - department store market share declining, traffic-driving ability substantially decreased, replacement anchors that are not department stores demanding higher allowances or less favorable terms, and delay in leasing space adjacent to a vacant anchor.

**Occupancy is not one number.** Regency reports FY2025 pro-rata percent leased of 96.1% overall, 98.0% for anchor space (spaces at or above 10,000 SF), and 93.2% for shop space (under 10,000 SF). A co-tenancy occupancy threshold is measured on whatever base the clause names, which is frequently GLA excluding the subject tenant, and rarely matches the leased-occupancy figure on the rent roll.

## Benchmark and Formula Decisions

Suitable as repo defaults:

- Classify every anchor by ownership before anything else: owned by the landlord and leased, ground-leased to the anchor, owned in fee by the anchor and bound only by an REA, or shadow / non-owned and off the site entirely. Macerich, Regency, Brixmor, and CBL all disclose this distinction as material.
- Build a trigger-to-remedy map: every co-tenancy clause in the rent roll linked to the specific anchor(s) or occupancy threshold that triggers it and to its remedy, cure period, election requirement, sunset, and snap-back. This is a document inventory, not a benchmark, and it travels across all formats.
- Model the cascade in stages rather than as one vacancy: lost anchor rent and recoveries, then triggered abatement and substitute rent, then triggered terminations, then downtime and re-tenanting capital, then the recovery-pool denominator effect. Each stage cites its own lease or REA provision.
- Treat a dark-but-paying anchor as a co-tenancy event, not an occupancy event (Regency; ICSC 2025).
- Test any assumed backfill against recorded REA prohibited uses, exclusives, site plan controls, and approving-party consent rights before it enters the model (ICSC 2018).
- On a tenant filing, run the 365(b)(3) shopping center checklist - source of rent and comparable assignee, percentage rent, radius / location / use / exclusivity compliance across every other lease and financing agreement, and tenant mix - and calendar the 365(d)(4) deadline.

Case-by-case only, no repo default:

- Backfill downtime in months. No source retrieved this session publishes a defensible U.S. average for re-tenanting a vacated anchor. Downtime must come from local comps, broker LOIs, and named tenant demand for that specific box size and configuration. Blog-sourced "industry average" ranges were reviewed and rejected.
- Occupancy thresholds, named-anchor lists, abatement percentages, substitute rent formulas, cure lengths, and termination windows. Cox Castle's 50% abatement and six-to-twelve-month termination window and the JJD-HOV three-anchors-or-60% structure are illustrative negotiated outcomes, not market rules.
- Anchor replacement probability by category. Sprouts and Burlington evidence supports a directional statement that mid-box and grocer demand exists; it does not support a probability table.
- Re-tenanting cost PSF. Regency's FY2025 anchor new-lease figures ($17.46 base rent, $28.67 allowance and landlord work, $4.65 commissions) and Brixmor's $11.37 expiring vs $17.84 new anchor ABR are institutional open-air portfolio figures, usable as a sanity check on a broker's budget, not as a default input.

Formulas the skill uses, all assembled from provisions rather than benchmarks:

```text
Triggered Rent Relief = sum over affected leases of (in-place annual rent - substitute or abated rent) x months in effect / 12
Cascade NOI Loss = lost anchor rent + lost anchor recoveries + triggered rent relief + rent lost to triggered terminations
Backfill Capital = TI or allowance PSF x SF + leasing commissions + landlord work + carry during downtime
Recovery Leakage = anchor share of the CAM / tax / insurance denominator that the pool must re-absorb while the box is dark
```

## Conflicting Source Resolution

- Macerich states it does not anticipate a significant negative revenue impact from triggered co-tenancy clauses, while CBL discloses material difficulty and delay replacing anchors and adverse effects on adjacent space. These are not in conflict on mechanism, only on magnitude at two different portfolios. The skill therefore takes the mechanism from the filings and requires the magnitude to be computed from the subject property's own leases, never assumed from an issuer's outlook.
- Trade press (Bisnow, February 2024) reports strong mark-to-market on recaptured boxes; CBL's FY2025 filing reports replacement anchors demanding higher allowances and less favorable terms. Primary filings take priority, and the resolution is that recaptured-box economics are strongly asset-quality dependent: the same event is accretive in a well-located open-air center and dilutive in a declining enclosed mall.
- Cox Castle's abatement and termination conventions and the ICSC materials' landlord-defense list are both practitioner syntheses. Where they informed a number, the number is labeled a negotiation convention. Where the subject lease says otherwise, the lease controls.
- California's JJD-HOV holding is favorable to co-tenancy enforceability; Grand Prospect went the other way on different facts. Neither is generalizable outside California, and the skill flags enforceability as a counsel question in every jurisdiction rather than importing a rule.

## Edge Cases and Red Flags

- Anchor is dark but current on rent. It does not appear as vacancy anywhere in the rent roll and may already be running co-tenancy relief at the shop level.
- Anchor owns its box in fee and is bound only by an REA. There is no lease to abstract, no term to model, no landlord remedy, and possibly a permanent operating-covenant expiration that already lapsed.
- Shadow anchor sits outside the property boundary. Its closure hits traffic and can satisfy a named-anchor trigger drafted by trade name rather than by parcel, with no landlord control at all.
- Reduced or substitute rent is already in effect and is presented in the rent roll as in-place rent, so the underwriting starts from an already-impaired number and shows no downside.
- A co-tenancy remedy has no sunset or snap-back, so relief that begins during a lease-up never ends.
- Named-anchor triggers list an entity or trade name that no longer exists after a merger, rebranding, or banner conversion, leaving the trigger arguably live or arguably dead.
- REA operating covenants and lease continuous-operation covenants have different expiry dates, so the box can legally close while the co-tenancy trigger it feeds remains active.
- Recorded exclusives with no use-it-or-lose-it clause survive the closure of the retailer they protected and block the obvious backfill.
- The backfill plan requires a site plan or building area change that needs approving-party consent from an anchor with an economic reason to withhold it.
- Percentage rent decline is used as the adequate assurance argument in a 365 assignment where percentage rent is immaterial, so the landlord's real objection has to run on use, exclusivity, radius, or tenant mix instead.
- Ground-leased anchor parcel that does not participate in CAM, so its vacancy shifts nothing to the pool but takes all of the traffic.
- Kick-out clauses driven by that tenant's own sales, not by the anchor, arriving in the same window as the co-tenancy cascade and compounding it.
