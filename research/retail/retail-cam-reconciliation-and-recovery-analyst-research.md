# Retail CAM Reconciliation and Recovery Analyst Research

## Purpose

- Supports `skills/retail/retail-cam-reconciliation-and-recovery-analyst.md`
- Also supports the recovery sections of [Retail Lease Structures](knowledge/retail-lease-structures.md) and [Retail Benchmarks](knowledge/retail-benchmarks.md)
- Intended users: acquisition, asset management, lease administration, and lender diligence teams testing U.S. retail CAM reconciliations, recovery ratios, and underwritten recovery income

## U.S.-Only Assumptions

- Geography: United States
- Asset types: grocery-anchored, power, strip / unanchored, lifestyle, mall, mixed-use retail, pad, and single-tenant net lease (STNL)
- Deal type: acquisition diligence, refinancing, and asset review, plus annual reconciliation testing during ownership; not development budgeting
- Legal assumptions: recovery, cap, exclusion, gross-up, and audit provisions are contract terms interpreted under state law. Nothing here is legal, tax, accounting, or financing advice, and a reconciliation dispute needs counsel.
- Accounting assumptions: U.S. GAAP under ASC 842. Fixed CAM is fixed lease consideration recognized straight-line; variable reimbursements are recognized in the period the underlying expense is incurred, with estimate-to-actual differences trued up in the following year.
- Convention assumption: "recoverable pool" means the expenses the lease permits the landlord to bill, which is narrower than the property's total operating expenses in every structure reviewed.

## Key Findings

### The recoverable pool is a lease-defined subset, never the operating statement

Every reviewed structure narrows the pool. Cox Castle documents a standard exclusion list (ground rent, debt service, casualty repair costs, tenant improvements, brokers' commissions and attorneys' fees for new leases, costs of services not available to the tenant, and above-fair-market-value costs paid to landlord affiliates), and separately documents that building-related costs such as roofs, common utility systems and exterior painting are customarily inside CAM even though they are not literally common area. The ICSC REA model definition excludes real estate taxes, management and supervisory wages, promotional and holiday event costs, warranty-covered repairs, profit and overhead, legal and accounting services, management fees, salaries above manager level, capital expenditures, and building construction. The two lists disagree on management fees and capital, which is exactly why the pool must be built from the actual documents rather than assumed.

Kimco's FY2025 filing shows the pool can also be wider than expected: many of its leases place roof and structural repair responsibility on the tenant, and many provide for reimbursement of capital expenditures. Brixmor and Simon both describe recovering certain capital expenditures related to maintenance of the properties.

### Denominators differ by format, and anchors are usually outside the pool

Cox Castle sets out three conventions. In regional malls, anchors above a stated square footage are excluded from the denominator, and the anchor's own contribution is first backed out of CAM expenses to reach the net pool the in-line tenants share; the denominator is store floor area occupied and open for business, with tenants negotiating a minimum-percentage floor. In power and neighborhood centers, the share is the tenant's floor area over the floor area of other buildings in the center, with tenants seeking a minimum denominator based on the pre-approved site plan so they do not subsidize unbuilt outparcels. In ground leases, the share is the land area of the leased premises over total land area.

The ICSC REA materials confirm the parcel-level analogue: allocation by store square footage over center square footage or by parcel acreage over center acreage, and enclosed-mall anchors contributing pro rata to exterior maintenance but a fixed amount to interior mall maintenance set in a supplemental agreement between developer and anchor rather than in the REA itself.

Simon's FY2025 filing states the reimbursement obligation applies to a substantial portion of its leases "other than those for anchor stores." Treat anchor exclusion as the mall default and verify it everywhere else.

### Leased occupancy is the wrong denominator; billed or economic occupancy is the right one

Three FY2025 filings quantify the gap. Brixmor reported percent billed of 91.6% against percent leased of 95.1% portfolio-wide at 12/31/2025, and 91.7% billed against 95.3% leased in the 332-property same-property pool; its unit-size table shows the gap concentrating in smaller shop bands (units of 20,000 to 34,999 SF were 90.8% billed against 96.1% leased, while units of 35,000 SF and above were 95.7% billed against 97.6% leased). Kite Realty reported same-property leased occupancy of 95.0% against economic occupancy of 91.7% at 12/31/2025. Phillips Edison reported total leased occupancy of 97.3% split into 98.7% anchor and 95.1% inline.

A recovery model built on leased square footage over-collects on paper by roughly 300 to 400 basis points of GLA at institutional quality, and by more at a center with signed-not-open leases or free-rent periods.

### Gross-up in retail is usually a denominator floor, not an office-style expense gross-up

No reviewed retail source describes the office convention of restating variable expenses to a stabilized occupancy. The retail equivalent documented by Cox Castle is a negotiated minimum percentage of store floor area in the denominator, or a minimum denominator based on the site plan. Both push vacancy cost back to the landlord at a contractually fixed level rather than restating the numerator. Office-style gross-up language does appear in retail leases written on modified gross or base-year forms, which Holland & Knight describes as using a base-year floor; where that form is used, base-year integrity and gross-up must be tested together because an ungrossed base year in a low-occupancy year permanently overstates future pass-throughs.

### Caps: cumulative versus non-cumulative is the largest single modeling error

Cox Castle states caps are fairly unusual, are usually limited to controllable items with real property taxes, insurance, security and utilities excluded at a minimum, and from the landlord's side should be cumulative and compounding so unused cap room from prior years is available later. Lowndes gives the arithmetic at a 5% cap: expenses rising 3% in year 2 leave 2% of unused capacity, so a cumulative cap permits 7% in year 3 while a non-cumulative cap holds the billing at 5%. Kite Realty's FY2025 filing states the general risk directly, noting that stated rent increases or limits on a tenant's obligation to pay its share of operating expenses could be lower than inflation.

### Fixed CAM is now a first-class structure and behaves nothing like pro rata

Simon states that for substantially all U.S. mall leases it receives a fixed CAM payment recognized straight-line under ASC 842. Kimco states certain leases provide a fixed-rate reimbursement of taxes, insurance, utilities and CAM. Tanger describes most leases as using a pro-rata share or escalating fixed contributions. Phillips Edison and Curbline both split reimbursement income into a fixed component recognized straight-line and a variable component recognized as the related expenses are incurred. Under fixed CAM the landlord holds inflation risk, no reconciliation exists for that tenant, and the recovery ratio decays whenever actual costs outrun the contractual bump.

### Administrative and management fees are two different charges with different market ranges

Cox Castle reports an administrative fee usually calculated as a percentage of other CAM expenses at 10-15%, often reduced from 15% to 10% and calculated exclusive of real property taxes and insurance in anchor deals, against a typical third-party management fee of 2.5-4% of gross project revenues; landlords typically expect both, which tenants characterize as double dipping. The ICSC REA materials use a different base for a different actor: where management fees and overhead are excluded from the REA CAM budget, the operator is usually allowed an administrative fee of 4% to 7% of the annual CAM budget. Macerich discloses that its management companies receive fees generally ranging from 1.5% to 4.0% of gross monthly rental revenue.

These are negotiating ranges from three different vantage points, not a market rule. The recurring audit exposure is charging an administrative fee on taxes, insurance and utilities that pass through at cost.

### Reconciliation timing, audit rights, and the cost-shifting threshold

The ICSC REA materials describe the operator performing the annual reconciliation within 90 or 120 days after year end and issuing a statement showing costs incurred, payments made, and the balance or credit due, with owner audit rights and an operator reimbursement obligation if a 3% or 5% discrepancy is found. Cox Castle describes the tenant-side analogue: audit on prior notice, at the landlord's offices, not more often than once per year, within a specified window after delivery of the annual statement, by a reputable national or regional CPA firm not compensated on contingency, with landlord reimbursement of audit cost if the overstatement exceeds a threshold typically ranging from 3-5%. Cox Castle also notes that where the lease has percentage rent, the tenant may seek to mirror the landlord's gross-sales audit standards for its own CAM audit.

Simon's accounting policy provides the year-two mechanic: estimated recoveries are accrued as the expense is incurred and the difference between estimate and final billed amount is recognized in the subsequent year. Federal Realty and Macerich describe the same accrual pattern.

### Observed recovery ratios cluster below 100 percent even at institutional quality

Phillips Edison supplies the definition: recovery rate is total recovery income divided by total recoverable expenses for the period. Applying it to disclosed figures:

- Regency FY2025 pro-rata same-property recoveries of $404,326K against operating and maintenance of $265,592K plus real estate taxes of $205,725K is roughly 86%.
- Rock River Plaza in the Wells Fargo 2026-5C10 pool, a Walmart and Lowe's anchored center in Moline, IL: underwritten recoveries of $531,720 against underwritten real estate taxes, insurance and other operating expenses of $637,632 is roughly 83%; the 2025 actuals of $452,376 against $555,002 are roughly 82%. Anchor-heavy centers recover less because anchors self-maintain or pay fixed contributions.
- Astor Place Retail in the same pool, a New York street-retail asset whose anchor tenant is on a modified gross lease: underwritten recoveries of $1,368,446 against underwritten taxes, insurance and other operating expenses of $1,775,605 is roughly 77%.

The management fee is deliberately excluded from those denominators. Including the underwritten management fee at Rock River Plaza moves the ratio from roughly 83% to roughly 70%, which is why the ratio must state its own denominator.

### Underwritten recoveries are a separate number that must be reconciled to the leases

The Wells Fargo 2026-5C10 cash-flow format is explicit: Base Rent, then Grossed Up Vacant Space, then Gross Potential Rent, then Total Recoveries, then Vacancy and Credit Loss, then Effective Gross Income. Vacant space is grossed up on the rent line and then removed through the vacancy deduction, while Total Recoveries is stated as a single underwritten figure. At Rock River Plaza the underwriter carried recoveries above the trailing actuals ($531,720 UW against $452,376 in 2025) while also underwriting economic occupancy of 95.0% against 100.0% physical in 2025. That combination is exactly the seam where recovery assumptions and occupancy assumptions can double count, and it is why a recovery model has to be rebuilt lease by lease rather than trended.

## Benchmark and Formula Decisions

Suitable as repo defaults:

```text
Recovery Ratio = Total Recovery Income / Total Recoverable Expenses
Recovery Leakage ($) = Recoverable Expenses in Pool - Recovery Income Billed
Tenant Share = Tenant GLA / Denominator GLA (denominator per the lease, not per the rent roll)
Net Pool = Gross Recoverable Expenses - Anchor and Fixed-CAM Contributions Backed Out
Cumulative Cap Ceiling (year n) = Prior Billable Base x (1 + cap rate) + Unused Cap Carryforward
Non-Cumulative Cap Ceiling (year n) = Prior Billable Base x (1 + cap rate)
```

- State the denominator of any recovery ratio (with or without management fee, with or without taxes) every time it is quoted. The Rock River Plaza swing from roughly 83% to roughly 70% on that choice alone is the reason.
- Use billed or economic occupancy, never leased occupancy, as the recovery denominator. The 300 to 400 basis point institutional gap is documented above.
- Treat a stabilized open-air center recovering materially less than roughly 85% of operating and maintenance plus real estate taxes as requiring a structural explanation, consistent with the Regency-derived figure already published in [Retail Benchmarks](knowledge/retail-benchmarks.md). Anchor-heavy power centers legitimately sit lower, near the low 80s on the same evidence.
- Reconciliation delivery inside 90 to 120 days after year end and audit cost-shifting at a 3% or 5% overstatement are conventions, not rules, and both are sourced above.

Case-by-case only, with no repo default:

- Administrative fee percentage, management fee percentage, and whether either is charged on uncontrollable pass-throughs. Three sources give three different bases (10-15% of other CAM expenses; 4-7% of the REA CAM budget; 1.5-4.0% of gross monthly rental revenue).
- Cap percentages, cap base years, and the controllable/uncontrollable line.
- Capital amortization periods and interest rates, and the initial-years capital exclusion window (Cox Castle observes 5-7 years in new development and acquisition contexts, described as a negotiated outcome).
- Any per-square-foot CAM, tax, or insurance figure. No reviewed source supports a national retail default.

## Conflicting Source Resolution

- Management fees inside CAM: the ICSC REA model definition excludes management fees and overhead from the CAM budget, while Cox Castle describes landlords expecting both a third-party management fee and an administrative fee inside CAM Expenses. These are not truly in conflict because they describe different instruments (an owner-to-owner REA budget versus a landlord-to-tenant lease). Resolution: build the pool separately at the REA level and at the lease level, and never assume the lease pool equals the REA pool.
- Roof and structure: Cox Castle treats roof and exterior building costs as customarily inside CAM, while Kimco discloses that many of its leases require the landlord to make roof and structural repairs and a number place that responsibility on the tenant. Resolution: primary filings show real variance, so this is a lease-by-lease extraction, not a convention.
- Cap direction: Cox Castle argues caps should be cumulative and compounding from the landlord's perspective; Lowndes states tenants generally prefer non-cumulative. Both agree on the mechanics. Resolution: the mechanics are the finding; the direction is negotiation.
- Recovery ratio level: Regency's disclosure-derived roughly 86% is higher than the roughly 82-83% derived from a Walmart and Lowe's anchored loan in the Wells Fargo pool. Resolution: both are correct for their format. Portfolio disclosures skew to grocery-anchored and mixed-use, anchor-heavy centers recover less, and format must be stated with the ratio.
- Where a REIT disclosure and a practitioner article disagree on what is recoverable, the SEC filing governs as evidence of what large owners actually bill, and the subject property's own lease governs the answer.

## Edge Cases and Red Flags

- Fixed CAM tenants inside a pro rata model. There is no reconciliation for them, and their contribution must be backed out of the pool before the pro rata denominator is applied, exactly as Cox Castle describes for mall anchors.
- Anchors that self-maintain their tract under an REA. They may contribute nothing to the lease-level CAM pool while still appearing in the rent roll GLA, silently inflating the denominator.
- Ground-leased pads and outparcels. Cox Castle's land-area share convention means an outparcel's share is computed on a different basis than every in-line tenant, and some pads participate in no pool at all.
- Approved-budget anchors. Where a major tenant must approve the annual CAM budget, a late or unapproved budget can strand a full year of billing. Cox Castle lists the fallback mechanics that should exist and often do not.
- Cost-pooling exclusions. A food court, patio, or enclosed-mall cost pool means the property has more than one denominator; a single blended pro rata calculation will be wrong for every tenant.
- Capital amortization that outlives the lease. Cox Castle notes many leases expire before an amortization period ends and new leases will not pick up pre-commencement amortization, so a recovery model that carries the full annual amortization forward overstates income.
- Base-year and expense-stop retail leases in mixed-use and urban street retail, where the recovery test is base-year integrity rather than pool arithmetic.
- Percentage-rent interaction: Cox Castle notes tenants may mirror the landlord's gross-sales audit standard for CAM audits, so an aggressive sales-audit posture can invite a symmetrical CAM audit.
- Prior-year reconciliations delivered late, never delivered, or delivered without supporting detail. This is the most common source of unbillable and uncollectible recovery income at closing, and it is a purchase-agreement allocation issue as well as an underwriting issue.
- Any conclusion that a reconciliation was mis-billed is a contract interpretation and a potential dispute. Route it to counsel and to the property's lease administration record before it is asserted to a tenant.

## Open Questions

- No reviewed public source publishes retail CAM, tax, or insurance expense per square foot by format, so the skill cannot benchmark the size of a pool, only its recovery percentage.
- Retail expense gross-up to a stabilized occupancy is not documented in any retail-specific source reviewed. The denominator-floor mechanism is documented instead. Whether office-style gross-up is materially used in open-air retail remains unresolved.
- Recovery ratios by format (grocery-anchored, power, strip, lifestyle, mall, mixed-use) are inferred here from a small number of disclosures and two loan-level examples. A wider loan-level sample would firm the ranges.
- State-law treatment of CAM audit findings, limitations periods on reconciliation claims, and the enforceability of audit cost-shifting thresholds were not researched and require counsel.
