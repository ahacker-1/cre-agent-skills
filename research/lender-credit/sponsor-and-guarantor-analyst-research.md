# Sponsor and Guarantor Analyst Research

## Purpose

- Supports `skills/lender-credit/sponsor-and-guarantor-analyst.md`
- Pairs with `knowledge/lender-credit-policy-benchmarks.md` and `knowledge/regulatory-risk-rating-and-classification.md`
- Intended users: bank and credit union credit analysts, underwriters, credit officers, portfolio managers, loan review staff, and special assets officers who must decide how much credit to give a sponsor and a guaranty when sizing, approving, rating, or restructuring a U.S. CRE loan

## U.S.-Only Assumptions

- Geography: United States only. Supervisory guarantor and global cash flow expectations, beneficial ownership identification, OFAC screening, and SBA guaranty rules are U.S. federal constructs with no direct offshore analogue.
- Deal type: income-producing CRE and acquisition, development, and construction lending held on balance sheet by federally insured depositories and credit unions, plus non-bank CRE lenders (debt funds, life companies, CMBS originators) that use the same underwriting inputs under internal rather than supervisory grades.
- Entity assumption: the borrower is a single-purpose entity and the credit support comes from natural persons or upper-tier entities that are not the borrower. Where the borrower is an operating company (owner-user CRE), the SBA program frame in this note is the closer analogue.
- Legal and market assumptions: educational decision support, not legal, tax, investment, accounting, or financing advice. Guaranty enforceability, fraudulent transfer exposure, spousal and community property issues, and entity authority are counsel questions decided under state law. Classification, nonaccrual, and allowance conclusions belong to the institution and its regulator.
- Formula definitions for property-level DSCR, debt yield, LTV, and NOI are not restated here; they live in `knowledge/underwriting-calc.md`.

## Key Findings

### The regulators define the analysis, not the thresholds

- The OCC handbook frames underwriting as "the analysis of the borrower's overall financial condition and resources, the financial responsibility of any guarantor, the nature and value of any underlying collateral, and the borrower's capacity and willingness to repay as agreed." Banks are expected to obtain income, liquidity, cash flow, and contingent liability information on borrowers and guarantors, and to write covenants requiring periodic resubmission so the bank can keep monitoring.
- A bank's CRE loan policy is expected to state standards for evaluating borrower and guarantor creditworthiness and global financial condition across four named dimensions: assets by type, amount, and liquidity; global cash flow; direct and contingent liabilities; and any tertiary repayment sources available on recourse. Policy is also expected to set minimum standards for net worth, cash flow, and debt service coverage, and limits on partial-recourse and nonrecourse loans with guarantor support requirements.
- No federal source publishes a minimum sponsor liquidity or net worth multiple. Those are institution-set. The OCC handbook lists "borrower/guarantor minimum net worth or liquidity" as a common covenant without stating a level, and lists "the borrower's or guarantor's minimum liquidity, net worth, debt-to-worth ratios" as tract-development covenant subjects on the same terms.

### Global cash flow has a defined construction

- The OCC handbook: "Cash flows should be assessed on a global basis. Global cash-flow analyses can be complex and may require integrating cash flows from business financial statements, tax returns, and Schedule K-1 forms for multiple partnerships, limited liability companies, and corporations."
- The analysis must consider required and discretionary cash flows from all activities, actual and contingent liabilities and their effect on repayment capacity, and realistic projections of personal debt payments, property and income taxes, and living expenses. It should focus on recurring cash flows, and treat anticipated capital gains separately where income has historically been capital-gain dependent.
- The decisive instruction: "Comprehensive global cash-flow analyses should be performed despite the presence of significant liquid assets as those assets may be needed to fund other actual or contingent liabilities and other cash flow shortfalls." A large balance-sheet liquidity number is not a substitute for the coverage math.
- The 2023 interagency policy statement supplies the coverage definition, at footnote 17: "Global debt service coverage is inclusive of the cash flows generated by both the borrower(s) and guarantor(s), as well as the combined financial obligations (including contingent obligations) of the borrower(s) and guarantor(s)."
- A workout file that lacks a global debt service coverage analysis with realistic projections and an assessment of the availability, continuity, and accessibility of repayment sources is one of the named gaps that costs an institution the examiner safe harbor for workouts.

### The "financially responsible guarantor" test is a three-part test

- Both the 2023 policy statement and the OCC handbook define a financially responsible guarantor as one possessing (a) the financial ability, (b) the demonstrated willingness, and (c) the incentive to support the loan through ongoing payments, curtailments, or re-margining. The OCC adds that the guarantee must be adequate to support repayment in whole or in part over the remaining term and must be written and legally enforceable.
- The presence of such a guarantee "may be sufficient to preclude adverse loan classification or reduce the severity of the loan classification." That is the practical payoff of the analysis: guarantor strength moves the rating only when all three legs are documented.
- Willingness has an evidentiary test. Examiners "should consider whether a guarantor has demonstrated the willingness to fulfill all current and previous obligations, has sufficient economic incentive, and has a significant investment in the project," and "an important consideration is whether any previous performance under its guarantee(s) was voluntary or the result of legal or other actions by the lender to enforce the guarantee(s)." A guarantor who paid only after suit is not a willing guarantor.
- The OCC handbook adds a presumption that runs the other way at origination: "A presumption of willingness to provide borrower (project) support, when the guarantor has an economic incentive, is usually appropriate unless there is evidence to the contrary." Economic incentive is the pivot; a guarantor with no remaining equity has no presumption behind him.
- Sponsors get the same analysis without the contract. The 2023 statement: "Even if not legally obligated, financially responsible sponsors are similar to guarantors in that they may also possess the financial ability, the demonstrated willingness, and may have an incentive to provide support."

### Contingent liabilities are counted across all lenders, not just yours

- The 2023 statement: "An effective assessment includes consideration of whether the guarantor has the financial ability to fulfill the total number and amount of guarantees currently extended by the guarantor. A similar analysis should be made for any material sponsors."
- The OCC handbook says the same in stronger terms: the assessment "should include consideration of the total number and amount of guarantees currently extended to all lenders, to evaluate whether the guarantor has the financial capacity to fulfill the contingent claims that exist."
- The practical consequence for the REO and contingent liability schedule: the schedule must be complete across all lenders and all entities, and stale or partial schedules are a documentation exception, not a rounding issue.

### Two hard verification rules

- "A guarantor's unpledged assets should not be considered a substitute for project equity." (OCC handbook.) Net worth on a personal financial statement is not equity in the deal.
- "Guarantor liquidity should be verified by the bank." (OCC handbook.) A signed personal financial statement is the borrower's assertion; statements, custodial confirmations, and account verifications are the evidence. The OCC handbook also expects "signed financial statements for borrowers and guarantors" in the loan file, and expects the bank to demonstrate continuous and diligent collection efforts when borrowers or guarantors do not respond to information requests.
- The OCC handbook further directs that the analysis "consider the liquidity of any assets that collateralize the guarantee," which separates a pledged liquid account from an illiquid closely held interest carried at an owner estimate.

### Guaranty forms and the legal mechanics that make them collectible

- The OCC handbook enumerates the limited forms: "interest only, construction completion only, partial principal, reduced (stepped-down) in amount, or released during the loan term as certain conditions are met," and then adds the monitoring obligation: "The bank should monitor and assess the achievement of these conditions before releasing a guarantor of their obligation."
- For nonrecourse loans the handbook describes carve-outs limiting liability "to losses incurred as a result of certain acts or omissions of the borrower (or 'bad acts') such as fraud or misrepresentation, voluntary bankruptcy, environmental issues, unapproved liens, waste of the collateral, prohibited transfers, and the diversion of funds," and notes that "commonly, there are certain carve-out provisions that cause the loan to become full recourse."
- The FDIC exam manual supplies the enforceability mechanics. A guarantee is either of payment (the guarantor pays on default without the holder resorting to any other party) or of collection (the guarantor pays only after the holder reduces a claim against the maker to judgment and execution is returned unsatisfied, or the maker is insolvent). Contracts are further divided into a limited guarantee tied to a specific note or period, and a continuing guarantee enforceable for future transactions until revoked.
- The FDIC manual's execution controls: "Unless the guarantee is given prior to or at the time the initial loan is made, the guarantee may not be enforceable because of the difficulty of establishing that consideration was given. Institutions should not disburse funds on such loans until they have the executed guarantee agreement in their possession." It also directs that the guarantee be signed in the loan officer's presence or notarized, and that for a partnership, joint venture, or corporate guarantor the signing party's authority be confirmed, with counsel consulted where corporate authority is in question. Courts "tend to construe contracts of guarantee strictly against the party claiming under the contract."
- A guarantee also bears on accrual status. Under the FDIC manual, a debt is "well secured" if collateralized by liens or pledges with realizable value sufficient to discharge the debt in full, "or by the guarantee of a financially responsible party" - which links guaranty quality directly to the 90-day nonaccrual exception.
- The NexPoint CMBS guaranty filed 2026-03-31 is a working example of how the paper is built: the guaranteed obligations are the borrower's recourse liabilities plus, "from and after the date that any Springing Recourse Event occurs, payment of all the Debt"; net worth is defined as total assets "exclusive of any interest in the Property or in any other asset that is part of the collateral for the Loan" less total liabilities under GAAP; and the covenant pairs a net worth threshold of $28,500,000 with a liquid assets threshold of $2,850,000, with a transfer restriction that prevents dropping below either. That deal-specific 10-to-1 net worth to liquidity relationship is an illustration of covenant construction, not a market benchmark.

### Ownership, control, and screening

- Under 31 CFR 1010.230, a covered financial institution must identify and verify, for each legal entity customer, each individual who directly or indirectly owns 25% or more of the equity interests (up to four individuals) and a single individual with significant responsibility to control, manage, or direct the entity - "an executive officer or senior manager (e.g., a Chief Executive Officer, Chief Financial Officer, Chief Operating Officer, Managing Member, General Partner, President, Vice President, or Treasurer)" or any other individual regularly performing similar functions. Where a trust owns 25% or more, the beneficial owner is the trustee. The rule expressly permits identifying additional individuals on the basis of risk.
- OFAC's 50 Percent Rule aggregates: an entity owned 50% or more in the aggregate by one or more blocked persons is itself blocked, so 25% held by Blocked Person X plus 25% held by Blocked Person Y blocks the entity. The rule "speaks only to ownership and not to control" - an entity controlled but not 50% owned by blocked persons is not automatically blocked, though OFAC "urges caution" where blocked persons hold a significant sub-50% interest or control by other means.
- NCUA's rule requires "thorough due diligence of the principal(s) to determine whether any related interests of the principal(s) might have a negative impact or place an undue burden on the borrower and related interests with regard to meeting the debt obligations," plus standards for "the financial statement quality and the degree of verification sufficient to support an accurate financial analysis and risk assessment."
- The OCC handbook treats public-record monitoring as ongoing credit administration, not just origination diligence: an updated credit report can show unpaid bills, late vendor payments, or "suits or judgments... entered against the borrower or guarantor(s)," and in many localities banks can access weekly legal and trade reports. It also directs monitoring of real estate tax payments, because delinquent taxes "are nearly always an indicator of a distressed property, borrower, or guarantor."

### Where a program does publish a hard rule

- SBA's SOP 50 10 8.1 (effective 2026-10-01) is the one retrievable U.S. lender program document in this note that sets numeric guaranty and documentation rules: "Any individual who has direct and/or indirect ownership of 20% or more of an Applicant must provide an unlimited full guaranty," with supplemental guarantors permitted "without regard to the percentage of their ownership interests" where credit reasons warrant. SBA Form 413 personal financial statements must be signed and dated within 90 days, and business financial statements within 120 days, of submission to SBA. Where there is a collateral shortfall, lenders must take available equity in solely owned personal real estate of 20%-or-more owners and guarantors, with liens limitable to the shortfall or to 150% of the equity, and property with less than 25% equity need not be pledged.
- NCUA sets the only explicit federal default on whether to take a guaranty at all: a federally insured credit union "that does not require the full and unconditional personal guarantee from the principal(s) of the borrower who has a controlling interest in the borrower must determine and document in the loan file that mitigating factors sufficiently offset the relevant risk." Non-recourse is available, but it must be justified in writing.

## Benchmark and Formula Decisions

Suitable as repo defaults because they are traceable to a primary source:

- Global debt service coverage is defined as the combined cash flows of borrower and guarantor over their combined financial obligations including contingent obligations (2023 interagency policy statement, footnote 17). The skill uses that definition and does not invent an alternative.
- The three-part financially responsible guarantor test - financial ability, demonstrated willingness, incentive - plus written and legally enforceable, and adequate over the remaining term.
- Contingent liability tallies must span all lenders and all entities, not just the subject institution's exposure.
- Guarantor liquidity must be bank-verified; unpledged assets are never project equity.
- Willingness evidence distinguishes voluntary prior performance from performance compelled by enforcement action.
- Beneficial ownership identification at 25% equity plus one control-prong individual; OFAC blocking aggregated at 50%.
- Guaranty enforceability mechanics: consideration timing at or before the initial loan, executed original in hand before funding, signature authority confirmed for entity guarantors, and payment vs collection guarantee distinguished.
- Burn-off and release conditions must be tested and confirmed before release.

Case-by-case only, because no primary source publishes a number:

- Minimum guarantor net worth as a multiple of loan amount and minimum liquidity as a percentage of loan amount or months of debt service. Both are institution-set covenant levels. The skill asks the analyst to state the institution's policy test, the measurement date, and the verification method rather than asserting a market standard. The frequently repeated conventions of net worth at or above the loan amount and liquidity at roughly ten percent of the loan amount could not be tied to any retrievable primary source in this session; the NexPoint guaranty's $28.5 million net worth against $2.85 million of liquid assets is one deal's construction, not evidence of a market rule.
- Minimum sponsor track record - number of comparable deals, units or square feet delivered, cycles operated through. No regulator or agency publishes a count.
- Global DSCR floors. Property-level DSCR conventions live in `knowledge/underwriting-calc.md`; the global figure is compared to the institution's own policy minimum.
- Personal financial statement and tax return staleness limits outside the SBA program. The 90-day PFS and 120-day business statement rule is an SBA 7(a) and 504 program rule and is offered in the skill as a defensible reference point, explicitly labeled as such, not as a bank CRE standard.

Derived guidance, not a sourced benchmark:

- The sponsor risk grade in the skill is a five-band ordinal scale built from the sourced tests above. It is not a regulatory grade and does not map to special mention or substandard on its own. The regulatory classification decision belongs to `knowledge/regulatory-risk-rating-and-classification.md` and the annual review skill.

## Conflicting Source Resolution

- The 2023 policy statement and the OCC handbook state the same guarantor test in slightly different words. The handbook's "financial capacity and ability" and the statement's "financial ability" are treated as the same leg; the handbook's added requirements that the guarantee be adequate over the remaining term and be written and legally enforceable are kept, because they are additive and the handbook is the more operational of the two.
- The handbook's origination-stage presumption of willingness where economic incentive exists appears to cut against the statement's evidence-based willingness test. Resolved by sequence: the presumption is an origination default that survives only until contrary evidence appears, while the statement's test governs once the credit is being classified or worked out. The skill applies the presumption at screening and the evidence test at every review thereafter.
- SBA's 20% ownership guaranty trigger and FinCEN's 25% beneficial ownership threshold are different thresholds for different purposes and are not reconciled into a single number. The skill keeps them separate and labels each with its purpose.
- OFAC's 50% ownership rule and its explicit statement that control alone does not block conflict with the intuition that a controlling blocked person taints the entity. The primary source controls: aggregate ownership blocks automatically, control does not, and sub-50% blocked ownership is a caution flag requiring escalation rather than an automatic decline.
- Where the SBA program rules and bank CRE supervisory expectations differ, the supervisory frame governs investor CRE and the SBA frame governs 7(a) and 504 owner-user credits. Neither is applied to the other silently.

## Edge Cases and Red Flags

- Single-purpose entity borrowers with no operating history: the entity has no credit story, so the whole analysis sits on the sponsor and guarantor. Do not let a clean SPE credit report read as sponsor strength.
- Guarantor net worth concentrated in the collateral property or in other pledged real estate: the NexPoint guaranty's exclusion of any interest in the Property from the net worth definition is the drafting answer; the analytic answer is to restate net worth net of the subject deal and of assets already pledged elsewhere.
- Closely held and non-marketable assets carried at owner estimates on a personal financial statement, with no valuation support. Discount or exclude, and say which.
- Illiquid retirement accounts, restricted stock, and life insurance cash value counted as liquidity without regard to penalty, lockup, or loan-against-policy status.
- Joint, spousal, and community property assets shown on a personal financial statement where only one spouse signs the guaranty. This is a counsel question and a red flag against crediting the full balance sheet.
- A guarantor whose contingent liabilities are disclosed only for the subject lender. Incomplete schedules should be treated as a documentation exception and aged to clearance.
- Multiple guarantors with overlapping assets, where summing individual net worth double counts the same holdings.
- Burn-off or stepped-down guaranties with no defined test, measurement date, or verification obligation; and releases granted without confirming the conditions were achieved.
- A guaranty executed after funding, an entity guarantor signed by a person without authority, or a copy in the file with no executed original.
- Non-marital or offshore ownership layers that stop the beneficial ownership analysis short of a natural person.
- OFAC sub-50% blocked ownership, politically exposed persons, and adverse media hits: escalate rather than clear or decline mechanically.
- Prior guarantee performance that was compelled by litigation, prior deed-in-lieu or short-sale history, prior lender workouts, and personal or entity bankruptcy filings: these go to willingness, and they belong in the narrative even when the numbers are strong.
- Sponsors whose experience is real but in a different property type, market, or business plan than the subject deal. Track record is only relevant experience.
- Guarantor strength used to override a property that does not cover its own debt service. Supervisory guidance treats guarantor support as secondary and supplemental; it can move a rating, but a permanent reliance on outside support is itself the finding.
- Owner-user CRE where the operating company is the real credit. The property analysis and the business analysis both have to clear.
- Non-bank lenders (debt funds, life companies, CMBS originators) applying bank classification vocabulary. The sponsor analysis transfers; the supervisory grade language does not.

## Open Questions

- No retrievable primary source publishes minimum guarantor net worth or liquidity covenant levels for bank CRE. The widely repeated conventions remain unsourced and are intentionally left as institution-set in the skill.
- Agency multifamily sponsor and guarantor requirements (Fannie Mae Key Principal and Guarantor standards, Freddie Mac Optigo borrower principal net worth and liquidity tests) could not be retrieved on the access date; both guide sites returned 403 or JavaScript-only shells to automated retrieval. Confirm those directly in the current Fannie Mae Multifamily Selling and Servicing Guide and Freddie Mac Multifamily Seller/Servicer Guide before relying on any agency-specific number.
- HUD MAP Guide previous participation and Form 2530 review, which is the closest published federal analogue to a formal sponsor track record review, could not be retrieved on the access date.
- State law on guaranty enforceability, spousal consent, community property, anti-deficiency statutes, and one-action rules varies materially and is left to counsel.
- Fraudulent transfer exposure where a guarantor moves assets after a covenant test date is a legal question outside this note.
- Whether and how a sponsor risk grade should feed a dual obligor and facility rating scale is institution-specific; see `knowledge/regulatory-risk-rating-and-classification.md` for the rating system expectations.
