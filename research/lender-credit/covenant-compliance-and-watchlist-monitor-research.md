# Covenant Compliance and Watchlist Monitor Research

## Purpose

- Supports `skills/lender-credit/covenant-compliance-and-watchlist-monitor.md` in the Lender / Credit v1 pack
- Intended users: credit analysts, portfolio managers, loan administration and servicing staff, credit officers, loan review (credit risk review), and special assets officers who monitor a closed U.S. commercial real estate loan between annual reviews
- Secondary users: borrowers and advisors who need to know which delivered numbers a lender tests, what trips a watchlist, and what a waiver or cure actually requires

## U.S.-Only Assumptions

- Geography: United States only. All supervisory, regulatory, and reporting standards cited are U.S. federal banking, credit union, or U.S. CMBS industry standards.
- Institution type: federally insured depository institutions (OCC, Federal Reserve, FDIC supervised) and federally insured credit unions (NCUA), plus CMBS master servicers applying the CREFC Investor Reporting Package and agency multifamily lenders applying published program terms. Debt funds and life companies monitor to their own documents and internal grades; the interagency classification vocabulary does not apply to them.
- Deal type: closed, income-producing CRE loans and CRE construction loans in the monitoring period between closing and maturity, including loans in short-term accommodation.
- Document assumption: the loan agreement, note, mortgage or deed of trust, guaranty, cash management agreement, and any modification control. Every covenant test below is only as good as the definition written into those documents.
- Legal assumption: this is educational decision-support material, not legal, tax, investment, accounting, or financing advice. Default declarations, notices, reservation-of-rights letters, waivers, forbearance, classification, nonaccrual, and charge-off decisions belong to the institution and its counsel.

## Key Findings

### The covenant set is short and the definitions are the whole argument

- The OCC names five financial covenants as the common set for income-producing CRE loans: debt yield, DSCR, LTV, LTC, and borrower or guarantor minimum net worth or liquidity. The handbook adds that other covenants exist depending on the complexity or type of loan.
- The same handbook warns that where loan documents contain debt service coverage covenants, the definitions of income and expenses should be clearly defined, and that the covenant compliance calculation may differ from the DSCR used for underwriting and risk rating. This is the single most common source of a false pass or a false trip: the analyst runs the underwriting formula against a covenant written on different terms.
- For construction and development loans the OCC lists a different covenant family: speculative unit limits, unsold unit limits across all of a builder's projects, raw land inventory limits, limits on additional debt, guarantees, and liens, minimum liquidity, net worth, and debt-to-worth for borrower or guarantor, and restrictions on distributions to partners or owners before repayment.
- Formula definitions are not restated here or in the skill. They belong to `knowledge/underwriting-calc.md`.

### Reporting frequency is a policy decision keyed to property stability, not a market standard

- The OCC states that loan covenants should require periodic financial information on the project, the borrowing entities, and any guarantors, and that frequency should consider the stability of the property. Annual operating statements and rent rolls may be adequate for a property with few tenants on long-term leases extending beyond the loan term, or for stabilized multifamily. Properties in lease-up, or nonresidential properties with many tenants or frequent lease expirations, could warrant monthly, quarterly, or semiannual information.
- NCUA regulation is explicit that the commercial loan policy must address the use of covenants including the frequency of borrower and guarantor financial reporting, and periodic loan review consistent with those covenants.
- The OCC also expects receipt and analysis to be tracked, so management can evaluate the effectiveness of the monitoring program, and expects that when borrowers or guarantors do not respond, collection efforts remain continuous and diligent. Non-delivery is itself a monitored condition, not a filing gap.
- In CMBS, CREFC treats a failure to submit financial statements as required by the loan documents for four consecutive quarters as an Informational watchlist trigger (code 1I), released on submission of complete statements resulting in a reported OSAR.

### Delinquent taxes and insurance are treated as credit events, not administrative items

- The OCC states that delinquent real estate taxes threaten the lender's interest in the collateral and are "nearly always an indicator of a distressed property, borrower, or guarantor," and lists delinquent real estate taxes both as a warning indicator and as a well-defined weakness supporting a substandard classification.
- CREFC codes reach the same conclusion mechanically: verified non-escrowed taxes more than 60 days past due is a Credit trigger (1B), delinquent or force-placed property or liability insurance more than 60 days is an Informational trigger (1C), and outstanding servicing advances more than 30 days delinquent and over $10,000 in aggregate is a Credit trigger (1D).
- Freddie Mac's published conventional fixed-rate terms state that tax and insurance escrows and replacement reserve deposits are generally required, which is why an escrowed agency loan and an unescrowed bank loan need different monitoring routines for the same risk.

### Cash management and sweeps are structural triggers with their own cure arithmetic

- The 2026 CMBS prospectus retrieved shows the standard mechanics: a hard lockbox on commercial income and a soft lockbox on residential income with springing cash management; rents deposited into a lender-controlled lockbox within two business days; on a trigger, tenant direction letters within five business days and a daily sweep into a lender-controlled cash management account; and excess funds released to the borrower only while no trigger period is in effect, otherwise to a lender-controlled excess cash flow account.
- Trigger definitions in that filing are short and testable: for one loan, an event of default, a significant tenant trigger, or DSCR below 1.15x, cured at 1.20x for two consecutive quarters; for another, an event of default or DSCR below 1.10x as of any determination date, cured at 1.15x for six consecutive months with a clean six-month period.
- CREFC code 2C makes any occurrence of a servicing trigger event in the loan documents (springing lockbox, establishment of a letter of credit, trapping excess cash) a Credit watchlist trigger, released on cure of the event or establishment of the required lockbox or letter of credit.
- The practical rule is that a cure threshold is almost always higher than the trip threshold and requires sustained performance over consecutive periods. A single strong quarter does not release a sweep.

### Watchlist triggers: the CREFC Portfolio Review Guidelines are the only published, code-level trigger grid

- The Servicer Watchlist is a monthly report prepared by the master servicer under the Portfolio Review Guidelines, organized in seven categories: financial conditions, borrower issues, property condition issues, lease rollover / tenant issues / vacancy, maturity, other (servicer discretion), and loans returned from the special servicer. Any one triggered item lists the loan; each trigger has a matched release threshold; each code is labeled Credit or Informational.
- Named thresholds worth carrying into a bank context as calibration, not as policy: NCF DSCR below 1.10x (1.20x for healthcare and lodging) as a Credit trigger; NCF DSCR below 1.40x and below 75% of underwritten DSCR as an Informational trigger; floating-rate DSCR below 1.0x and below 90% of underwritten in-place NOI; delinquent principal and interest of more than two payments due; occupancy below 80% of underwritten for fixed-rate loans, below 90% of in-place tenants as of underwriting for floating-rate loans, or below 80% outright for multifamily; a single tenant or any tenant over 30% of NRA expiring within 12 months for loans of $30 million or more, or within 6 months for smaller loans, or on any notice of non-renewal; any top-three tenants each at 5% or more of NRA and together over 30% expiring within 6 months; a major tenant over 30% of NRA in default, terminated, or dark; bankruptcy of borrower, owner, or guarantor on notice; ground lease expiring within 6 months or in default; a property inspection scored 4 or 5 on the MBA form; and pending maturity or anticipated repayment date with a balloon due in less than 90 days.
- The catch-all (6A) is instructive for bank use: any other situation indicating increased default risk that may create potential material losses, with named examples of an unplanned draw on a letter of credit or reserve to pay debt service, or three or more delinquencies in a trailing twelve months.
- Loans returned from the special servicer stay listed for three months absent another trigger, and loan modifications that created a B note, cumulative interest shortfalls, and WODRA repayment periods stay listed until payoff.

### Being current is not evidence of soundness

- The OCC handbook states that timely payments are not by themselves a fully reliable indicator of future performance, that being contractually current can mask a troubled loan when repayment sources are inadequate or other obligations go unpaid, and that late or missed payments may not occur until a credit has already deteriorated significantly. Cash flow should cover all of the borrower's obligations, not just this loan's debt service.
- The Eagle Bancorp FY2025 10-K quantifies the point from the lender side: at 2025-12-31, 99% of its special mention loans and 79% of its substandard loans were current.
- The same handbook flags the two structures that most often produce a current-but-troubled loan: interest reserves that continue to be applied to interest payments while expected leases or sales have not occurred, and liberal use of extensions or renewals that obscures an inability to meet reasonable repayment terms. Repacking a depleted interest reserve with new debt is called out as a red flag indicating possible credit deterioration.

### Early warning indicators are published, and they are mostly not payment data

- The OCC's list of warning indicators for CRE: delinquent real estate taxes; declining sales prices or rental rates; cancellations of sales contracts or reservations; liberal or unusually generous concessions including rent, tenant improvement allowances, moving allowances, and lease buyouts; slower absorption than anticipated; delinquent lease payments from major tenants; increasing vacancy and turnover; changes to the initial concept or development plan; construction budget overruns and reallocation requests; draw requests ahead of schedule; construction delays; liens from worker or supplier payment disputes; borrower requests for additional financing due to unanticipated costs; deterioration in the performance of the borrower's other properties or businesses; repacked interest reserves; and late or delinquent payments.
- Special mention potential weaknesses named for CRE: construction delays, changes in concept or project plan, slower than projected leasing, rental concessions, deteriorating market conditions, and impending expiration of a major lease. The handbook says such loans should receive an elevated level of monitoring, which is the definition of a watchlist action.
- FDIC's examination module gives the monitoring checklist a supervisor will test against: independent loan reviews, periodic credit memoranda, loan covenant compliance, loan performance reports, borrower contacts, collateral inspections, property tax status, building code violations, and deviations from loan policy.

### Maturity and refinance risk earn attention without earning a downgrade

- OCC Bulletin 2024-29 defines refinance risk as the risk that borrowers will not be able to replace existing debt at a future date under reasonable terms and prevailing market conditions, and expects banks to track the volume and amount of loans maturing with outstanding principal balances and to evaluate refinance capacity at origination, in ongoing annual monitoring, and near maturity.
- The bulletin also states that increased refinance risk does not automatically warrant a change in a loan's risk rating; classification is appropriate where well-defined weaknesses jeopardize repayment, and otherwise such loans may be flagged for management attention or watch lists.
- CREFC's own maturity trigger is much tighter (a balloon due in under 90 days) because a servicer's job is reporting, not credit administration. A lender running a monitoring routine needs a longer runway to act, which is why the skill uses a 12 to 18 month maturity window as a review prompt rather than a defect.

### Watchlist is a management designation, not a regulatory grade

- The interagency credit risk review guidance names identification or grouping of loans warranting special attention, or designated watch lists of loans management is more closely monitoring, as an attribute of an effective credit risk rating framework, paired with a clear explanation of why those loans warrant attention. The footnote adds that this identification typically also includes loans rated special mention, substandard, doubtful, or loss.
- The 2023 interagency policy statement says loans to sound borrowers modified under prudent underwriting standards should not be adversely classified absent well-defined weaknesses, but could be flagged for management's attention or included in designated watch lists.
- Eagle Bancorp's disclosure shows the common implementation: watch list sits inside the pass grades, above special mention, for credits that are acceptable but need more than normal monitoring.
- Consistent with `knowledge/regulatory-risk-rating-and-classification.md`, watchlist placement is not itself an adverse classification, and the escalation path runs watchlist, then special mention, then substandard.

### Valuation is a monitoring trigger too

- The interagency appraisal guidelines expect collateral risk to be monitored on both a portfolio and an individual credit basis, expect policies for obtaining current valuation information over the life of a credit and for collateral supporting a credit that may be modified or considered for workout, and expect portfolio monitoring criteria for ordering a new appraisal or evaluation that address deterioration in the credit since origination and changes in market conditions.
- The ten validity factors (passage of time, local market volatility, terms and availability of financing, natural disasters, over or under supply of competing properties, improvements to the subject or competing properties, lack of maintenance, changes in economic and market assumptions such as capitalization rates and lease terms, changes in zoning, building materials, or technology, and environmental contamination) are the checklist for deciding whether the LTV covenant can even be tested against the existing appraisal.
- No interagency source sets a reappraisal calendar, so ordering intervals stay case-by-case under board-approved policy.

### Current directional context (dated)

- FDIC Quarterly Banking Profile, second quarter 2026: industry past-due and nonaccrual 1.44%, down 9 basis points; nonfarm nonresidential CRE 1.52%, down 14 basis points and the largest quarterly dollar decline of any loan category; nonfarm nonresidential noncurrent 1.23%; reserve coverage 172.7%.
- Federal Reserve July 2026 Senior Loan Officer Opinion Survey, released 2026-08-03: a moderate net share of banks eased standards on nonfarm nonresidential loans and a modest net share eased on multifamily during Q2 2026; construction and land development standards were basically unchanged; CLD demand was weaker.
- Read together, aggregate delinquency is improving while standards ease. That combination argues for monitoring discipline on the individual credit rather than comfort from portfolio averages, and it is exactly the setting in which a current-but-weak loan escapes attention.

## Benchmark and Formula Decisions

Suitable as repo defaults:

- The five-covenant frame for income-producing CRE (debt yield, DSCR, LTV, LTC, guarantor net worth or liquidity) is sourced to the OCC handbook and can be used as the default covenant inventory.
- The rule that the covenant definition in the loan documents governs the test, and may differ from the underwriting DSCR, is sourced and is a hard rule in the skill.
- Reporting frequency tiering by property stability (annual for stabilized single-tenant or stabilized multifamily; monthly, quarterly, or semiannual for lease-up or multi-tenant with frequent rollover) is sourced to the OCC handbook.
- The tax, insurance, and servicing advance windows (taxes over 60 days, insurance over 60 days, advances over 30 days and over $10,000 aggregate) come from CREFC and are usable as monitoring tripwires for any lender, labeled as CMBS servicing standards.
- The CREFC Portfolio Review Guidelines trigger grid is usable as a published calibration set for building or testing a bank watchlist trigger list, cited as a CMBS servicing standard rather than a bank policy requirement.
- The escalation ladder watchlist, then special mention, then substandard, with watchlist inside pass, matches both the interagency guidance and observed bank practice and is the default in the skill.
- Cure thresholds set above trip thresholds and measured over consecutive periods is a sourced structural pattern from the CMBS filing and is used as an expectation, not a number.
- Freddie Mac's 1.25x minimum amortizing DCR and its LTV grid are program terms usable to test whether an agency loan's delivered performance still sits inside the product's own underwriting floor.

Case-by-case only, no repo default:

- Bank DSCR, debt yield, LTV, liquidity, and net worth covenant levels. No regulator publishes them; the institution's board-approved policy and the specific loan documents control. `knowledge/lender-credit-policy-benchmarks.md` already records that sponsor liquidity and net worth covenant levels have no primary published standard.
- Reappraisal ordering intervals. No interagency source sets a calendar.
- Cure period length, notice and grace periods, and default interest. These are document terms and vary by lender and deal.
- The number of waivers before a loan must be treated as structurally weak. No source sets one; the skill requires waiver history to be disclosed and reasoned rather than counted against a threshold.
- Any occupancy covenant level for a bank loan. The CREFC 80% of underwritten figure is a CMBS reporting trigger, not a lending standard.

Derived guidance carried into the skill:

- Test every covenant on the definition written in the documents, then separately on the underwriting definition, and report both when they diverge.
- Treat non-delivery of required financials as a monitored condition with its own aging, not as a pending item.
- Use a 12 to 18 month maturity window as a monitoring prompt, with a documented refinance capacity view, because OCC Bulletin 2024-29 requires the tracking but forbids the automatic downgrade.
- Never remove a loan from the watchlist on one period of improvement; require the condition to be cured against a stated release threshold, mirroring the CREFC release-threshold construction.

## Conflicting Source Resolution

- CREFC's DSCR watchlist triggers (Credit at NCF DSCR below 1.10x, Informational at below 1.40x and below 75% of underwriting) sit well below typical bank covenant levels and below Freddie Mac's 1.25x minimum amortizing DCR. These are not in conflict; they answer different questions. CREFC sets a bondholder reporting floor, the agency sets an origination floor, and a bank covenant sets a contractual test. The skill keeps them separate and labels each.
- The OCC says loans should not be adversely classified solely because collateral value has fallen below the balance, while the appraisal guidelines expect active collateral value monitoring. Resolution: monitor and document value, order a new valuation when the credit has deteriorated or market conditions have changed, and use the result to inform loss exposure and structure, not to drive a rating by itself. The interagency guidance controls on the rating question.
- CREFC's maturity trigger of under 90 days conflicts with practical bank monitoring lead times. Resolution: the CMBS trigger is a reporting obligation, and OCC Bulletin 2024-29 governs a lender's monitoring behavior, so the longer window controls for a lender-side routine.
- Payment status as an indicator: common practice builds watchlists off delinquency, while the OCC handbook says late or missed payments may not occur until a credit has already deteriorated significantly, and the Eagle Bancorp filing shows most criticized loans current. Resolution: delinquency is a trigger but never a screen. Regulator guidance and the primary filing both control over practice convenience.
- Where an institution's own board-approved policy sets a stricter trigger than anything cited here, the policy controls. Supervisory material sets floors and expectations, not the institution's operating thresholds.

## Edge Cases and Red Flags

- Covenant definitions that differ between the loan agreement, the guaranty, and the cash management agreement, so the same loan trips at three different levels.
- A DSCR covenant with no stated management fee, replacement reserve, or ground rent treatment, making the number unauditable against the delivered statements.
- Borrower-prepared statements that are unaudited, cash basis, or annualized from a partial period. Cash basis statements understate expenses where taxes went unpaid, which is the precise scenario that also trips a tax trigger. CREFC allows annualization only with at least six months of data.
- Occupancy measured as physical, economic, or leased. A leased-occupancy covenant can pass while economic occupancy collapses through free rent or a dark but paying tenant.
- Signed-not-commenced leases counted as occupancy or as NOI.
- A borrower who cures a DSCR test by prepaying principal or posting a letter of credit rather than by property performance. CREFC's catch-all names an unplanned draw on a letter of credit or reserve to pay debt service as its own trigger.
- Serial waivers, or a modification that resets the covenant to the delivered number, which removes the test rather than curing the condition.
- Loans in short-term accommodation that are never entered into the grading system as modified, so watchlist volume and migration reporting understate the portfolio.
- Guarantor covenants tested against an unverified personal financial statement, or a burn-off condition released without a documented test.
- A property inspection that has not been performed in the monitoring cycle, so property condition triggers cannot be evaluated at all.
- Non-bank lenders. A debt fund, life company, or CMBS borrower-side matter has no interagency classification vocabulary; use the document's own trigger language and the servicing standard.
- Anything requiring a default declaration, notice, reservation of rights, waiver, or forbearance is a legal act and belongs to counsel and the institution.

## Open Questions

- No public source establishes a standard number of consecutive covenant breaches or waivers after which a loan must be criticized. Left to policy and judgment.
- Reappraisal ordering intervals for watchlisted but performing CRE loans remain unpublished by the agencies.
- Bank-side occupancy covenant levels by property type have no published benchmark set; only the CMBS reporting triggers are public.
- Whether a CREFC IRP version later than 8.4 has been issued was not confirmed this session, because the CREFC IRP landing page was not retrieved; the v8.4 PDF was retrieved directly. Check before the next update.
- Life company and debt fund monitoring covenant conventions are not published in a form comparable to the agency product sheets, so those lanes remain described qualitatively.
