# Regulatory Risk Rating and Classification Research

## Purpose

- Supports `knowledge/regulatory-risk-rating-and-classification.md` in the Lender / Credit v1 pack
- Intended users: credit analysts, underwriters, portfolio managers, credit officers, loan review (credit risk review) staff, and special assets officers at U.S. banks, credit unions, and other regulated CRE lenders
- Secondary users: borrowers and advisors who need to understand how a regulated lender will grade, report, and monitor their loan

## U.S.-Only Assumptions

- Geography: United States only. All classification, nonaccrual, allowance, and appraisal rules cited are U.S. federal banking and credit union supervisory standards.
- Institution type: federally insured depository institutions (OCC, Federal Reserve, FDIC supervised) and federally insured credit unions (NCUA). Debt funds, life companies, and CMBS originators are not subject to these classification regimes and use internal or rating-agency frameworks instead; the knowledge base flags this explicitly.
- Deal type: income-producing commercial real estate loans, CRE construction and development loans, and CRE loans in accommodation or workout status.
- Accounting assumption: the institution has adopted ASC Topic 326 (CECL) and ASU 2022-02. Institutions that have not adopted these standards follow different (legacy TDR) reporting, per the 2023 interagency policy statement.
- Legal assumption: this is educational decision-support material, not legal, tax, investment, accounting, or financing advice. Classification, nonaccrual, charge-off, and allowance decisions are the institution's own and are subject to examiner review.

## Key Findings

### Classification definitions are interagency and unchanged

- The agencies use one common supervisory scale: pass, special mention, substandard, doubtful, loss (OCC Rating Credit Risk handbook). No formal regulatory definition of "pass" exists; pass is the residual for credits not covered by the four adverse or watch definitions.
- Special mention: potential weaknesses deserving management's close attention that, if uncorrected, may deteriorate repayment prospects or the institution's credit position at a future date. Special mention assets are explicitly not adversely classified. The handbook states special mention "is not a compromise between pass and substandard and should not be used to avoid exercising such judgment."
- Substandard: inadequately protected by the current sound worth and paying capacity of the obligor or the collateral, with a well-defined weakness jeopardizing liquidation of the debt and a distinct possibility of some loss. Typical markers per the handbook: unprofitable operations, inadequate debt service coverage, inadequate liquidity, marginal capitalization.
- Doubtful: all substandard weaknesses plus collection or liquidation in full is highly questionable and improbable. Loss classification is deferred pending a specific event. Nonaccrual accounting treatment is required for doubtful assets (OCC handbook).
- Loss: uncollectible and of such little value that continuance as a bankable asset is not warranted; charged off in the period the obligation becomes uncollectible.
- The 2023 interagency policy statement reproduces the same four definitions verbatim in its Appendix 4, confirming they are current as of the most recent CRE-specific interagency statement.

### Criticized vs classified

- Classified assets are those rated substandard, doubtful, or loss. Classified assets do not include pass and special mention exposures (OCC Rating Credit Risk handbook).
- "Criticized" in common supervisory usage is the broader bucket that adds special mention on top of the classified categories. The OCC handbook and the 2023 statement treat special mention as a separate, non-classified designation, which is what makes the criticized / classified distinction meaningful for reporting.
- Watchlist is an institution-defined category below special mention. The 2023 policy statement says loans modified under prudent underwriting standards should not be adversely classified absent well-defined weaknesses, but "could be flagged for management's attention or for inclusion in designated 'watch lists' of loans that management is more closely monitoring." Watchlist is therefore a monitoring tool, not a regulatory grade.

### Dual risk rating and PD / LGD

- The OCC does not advocate any particular rating system. It expects all rating systems to address both the ability and willingness of the obligor to repay and the support provided by structure and collateral; a single rating or dual ratings may be used.
- Dual rating systems typically assign one rating to the obligor's general creditworthiness and a second to each facility, with the facility rating capturing collateral and structural loss protection. The handbook notes obligor ratings often support deal structuring and administration while facility ratings support allowance and capital estimates.
- Appendix C of the handbook maps PD, LGD, and EL to the regulatory scale: PD measures repayment capacity, and when repayment capacity shows well-defined weaknesses the analysis shifts to secondary sources and expected loss. EL is the product of PD and LGD. The handbook notes these terms are not used in the regulatory definitions but the concepts are inherent to them.
- Stated system expectations from the handbook: board approval and clear accountability; all credit exposures rated; enough grades to differentiate pass credits (most systems require several pass grades); ratings accurate and timely; criteria defined using objective and subjective factors; ratings dynamic so they change when risk changes; the rating process independently validated; back-testing of rating definitions; ratings supported and documented in the credit file.

### Split ratings

- The handbook describes three common split ratings: substandard / doubtful / loss on collateral-dependent loans where collateral value falls in a range; pass / adverse rating where part of the credit has an unquestionable repayment source (cash or liquid collateral, or an unconditional payment guarantee from a responsible, liquid, creditworthy third party); and partial charge-off where the residual serviced balance may merit pass or an adverse rating.
- The 2023 policy statement states the CRE-specific version: for a problem CRE loan dependent on the sale of collateral, the portion adequately secured by the fair value of the real estate less costs to sell generally should be classified no worse than substandard; the excess should be classified loss; a doubtful classification applies to the excess when full loss may be mitigated by pending events or loss is expected but not reasonably quantifiable. The statement adds that examiners should use doubtful infrequently because it is temporary and subject to timely reassessment.

### What must not drive a downgrade by itself

- The 2023 policy statement: examiners should not adversely classify or require partial charge-off on a performing commercial loan solely because the value of the underlying collateral has declined below the loan balance. Well-defined weaknesses that jeopardize repayment are still required.
- The same statement: examiners should not adversely classify loans solely because the borrower is associated with an industry experiencing financial difficulties.
- Renewals or restructurings of maturing loans to commercial borrowers who can repay on reasonable terms are not automatically subject to adverse classification, but should be identified in the internal grading system and may warrant close monitoring.
- OCC Bulletin 2024-29: increased refinance risk does not automatically warrant a change in a loan's risk rating unless well-defined weaknesses jeopardize repayment.
- Conversely, the policy statement warns that being contractually current can be misleading where liberal use of extensions and renewals, or an interest reserve, masks credit weakness; adverse classification may be appropriate for a contractually current loan in that fact pattern.

### Nonaccrual

- General rule (OCC handbook, citing FFIEC call report instructions): do not accrue interest, amortize deferred net loan fees or costs, or accrete discount when the asset is maintained on a cash basis due to borrower deterioration, when payment in full of principal or interest is not expected, or when principal or interest has been in default 90 days or more unless the asset is both well secured and in the process of collection.
- OCC BAAS (August 2026) restates this for commercial loans and adds that if interest or principal has been waived, the loan generally should be placed on nonaccrual; if deferred but not waived, judgment applies.
- "In the process of collection": the BAAS says the commonly applied 30-day collection period is a benchmark, not an outer limit; a longer period does not itself prevent the designation where timing and amount of repayment are reasonably certain.
- Nonaccrual loans will almost always be classified (OCC handbook). Doubtful assets require nonaccrual treatment.
- Return to accrual after restructuring: the 2023 policy statement says a restructured loan in nonaccrual should not return to accrual until the borrower demonstrates sustained repayment performance for a reasonable period, "generally ... a minimum of six months," involving payments of cash or cash equivalents, and that historical periods before the restructuring may count. The OCC handbook independently states restructured loans generally require six months of sustained performance before upgrade to pass. The BAAS says the same six-month figure is a general expectation for a monthly amortizing loan and that neither ASC 942-310-35 nor regulatory policy specifies a minimum.
- Note A / Note B: when a collateral-dependent loan is split, Note A is sized using the fair value of the collateral, may return to accrual where conditions are met, and generally would not be adversely classified; Note B is deemed uncollectible, classified loss, and must be charged off. If the institution does not split the notes but takes an internal partial charge-off, the loan should remain on or be placed in nonaccrual (2023 policy statement).
- Charged-off amounts are never rebooked. The 2023 policy statement footnote and the BAAS both state a charge-off should not be reversed or re-booked under any condition to increase the recorded investment or amortized cost, although expected recoveries are a component of the ASC 326 estimate.

### Modification accounting: ASC 310 to ASC 326

- ASU 2022-02 eliminated TDR recognition and measurement guidance in ASC Subtopic 310-40 for entities that adopted ASC Topic 326, replacing it with the general modification guidance and enhanced disclosures (RSM; confirmed by the 2023 policy statement, which removed TDR discussion from its examples for that reason).
- Institutions that have not adopted ASC Topic 326 or ASU 2022-02 continue to identify, measure, and report TDRs under regulatory reporting instructions (2023 policy statement).
- Post-ASU 2022-02, the reportable event is a "modification to a borrower experiencing financial difficulty" in one or more of four forms: principal forgiveness, interest rate reduction, other-than-insignificant payment delay, and term extension (OCC BAAS, citing ASC 310-10-50-42).
- Under ASC 326, expected credit losses on restructured or modified loans are estimated under the same CECL methodology as all other loans; modified loans are evaluated collectively unless they no longer share similar risk characteristics; changes in credit risk, borrower circumstances, charge-offs, or cash collections often require re-pooling (2023 policy statement Appendix 5).
- For collateral-dependent financial assets, the allowance is measured using the fair value of the collateral (less costs to sell where those costs will reduce cash flows available to repay). Any excess of amortized cost over that fair value is included in expected credit losses, and some or all of that difference may be a loss for classification purposes requiring timely charge-off (2023 policy statement Appendix 5).
- Classification and allowance are not the same measurement. The 2023 policy statement explicitly notes that "loss classifications may not be equivalent to the associated allowance measurements."

### The 2023 policy statement itself

- Adopted June 29, 2023 by OCC, Federal Reserve, FDIC, and NCUA in consultation with state regulators; issued as FDIC FIL-23-034, OCC Bulletin 2023-23, and Fed SR 23-5 (June 30, 2023). It supersedes the 2009 CRE workout guidance (OCC Bulletin 2009-32) and applies to institutions of all sizes, including those with $10 billion or less in assets.
- New relative to 2009: a section on short-term loan accommodations, CECL / ASC 326 references, and updated classification and accounting examples. The agencies deliberately declined to define "short-term accommodation" numerically, preferring flexibility.
- Six workout-program conditions under which examiners will not criticize an institution for engaging in a workout even if the loan is adversely classified: a well-conceived, prudent, documented workout plan per loan (updated borrower / project / guarantor financials, current collateral valuations, appropriate structure and covenants, appropriate legal analysis); global debt service coverage analysis including guarantor obligations; analysis of guarantor cash flow; demonstrated willingness and ability to monitor ongoing performance; an internal risk rating system that accurately and consistently reflects the risk in the workout; and a GAAP-compliant allowance methodology recognizing losses timely through provision and charge-offs.
- "Global debt service coverage" is defined in the statement as inclusive of the cash flows generated by both borrower(s) and guarantor(s) and their combined financial obligations including contingent obligations.
- Guarantees: a legally enforceable guarantee from a financially responsible guarantor may preclude adverse classification or reduce its severity; the guarantor must possess the financial ability, demonstrated willingness, and incentive to support the credit.

### Appraisal and evaluation triggers

- The 2023 policy statement lists six deterioration factors a prudent institution weighs in deciding whether to obtain a new appraisal or evaluation for a CRE loan in workout: performance of the project; conditions for the geographic market and property type; variances between actual conditions and original appraisal assumptions; changes in project specifications; loss of a significant lease or a take-out commitment; and increases in pre-sale fallout.
- A new appraisal may not be necessary where an institution-prepared evaluation appropriately updates the original assumptions to current market conditions and gives a reasonable estimate of fair value. If new money is being advanced, the agencies' appraisal regulations govern whether a new appraisal is required.
- The 2010 Interagency Appraisal and Evaluation Guidelines list ten factors that can invalidate an existing valuation: passage of time; volatility of the local market; changes in terms and availability of financing; natural disasters; limited or over supply of competing properties; improvements to the subject or competing properties; lack of maintenance of the subject or competing properties; changes in underlying economic and market assumptions such as capitalization rates and lease terms; changes in zoning, building materials, or technology; and environmental contamination.
- The 2010 guidelines treat a loan workout (modification adversely affecting collateral protection, renewal or extension, advancement of new monies, restructuring with or without concessions) as a new real estate-related financial transaction. Where no new money beyond reasonable closing costs is advanced, an evaluation may be used in lieu of an appraisal; where new money is advanced, an evaluation may still be used only if there has been no obvious and material change in market conditions and no change in physical aspects threatening collateral protection.
- The agencies reserve the right to require an appraisal or evaluation on an existing real estate credit when there are safety and soundness concerns; an examiner may direct an institution to obtain one and would allow a reasonable time to do so (2010 guidelines).
- Threshold: the CRE appraisal threshold was raised from $250,000 to $500,000 by final rule announced April 2, 2018; an evaluation may be used for exempt transactions. The $250,000 figure in the 2010 guidelines is superseded for CRE. A $1 million real-estate-secured business loan exemption also appears in the 2010 guidelines, conditioned on the loan not being dependent on the sale of or rental income from real estate as the primary repayment source.
- Examiner practice on appraisal assumptions (FDIC Examination Modules 05/24): appraisal assumptions by qualified appraisers appropriate to the property should be given reasonable deference; examiners should not challenge assumptions differing only marginally from norms; adjustments for credit analysis should not be based on unlikely worst-case scenarios; but examiners can adjust estimated value for credit and classification purposes where facts or assumptions are irrelevant or inappropriate or an alternative is supportable. For stale appraisals or material deviations, examiners adjust the estimated value for credit analysis and classification where reasonably possible and supportable.

### Loan review independence and rating migration reporting

- The Interagency Guidance on Credit Risk Review Systems (2020) replaced Attachment 1 of the 2006 ALLL policy statement and is the current standard. It calls credit risk review by any name (loan review, credit review, asset quality review).
- Independence: an independent assessment is achieved when the personnel performing the review do not have control over the loan and are not part of or influenced by individuals associated with the loan approval process. Smaller institutions may use an independent committee of outside directors or qualified staff not involved in originating or approving the specific credits and whose compensation is not influenced by assigned ratings. The credit risk review function is not intended to be performed by internal audit; internal audit must retain the ability to independently audit credit risk review.
- Reporting line: whether or not there is a dedicated department, it is prudent for the credit risk review function to report directly to the board or a board committee. Senior management may handle administrative functions provided independence is not compromised. Outsourcing is permitted, but responsibility stays with the board, and outsourcing to the external auditor raises additional independence considerations.
- Frequency: review and evaluation of significant loans, loan products, or groups of loans "typically annually, on renewal, or more frequently" when internal or external factors indicate potential deterioration.
- Scope (board-approved annually): loans over a predetermined size; a sufficient sample of smaller, new, and new-product loans; loans with higher risk indicators or approved as policy exceptions; portfolio segments with similar risk characteristics; segments experiencing rapid growth; non-lending credit exposures; past due, nonaccrual, renewed, and restructured loans; previously adversely classified loans and loans warranting special attention; insider and affiliate loans; and concentrations.
- Depth includes: credit quality and underwriting soundness, reasonableness of assumptions, creditworthiness of guarantors or sponsors, sufficiency of credit and collateral documentation, lien perfection, proper approvals, covenant adherence, compliance with internal nonaccrual and classification policies, appropriateness of credit loss estimation and timeliness of charge-offs, and the accuracy of risk ratings and timeliness of problem-loan identification by loan officers.
- Rating disputes: resolve differences between loan officers and review personnel through a pre-arranged process, which may include formal appeals and arbitration by an independent party, or may default to the lower-quality rating. Where review concludes credit quality is lower than portfolio management believes, the lower rating typically prevails unless additional information obtains the independent reviewer's concurrence.
- Migration reporting: results go to the board or board committee quarterly, with comparative trends identifying significant changes in overall portfolio quality, adequacy of and adherence to policy, quality of underwriting and risk identification, compliance, and management's response to criticisms. More frequent reporting is expected when material adverse trends are noted. The guidance separately notes that risk rating migrations, concentration trends, and shifting underwriting standards are the kind of trend information the reporting is meant to surface.

### Current supervisory and portfolio context (dated, directional)

- FDIC Quarterly Banking Profile, second quarter 2026: industry past-due and nonaccrual (PDNA) rate 1.44%, down 9 bps from the prior quarter; nonfarm nonresidential CRE PDNA 1.52%, down 14 bps and the largest quarterly dollar decline of any category; nonfarm nonresidential noncurrent rate 1.23%; reserve coverage ratio 172.7%.
- OCC Semiannual Risk Perspective, Spring 2026 (data as of December 31, 2025): CRE headwinds in several property types "have not resulted in significant credit defaults, although refinance risk merits continued attention"; office net absorption turned positive in the second half of 2025; certain banks continue to work through legacy problem loans, and banks over $250 billion in assets took additional charge-offs on office loans.
- Federal Reserve July 2026 SLOOS (released August 3, 2026): moderate and modest net shares of banks eased standards for nonfarm nonresidential and multifamily CRE loans respectively in Q2 2026; construction and land development standards were basically unchanged; a moderate net share reported weaker CLD demand.

## Benchmark and Formula Decisions

Suitable as repo defaults because they are stated in retrieved primary sources:

- The five-category supervisory scale and the verbatim definitions of special mention, substandard, doubtful, and loss (OCC handbook; 2023 policy statement Appendix 4).
- Classified = substandard + doubtful + loss; criticized adds special mention (OCC handbook).
- Nonaccrual triggers: cash-basis maintenance due to borrower deterioration; full payment not expected; 90+ days in default unless both well secured and in the process of collection (OCC handbook citing call report instructions; OCC BAAS).
- The 30-day "in the process of collection" period is a benchmark, not an outer limit (OCC BAAS).
- Six months of sustained repayment performance as the general minimum before returning a restructured nonaccrual loan to accrual, and generally before upgrading a formally restructured credit to pass (2023 policy statement; OCC handbook; OCC BAAS). The BAAS is explicit that no minimum is formally specified and that facts and circumstances control, so the knowledge base presents six months as a general expectation, not a bright line.
- Doubtful assets require nonaccrual treatment (OCC handbook).
- Collateral-dependent sale-of-collateral classification split: secured portion no worse than substandard, excess loss, doubtful used sparingly for the mitigated or unquantifiable portion (2023 policy statement).
- Note A / Note B treatment and the prohibition on rebooking charge-offs (2023 policy statement; OCC BAAS).
- The four reportable modification forms for a borrower experiencing financial difficulty (OCC BAAS citing ASC 310-10-50-42).
- The six workout-plan elements examiners will not criticize (2023 policy statement).
- The six CRE-workout appraisal reconsideration factors and the ten 2010-guidelines validity factors.
- The $500,000 CRE appraisal threshold (2018 final rule) and the $1 million real-estate-secured business loan exemption conditions (2010 guidelines).
- Credit risk review: annual-or-on-renewal baseline frequency, board-approved annual scope, quarterly board reporting, and the independence and dispute-resolution rules (2020 interagency guidance).

Case-by-case only, not repo defaults:

- Any mapping of internal grades (1-9, 1-10, or dual obligor / facility scales) to the regulatory categories. No source prescribes a grade count or a mapping; the OCC handbook says no single system is ideal for every bank and that the agencies do not advocate a particular system. The knowledge base presents a mapping illustration as a common convention and says the institution's own policy controls.
- Any numeric PD or LGD value tied to a regulatory grade. The handbook maps the concepts, not the numbers.
- Watchlist entry criteria (DSCR floors, occupancy triggers, days past due, covenant breach counts). These are institution policy; no interagency source sets them.
- The definition of "short-term" for an accommodation. The agencies expressly declined to define it.
- Appraisal re-order calendars (for example "every 12 months for criticized CRE"). The sources give trigger factors and a policy expectation, not an interval.
- Allowance quantification for any specific loan.

## Conflicting Source Resolution

- Appraisal threshold. The 2010 Interagency Appraisal and Evaluation Guidelines state a $250,000 appraisal threshold throughout. The 2018 final rule raised the CRE threshold to $500,000. Resolution: the later rulemaking controls for CRE transactions; the knowledge base uses $500,000 for CRE and notes the guidelines' text predates the change.
- Six-month sustained performance. The 2023 policy statement says "generally would be a minimum of six months"; the OCC handbook says "generally six months"; the OCC BAAS says no minimum is specified in ASC 942-310-35 or regulatory policy but that the period "generally would be at least six months for a monthly amortizing loan." Resolution: the accounting-authority source (BAAS) is the most precise about the absence of a bright line, so the knowledge base states six months as the general supervisory expectation while flagging that facts and circumstances govern.
- Credit direction in 2026. FDIC QBP Q2 2026 shows CRE PDNA improving and the July 2026 SLOOS shows banks easing CRE standards, while the OCC Spring 2026 Risk Perspective still flags refinance risk and legacy office problem loans. Resolution: these are not contradictory. Aggregate metrics are improving from elevated levels while the maturity and refinance overhang remains a forward-looking concern. The knowledge base presents both with dates and treats aggregate improvement as no substitute for loan-level analysis.
- Classification vs allowance. Practitioners often equate a loss classification with the allowance amount. The 2023 policy statement states directly that loss classifications may not be equivalent to allowance measurements. Regulator text controls.

## Edge Cases and Red Flags

- Non-bank lenders. Debt funds, life companies, CMBS originators and servicers, and mortgage REITs are not subject to the interagency classification, nonaccrual, or credit risk review regimes. Applying "substandard" to a non-bank facility is a loose analogy, not a regulatory grade. The knowledge base says so explicitly and points CMBS-specific questions to the existing servicing knowledge base.
- Credit unions. NCUA does not require credit unions to adopt the special mention designation or the uniform loss / doubtful / substandard schematic; credit unions apply a relative credit risk score per 12 CFR part 723, and "adversely classified" means more severely graded under the credit union's own system (2023 policy statement footnotes). Bank-style classification language should not be assumed for a credit union.
- Interest reserves. A construction or transitional CRE loan kept contractually current by an interest reserve can mask a stalled project. The 2023 policy statement flags adverse classification as potentially appropriate in exactly that fact pattern.
- Extension churn. The policy statement warns that liberal use of extensions and renewals can obscure a borrower's inability to meet reasonable repayment terms; payment status alone is not a rating.
- Value-only downgrades. Downgrading a performing loan solely because an appraisal came back below the balance is contrary to the policy statement. So is refusing to downgrade a loan with well-defined weaknesses because it is current.
- Doubtful overuse. The policy statement instructs infrequent use of doubtful and requires timely reassessment once pending events resolve; a doubtful rating left standing for multiple quarters is itself a finding.
- Guarantor reliance. A guarantee reduces or precludes adverse classification only when the guarantor is financially responsible with demonstrated willingness and incentive; the statement requires analysis of guarantor cash flow and global debt service coverage including contingent obligations.
- Environmental and bankruptcy exposures. The policy statement names bankruptcy and potential environmental concerns as situations where loss exposure cannot be reasonably determined, justifying a classification more severe than substandard on a restructured loan's remaining balance.
- Institution-specific policy. Internal grade definitions, watchlist criteria, delegated approval authority, and appraisal ordering calendars are set by board-approved policy and vary by institution. Nothing here overrides an institution's own credit policy, its primary regulator's direction, or examiner findings.
- Not advice. Classification, nonaccrual, charge-off, allowance, and regulatory reporting conclusions require the institution's own credit, accounting, and legal judgment. This material is educational decision support.
