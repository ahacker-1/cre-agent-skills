# Annual Loan Review and Risk Rating Research

## Purpose

- Supports `skills/lender-credit/annual-loan-review-and-risk-rating.md`
- Pairs with `knowledge/regulatory-risk-rating-and-classification.md`, `knowledge/lender-credit-policy-benchmarks.md`, and `knowledge/underwriting-calc.md`
- Intended users: bank and credit union credit analysts, portfolio managers, credit officers, credit risk review (loan review) staff, and special assets officers who have to perform the periodic review of an existing commercial real estate loan, refresh the performance and collateral metrics, confirm or change the regulatory classification and the internal grade, document the migration against the prior review, and set the actions and the next review date

## U.S.-Only Assumptions

- Geography: United States only. The supervisory classification scale (pass, special mention, substandard, doubtful, loss), the credit risk review expectations, nonaccrual conventions from the call report instructions, and the interagency appraisal and evaluation framework are U.S. federal constructs with no direct offshore analogue.
- Institution type: the classification vocabulary and credit risk review expectations in this note apply to banks, thrifts, and (with the NCUA's own variation) credit unions. Debt funds, life companies, mortgage REITs, and CMBS originators are not subject to the interagency classification framework and run internal or rating-agency grades instead; the analytical steps still transfer, the regulatory grade does not.
- Deal type: income-producing commercial real estate (multifamily, office, retail, industrial, hospitality, mixed use) plus transitional assets. Construction and land development appear only where the annual review of an ADC facility raises interest reserve, stalled project, or extension questions.
- Legal and market assumptions: educational decision support, not legal, tax, investment, accounting, or financing advice. Classification, nonaccrual, charge-off, allowance, and regulatory reporting conclusions belong to the institution and its regulator. Nothing here overrides a board-approved credit policy, a rating definition set, or an examiner's judgment.
- Formula definitions (NOI, DSCR, debt yield, LTV, break-even occupancy, debt constant) are not restated in this note or in the skill; they live in `knowledge/underwriting-calc.md`.
- Time-sensitive market figures are directional as of the access date and carry a date and a source in the text.

## Key Findings

### Annual is the floor, not the schedule

- The OCC's "Rating Credit Risk" booklet states the baseline directly: the officer primarily responsible for rating should review and update risk ratings whenever relevant new information is received, and all credits should receive a formal review at least annually to ensure ratings are accurate and up to date. Large credits, new credits, higher-risk pass credits, problem credits, and complex credits should be reviewed more frequently, while smaller performing credits may be excluded from periodic reviews and handled as exceptions.
- The 2020 Interagency Guidance on Credit Risk Review Systems frames the same expectation from the review side: an effective system provides for review and evaluation of significant loans, loan products, or groups of loans typically annually, on renewal, or more frequently when internal or external factors indicate a potential for deteriorating credit quality.
- FDIC Section 3.2 repeats it for the loan review function: reviews of significant credits are generally performed annually, upon renewal, or more frequently when factors indicate a potential for deteriorating credit quality, and the loan review policy itself is typically reviewed and approved at least annually by the board.
- The practical consequence is that an annual review is a calendar minimum layered on top of an event-driven obligation. A credit whose risk changed in month three should have been re-rated in month three; the annual review then confirms rather than discovers.

### The annual review is only as good as the reporting package it is built on

- The CRE lending handbook ties the package to the loan documents: loan covenants should require submission of periodic financial information on the project, the borrowing entities, and any guarantors, and the required frequency should consider the stability of the property. For a property with few tenants and long-term leases extending beyond the loan term, or for stabilized multifamily, annual operating statements and rent rolls may be adequate; properties in lease-up or nonresidential properties with many tenants or frequent lease expirations could warrant monthly, quarterly, or semiannual information.
- The information collected must be analyzed in a timely manner to assess financial performance, tenant rollover risk, and compliance with financial or performance covenants, and receipt and analysis should be tracked so management can evaluate the effectiveness of the monitoring program. Where borrowers or guarantors do not respond, the handbook's standard is that collection efforts remain continuous and diligent, not that the file goes quiet.
- Two non-financial items belong in the same package. Sound credit administration includes monitoring the timely payment of real estate taxes, and the handbook says delinquent real estate taxes threaten the bank's interest in the collateral and are nearly always an indicator of a distressed property, borrower, or guarantor. Periodic property inspections should verify that the property is adequately maintained and that tenants and vacancies have been accurately reported in the rent roll, with particular attention to troubled properties and troubled borrowers or guarantors.
- FDIC Section 3.2 sets the same reporting floor generally: financial information should be updated not less than annually, with officer file comments updated as needed and documenting significant meetings and events, and with reasonable flexibility depending on credit size, loan type, borrower strength, and external conditions.
- The 12 CFR part 30 appendix A documentation standards drive the file review. The handbook's list of documents typically maintained in the file includes the approval memorandum, signed borrower and guarantor financial statements, property operating statements and rent rolls, title insurance policy, recorded mortgage or deed of trust with note and assignments, copies of leases and executed tenant estoppels, insurance policies with proof of premium payment, the appraisal or evaluation together with the institution's own appraisal review and the engagement letter and appraiser qualifications, survey, organizational documents and borrowing resolutions, and evidence that property taxes are paid to date.

### The rating measures risk over the remaining term, not payment history

- The OCC handbook's worked example is the cleanest statement of the rule: a borrower in year three of a seven-year amortizing term loan, current on payments and historically healthy, whose business prospects are weakening such that projected cash flow will not cover required debt service, should be downgraded now, when default risk increases, rather than later when coverage goes negative or default occurs. Credit risk ratings are meant to measure risk rather than record history.
- Examiners assess performance expectations over at least the upcoming twelve months, and expected performance should be evaluated over the foreseeable future, not less than one year.
- The 2023 policy statement adds the CRE-specific caution: being contractually current can be misleading where the underwriting structure or the liberal use of extensions and renewals masks credit weaknesses and obscures a borrower's inability to meet reasonable repayment terms. Its worked example is the ADC interest reserve that keeps a stalled project current while principal repayment is in jeopardy, where adverse classification may be appropriate.
- Rating upgrades run the other way. When credits are classified for well-defined weaknesses, examiners normally await correction of the weaknesses and a period of sustained performance under reasonable repayment terms before upgrading; the mere existence of a plan for improvement does not warrant an upgrade. Formally restructured loans generally require six months of sustained performance under the restructured terms before an upgrade to pass.
- Structure alone can drive a criticism. The handbook says loans can be so poorly structured that they require classification even though the likelihood of default is low, naming deferred interest payments and no meaningful amortization, and Appendix F catalogs the structural weakness elements: indefinite or speculative purpose; indefinite or overly liberal repayment programs including evergreen loans, bullet maturities unrelated to the actual repayment source, rewrites or renewals that simply defer maturity, and advances to fund interest payments; nonexistent, weak, or waived covenants; inadequate debt service coverage; elevated leverage; inadequate tangible net worth; inadequate financial analysis; insufficient collateral support; inadequate collateral documentation and valuation; overly aggressive LTV or advance rates; and inadequate guarantor support, including a guarantor whose support was critical to the original decision being released without offsetting support.

### What does not justify a downgrade, and what does not justify holding a pass

- The 2023 policy statement is explicit on three points. Examiners should not adversely classify or require a partial charge-off on a performing commercial loan solely because collateral value has declined below the loan balance. Examiners should not adversely classify loans solely because the borrower is associated with an industry experiencing difficulty. Renewals or restructurings of maturing loans to commercial borrowers who have the ability to repay on reasonable terms will not automatically be subject to adverse classification, though such loans should be identified in the institution's internal credit grading system and may warrant close monitoring.
- The converse holds. It is appropriate to classify a performing loan when well-defined weaknesses exist that jeopardize repayment, and adverse classification of a renewed or restructured loan is appropriate where, despite the renewal, well-defined weaknesses jeopardize orderly repayment under reasonable modified terms.
- Where a modification is not supported by adequate analysis and documentation, examiners are expected to exercise reasonable judgment in determining classifications until the institution can support management's conclusions and internal grades. An undocumented annual review is therefore not a neutral act; it invites someone else's rating.

### Collateral, valuation age, and the re-order decision

- Collateral value is generally a tertiary repayment source and becomes important to the rating when primary and secondary sources become inadequate or questionable. When that happens, the CRE handbook directs attention to current and projected vacancy and absorption, lease renewal trends and anticipated rents, effective rents or sale prices net of concessions, time frame to stabilized occupancy or sellout, volume and trend of past-due leases, NOI against budget with reasonable operating and maintenance costs, and discount and capitalization rates.
- Examiners are generally not expected to challenge underlying valuation assumptions, including discount and capitalization rates, when those assumptions differ only in a limited way from norms associated with the collateral under review; the estimated value may be adjusted for credit analysis purposes when the examiner can establish that underlying facts or assumptions are inappropriate or can support alternative assumptions, and those adjustments should be discussed with the institution when determining the risk rating.
- The 2010 Interagency Appraisal and Evaluation Guidelines list the factors that can make an existing appraisal or evaluation no longer valid: passage of time; volatility of the local market; changes in terms and availability of financing; natural disasters; limited or over supply of competing properties; improvements to the subject or competing properties; lack of maintenance of the subject or competing properties; changes in underlying economic and market assumptions such as capitalization rates and lease terms; changes in zoning, building materials, or technology; and environmental contamination. Institutions should maintain policies and procedures establishing standards for obtaining current collateral valuation information to support a modification or workout decision, and should monitor the effect of collateral valuation trends on portfolio risk.
- The 2023 policy statement gives the workout-specific trigger list: in deciding whether to obtain a new appraisal or evaluation, a prudent institution considers whether there has been material deterioration in the performance of the project, conditions for the geographic market and property type, variances between actual conditions and original appraisal assumptions, changes in project specifications, loss of a significant lease or a take-out commitment, or increases in pre-sale fallout. A new appraisal may not be necessary where an evaluation appropriately updates the original appraisal assumptions to current market conditions and provides a reasonable estimate of fair value; if new money is being advanced, the appraisal regulations govern.
- Neither source sets a calendar. There is no supervisory rule that an appraisal expires at twelve, eighteen, or twenty-four months, so the re-order interval is a board-approved policy question and the review's job is to state the appraisal's effective date, the value type, and which trigger, if any, has fired.

### Nonaccrual and the accounting consequences of the grade

- The general rule the OCC handbook takes from the call report instructions: do not accrue interest, amortize deferred net loan fees or costs, or accrete discount where the asset is maintained on a cash basis because of deterioration in the borrower's financial condition, where payment in full of principal or interest is not expected, or where principal or interest has been in default 90 days or more unless the asset is both well secured and in the process of collection. "Well secured" means secured by collateral with realizable value sufficient to discharge the debt including accrued interest in full, or by the guarantee of a financially responsible party. "In the process of collection" means collection is proceeding in due course through legal action or, in appropriate circumstances, through non-legal collection efforts reasonably expected to result in repayment or restoration to current status in the near future.
- Nonaccrual loans will almost always be classified, and nonaccrual treatment is required for doubtful assets. Interest capitalization is inappropriate for most classified loans and should not be permitted where a loan is classified loss, doubtful, value-impaired, or nonaccrual.
- Return to accrual generally requires that none of the principal and interest is due and unpaid and the institution expects repayment of the remaining contractual principal and interest, or that the asset otherwise becomes well secured and in the process of collection. The CRE handbook adds that management should consider sustained historical repayment performance for a reasonable period, generally a minimum of six months of cash or cash-equivalent payments, and that performance before a restructuring may be taken into account.
- Charged-off amounts cannot be rebooked; once the cost basis is decreased it cannot later be increased.
- For a problem CRE loan dependent on the sale of collateral, the 2023 statement's split is: the portion adequately secured by fair value less costs to sell generally classified no worse than substandard, the excess classified loss, and doubtful used where pending events may mitigate full loss or where the loss amount cannot be reasonably determined, applied infrequently and reassessed promptly. Where a partial charge-off has been taken as part of a restructuring, the remaining recorded balance generally should be classified no more severely than substandard.

### Migration, documentation, and how the review gets reported

- The OCC handbook's list of rating MIS is the migration reporting spine: volume of credits whose ratings changed more than one grade (double downgrades), seasoning of ratings (how long credits stay in a grade), velocity of rating changes, default and loss history by rating category, the ratio of upgrades to downgrades, and rating changes by line of business, loan officer, and location, displayed by both dollar volume and item count because a single large account can skew a report.
- Rating disagreements are resolved by a pre-arranged process that may include formal appeals and arbitration by an independent party, or may default to the rating indicating lower credit quality; where credit risk review concludes quality is lower than portfolio management perceives, the lower rating typically prevails absent additional information.
- The credit risk review function prepares a list of loans and portfolio segments reviewed, the date of review, and a summary analysis substantiating the ratings assigned, and results go to the board or an appropriate board committee quarterly, more often when material adverse trends appear.
- Examiner tolerance for rating error is narrow. The OCC handbook says examiners investigate root causes when rating inaccuracies are found in generally more than 5 percent of the number of credits reviewed or 3 percent of the dollar amount, and that inaccuracies understating risk are of greatest concern.

### Market context for the review cycle (directional, as of 2026-09-01)

- The July 2026 Senior Loan Officer Opinion Survey, released 2026-08-03, reported that in Q2 2026 a moderate net share of banks eased standards on nonfarm nonresidential loans and a modest net share eased on multifamily, while construction and land development standards remained basically unchanged; demand was mixed, with large banks reporting stronger and other banks weaker demand, and a moderate net share of foreign banks reported tighter CRE standards.
- The OCC's Spring 2026 Semiannual Risk Perspective release (2026-05-07) said credit conditions and refinancing risk in certain segments of commercial real estate lending warrant ongoing monitoring, while describing capital and liquidity as high by historical standards.
- The FDIC's Quarterly Banking Profile for the second quarter of 2026, released 2026-08-25, reported an industry return on assets of 1.37 percent and aggregate net income of $90.1 billion.
- CBRE reported (2026-08-03) Q2 2026 averages of 59.6 percent LTV on commercial loans, 63.3 percent on multifamily, 1.43 DSCR, 10.2 percent debt yield, and a 5.7 percent coupon, with banks at 30 percent of non-agency closings. These are origination-market averages and are useful as a reasonableness check on a refinance exit, not as rating thresholds.

## Benchmark and Formula Decisions

Suitable as repo defaults because they are published supervisory expectations:

- Formal review of every credit at least annually, with off-cycle re-rating whenever relevant new information arrives, and more frequent review for large, new, complex, higher-risk pass, and problem credits. Adopted from the OCC "Rating Credit Risk" booklet and confirmed by the 2020 interagency credit risk review guidance and FDIC Section 3.2.
- The reporting-frequency frame by property stability: annual operating statements and rent rolls may suffice for stabilized multifamily or few-tenant long-lease properties; lease-up and multi-tenant nonresidential properties warrant monthly, quarterly, or semiannual reporting. Adopted from the OCC CRE lending handbook.
- The rating horizon: assess expected performance over at least the next twelve months, and rate the risk over the remaining term rather than the payment record.
- The upgrade rule: correction of the weakness plus a period of sustained performance under reasonable repayment terms, generally six months for a formally restructured loan, before returning to pass. Same six-month frame for return to accrual, with pre-restructuring performance countable.
- The collateral-dependent classification split (substandard to the extent of fair value less costs to sell, loss above it, doubtful only for pending events or unquantifiable loss, used infrequently), and the partial charge-off rule that the remaining recorded balance is generally no more severe than substandard.
- The appraisal validity factor list and the workout re-order trigger list, used as a checklist rather than a calendar, with the value type and effective date always stated.
- The migration reporting set: double downgrades, seasoning, velocity, upgrade-to-downgrade ratio, and rating changes by officer, line of business, and location, reported by count and by dollar.
- The documentation exception inventory drawn from the 12 CFR part 30 appendix A file documentation standards as itemized in the CRE lending handbook.

Case-by-case only, not defaults:

- Numeric internal grade definitions and the DSCR, debt yield, LTV, and occupancy trip points that map to each grade. No regulator publishes these; they are board-approved policy. The skill asks the user for them and, where absent, reports the metrics and the supportable regulatory classification without inventing an internal grade number.
- Appraisal re-order intervals in months. No interagency source sets one; the skill reports appraisal age and whether a trigger fired.
- Watchlist entry criteria and watchlist exit criteria. Watchlist is an institution-defined designation with no interagency definition.
- Review depth tiering by loan size, and which smaller performing credits may be reviewed as exceptions rather than individually.
- Any market metric used as a threshold. CBRE's Q2 2026 origination averages are calibration for an exit test, not rating criteria.

## Conflicting Source Resolution

- Event-driven re-rating versus the annual calendar. The OCC handbook requires both: update whenever relevant new information is received, and formally review at least annually. There is no conflict once the annual review is understood as a floor and a documentation event rather than the only occasion to change a grade. The skill therefore asks whether the rating should already have changed since the prior review, and treats a first-time downgrade at the annual review of a credit that deteriorated months earlier as a process finding as well as a credit finding.
- Payment status versus expected performance. FDIC Section 3.2's trouble indicators and the retail classification convention lean on payment performance, while the OCC handbook and the 2023 policy statement say the rating measures risk over the remaining term. The commercial CRE rule is the latter; payment status is one of several considerations and can mislead where interest reserves, extensions, or renewals fund it. The skill runs both and flags disagreement between them rather than letting current status settle the grade.
- Collateral decline as a downgrade trigger. Practitioners often downgrade on a low appraisal. The 2023 statement forbids classifying a performing loan solely on that basis, while the same statement and the CRE handbook make collateral central once primary repayment weakens. The skill resolves it by requiring a named well-defined weakness in repayment capacity or structure before an adverse classification, and by using the value to size the split once one exists.
- Guarantor credit in the rating. The Rating Credit Risk booklet says credit mitigants primarily affect loss rather than default and their impact on a rating should be negligible until the loan is classified, and warns against ratings that overstate mitigation; the CRE handbook and the 2023 statement say a financially responsible guarantee may preclude or reduce the severity of classification. Both are consistent: the guarantee earns rating credit at the point the primary source weakens, and only where financial ability, demonstrated willingness, and incentive are documented. The skill requires those three findings, plus the total number and amount of guarantees the guarantor has extended to all lenders, before any classification relief.
- FIL numbering for the 2023 policy statement. The FDIC's own letter page identifies it as FIL-34-2023 dated June 29, 2023; the same document circulates as OCC Bulletin 2023-23 and SR 23-5. This note cites the FDIC letter number as published on the retrieved page.

## Edge Cases and Red Flags

- Non-bank lenders. A debt fund, life company, mortgage REIT, or CMBS originator does not assign pass, special mention, substandard, doubtful, or loss under the interagency framework. Running the analysis is still right; labeling the output with bank classification vocabulary is not. The skill states the analytical grade and says the regulatory scale does not apply.
- Credit unions. The NCUA does not require the uniform regulatory classification schematic; credit unions apply a relative credit risk score to each commercial loan under 12 CFR part 723, and "adversely classified" means more severely graded under the credit union's own system. The regulatory grade line in the output should be labeled accordingly.
- The rating that never moved. A credit sitting in the same grade across several reviews with deteriorating metrics is a seasoning finding, and seasoning of ratings is one of the MIS metrics the OCC expects to be produced.
- The credit that is current because the lender is funding it. Interest reserves, serial extensions, re-amortization of a maturing balance over the original period, and interest-only renewals on stabilized property are all treated in supervisory guidance as possible indicators of a troubled credit rather than neutral choices.
- The guarantor released or diluted since the last review. Appendix F names release of a guarantor whose support was critical to the original credit decision, without offsetting support, as a structural weakness in its own right.
- Missing or stale reporting as the finding. Where the borrower has not delivered statements, rent rolls, or covenant certificates, the review documents a documentation exception and rates on what exists; it does not assume performance held. Special mention exists for exactly this kind of potential weakness, including a missing current financial package or an unordered valuation.
- A property type in a distressed segment. Industry association alone does not support a downgrade, but segment conditions do inform the collateral value assumptions and the refinance exit, both of which can carry a well-defined weakness on their own facts.
- Split-collateral and cross-collateralized relationships. Where one exposure is partly secured by cash, liquid collateral, or an unconditional payment guarantee, a pass or adverse split rating may be the accurate answer rather than a single grade for the whole relationship.
- Loans that are really a modification. If the annual review concludes the borrower needs an accommodation, term extension, rate concession, or payment deferral, the analysis moves to the workout frame and to [Problem Loan and Modification Analyst](skills/lender-credit/problem-loan-and-modification-analyst.md); classification and accrual conclusions there follow the 2023 policy statement rather than the routine review.
- Independence. Where the reviewer controls the loan or is influenced by the approval process, the output is a portfolio manager's self-assessment, not a credit risk review conclusion, and should say so.

## Open Questions

- Internal grade scales vary in length and in the metric trip points mapped to each grade, and no primary source publishes a model scale. The skill produces the regulatory classification and asks the user to map it to the institution's own numeric grade.
- Appraisal re-order intervals, watchlist entry and exit criteria, and review-depth tiering by loan size are all board-approved policy variables that this note deliberately declines to default.
- The FDIC Quarterly Banking Profile's detailed asset quality tables (past-due and nonaccrual rates by loan category, reserve coverage) sit in the full quarterly report rather than the landing page retrieved on the access date; the aggregate figures cited here are the ones actually read. See `knowledge/regulatory-risk-rating-and-classification.md` for the category-level figures carried in that file.
- NCUA-specific commercial loan review expectations under 12 CFR part 723 and the NCUA Examiner's Guide are referenced by the interagency documents but the Examiner's Guide text itself was not retrievable on the access date; credit union users should confirm against that guide.
- Rating agency and internal grade conventions for debt funds, life companies, and CMBS special servicers are documented in offering materials, servicing agreements, and presale reports rather than in public guidance, and are not covered here.
- Whether an institution's CECL methodology treats a downgraded CRE credit collectively or individually is an accounting judgment tied to whether risk characteristics have diverged; the skill flags the allowance consequence of a grade change but does not compute it.
