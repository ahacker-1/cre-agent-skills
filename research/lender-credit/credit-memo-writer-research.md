# Credit Memo Writer Research

## Purpose

- Supports `skills/lender-credit/credit-memo-writer.md`
- Pairs with `knowledge/credit-memo-and-appraisal-review-standards.md`, `knowledge/lender-credit-policy-benchmarks.md`, and `knowledge/regulatory-risk-rating-and-classification.md`
- Intended users: bank and credit union credit analysts, underwriters, credit officers, credit committee members, and loan review staff who draft or challenge a commercial real estate credit approval memorandum before it goes to an approval authority

## U.S.-Only Assumptions

- Geography: United States
- Institution type: federally insured depositories supervised by the OCC, Federal Reserve, FDIC, or NCUA. Debt funds, life companies, and CMBS originators are not bound by the interagency framework but commonly write memos on the same spine; where a rule is bank-specific this research says so
- Deal type: income-producing commercial real estate plus acquisition, development, and construction (ADC) credits, both new money and renewals
- Legal assumptions: this is educational decision support, not legal, tax, investment, accounting, appraisal, or financing advice. The institution's board-approved loan policy, its primary regulator, and its counsel control every conclusion. Classification, accrual, allowance, and regulatory reporting decisions belong to the institution

## Key Findings

### The memo is a regulatory artifact, not an internal formality

- The OCC handbook treats the approval memorandum as the document that lets approvers make a fully informed credit decision, and expects executed loan documents to be consistent with it. Loan documentation practices must let the institution identify the purpose of the loan and the source of repayment and assess the borrower's ability to repay.
- The handbook is explicit that certain judgment calls belong in the memo by name. On re-amortizing a maturing balance back over the original period it says the effect is to divert cash flow from the bank to the borrower, and "the rationale for accepting diversion of that cash flow should be clearly addressed in the bank's credit approval document."
- Examiners read the memo against a fixed checklist. The handbook's file-read procedures direct examiners to determine the primary source of repayment and evaluate its adequacy, evaluate underwriting quality on anything originated, renewed, or restructured in the past twelve months, evaluate external factors including supply, demand, rents, vacancy, interest rates, cap rates, and NOI, analyze secondary sources from guarantors, sponsors, or endorsers, evaluate collateral sufficiency and appraisal compliance, confirm covenant compliance, document every significant policy and underwriting exception and whether it was properly approved, and assign a risk rating. A memo organized to answer those items in that order survives review; one organized around the deal narrative does not.
- For credit unions, 12 CFR 723.4 makes the same content mandatory by rule: the policy must set the level and depth of financial analysis needed to evaluate the borrower's trends, condition, and debt service capacity, require due diligence on principals, require borrower-prepared projections where historical performance does not support projected debt payments, address financial statement quality and verification, specify collateral evaluation methods and LTV limits by collateral type, assign a credit risk rating at inception, and provide a process to identify, report, and monitor policy exceptions.

### The repayment waterfall has three named tiers, and they are not interchangeable

- Primary: for income-producing property, the cash flow of the property; for owner-occupied property, the cash flow of the occupying business. The handbook tells examiners to assess adequacy of cash flow to meet debt service, comment on trends in NOI, vacancy, and expenses, review current rent rolls and leases, assess tenant quality and mix, note significant lease expirations, and analyze the effect of tenant turnover on future coverage.
- Secondary: guarantors, financial sponsors, or endorsers. The handbook says a guarantor "can provide a secondary source of repayment that can favorably affect the credit's risk rating when the primary source of repayment becomes inadequate," and that when the primary source is satisfactory the guarantor's strength is supplemental in importance to the rating. Attributes of a financially responsible guarantor: financial capacity and ability to support through ongoing payments, curtailments, or re-margining; a guarantee adequate to support repayment in whole or in part during the remaining loan term; and a guarantee that is written and legally enforceable. The assessment must include the total number and amount of guarantees extended to all lenders, plus verified liquidity, global financial condition, income, cash flow, and contingent liabilities.
- Tertiary: collateral value. The handbook states collateral value "is generally a tertiary source of repayment" and becomes important in rating only when primary and secondary sources are inadequate or questionable. The reasonableness tests it lists for that value are current and projected vacancy and absorption, lease renewal trends and anticipated rents, effective rents or sale prices net of concessions and financing concessions, the time frame to stabilized occupancy or sellout, past-due lease volume and trend, and NOI compared with budget.
- The "Rating Credit Risk" booklet supplies the mechanism connecting the three: probability of default measures repayment capacity, and "when repayment capacity exhibits well-defined weaknesses, analysis shifts to the strength of secondary sources and the potential, or expected, loss." Loss given default is driven by underwriting quality and structure, including collateral and margin requirements, covenants, guarantees, and insurance.
- FDIC Section 3.2 adds the discipline for sponsor credit: an institution may consider sponsor support in the internal rating only when it can document the sponsor's history of demonstrated support plus economic incentive, capacity, and stated intent to continue, and even documented capacity and history may not mitigate supervisory concerns absent a documented commitment of continued support.

### Stress testing is a required section, and the variables are named

- The OCC handbook's list of what an effective CRE lending policy addresses includes "expectations for evaluating project feasibility and sensitivity to changes in economic conditions, including the sensitivity of projections to changes in market variables, such as interest rates, vacancy rates, and operating expenses."
- For projects involving unit sales, the analysis "should include stress testing to analyze sensitivity to changing economic conditions under a variety of scenarios (e.g., absorption rates, interest rates, and capitalization rates)."
- In the REIT lending discussion the same frame is stated as stress testing sensitivity "under a variety of scenarios, such as interest rates, capitalization rates, and DSCRs."
- FDIC Section 3.2, in the leveraged lending credit-analysis expectations, requires that projections "are stress tested for one or two downside scenarios, including a covenant breach," and that cash flow analyses not rely on overly optimistic or unsubstantiated projections. The covenant-breach scenario is the transferable idea: a CRE memo should show the point at which the DSCR or debt yield covenant fails, not only the base case.

### Structure, covenants, and the exit

- The handbook names the common financial covenants for income-producing CRE as debt yield, DSCR, LTV, LTC, and borrower or guarantor minimum net worth or liquidity, and notes others may apply depending on complexity or loan type.
- It warns that where loan documents contain debt service coverage covenants, income and expenses should be clearly defined, and that the covenant DSCR calculation may differ from the DSCR used for underwriting and risk rating. A memo that quotes one DSCR and hands the documents a different definition creates a monitoring gap on day one.
- Interest-only is treated as a risk to be priced in size. The handbook's worked example: a policy-conforming $1,000,000 loan at 6 percent, five-year term, 25-year amortization would amortize to $899,000 at maturity; in an interest-only scenario the mitigant "would be to lower the loan amount at origination to $899,000." It also states that a renewal, refinancing, or extension on an interest-only basis can indicate a troubled loan, and that IO periods should generally be limited to construction or stabilization periods when property cash flow is temporarily insufficient to support principal payments.
- Policy elements the memo has to answer against include minimum initial hard equity in cash or unencumbered investment, acceptable types, sources, and timing of equity, limits on interest reserves, limits on IO for stabilized CRE, preleasing requirements for income-producing property, presale and minimum release requirements for tract development, limits on partial and nonrecourse loans, takeout commitment requirements, and affirmative and negative covenant requirements.

### Leverage limits, HVCRE, and the exception basket

- Supervisory LTV limits reproduced in the handbook: raw land 65 percent; land development or improved lots 75 percent; construction of commercial, multifamily, and other nonresidential 80 percent; one- to four-family residential construction 85 percent; improved commercial, multifamily, and other nonresidential 85 percent; owner-occupied one- to four-family and home equity carries no set limit but expects credit enhancement at or above 90 percent. For a loan funding multiple phases, the applicable limit is the one for the final phase funded, though the handbook says it remains prudent to consider each phase separately.
- Exceptions: loans above the limits should be identified in the institution's records and their aggregate amount reported at least quarterly to the board. The aggregate basket should not exceed 100 percent of total capital, and within it commercial, agricultural, multifamily, and other non-one-to-four-family loans should not exceed 30 percent of total capital. The entire outstanding balance counts, not just the excess. A loan that met the limit at origination and later exceeded it because collateral value fell is not an exception and stays out of the basket. A loan leaves the basket when principal reduction, senior lien reduction, or additional collateral or equity brings LTV back within limits.
- HVCRE turns on a separate test: at least 15 percent of the real property's appraised as-completed value contributed by the borrower in cash, unencumbered readily marketable assets, paid development expenses out of pocket, or contributed real property or improvements, contributed before any advance of loan funds and contractually required to remain in the project until the exposure is reclassified. Reclassification requires substantial completion plus cash flow sufficient to support debt service and expenses under the institution's permanent-financing underwriting criteria. HVCRE exposures carry a 150 percent risk weight under the standardized approach, and ADC loans made before January 1, 2015 are exempt.

### Collateral and valuation inside the memo

- The 2010 interagency guidelines state that the collateral valuation program "is an integral component of the credit underwriting process and, therefore, should be isolated from influence by the institution's loan production staff," and that "prior to a final credit decision, an institution should review appraisals and evaluations to ensure that they comply with the Agencies' appraisal regulations and are consistent with supervisory guidance." A memo that recommends approval before the review is complete is out of sequence.
- Communicating a predetermined, expected, or qualifying value, or a loan amount or target LTV, to an appraiser or evaluator is prohibited. The memo should therefore never read as though the value was reverse-engineered from the requested proceeds.
- For construction, the appraiser should provide as-is market value and, as applicable, prospective market value upon completion and upon stabilization. The memo has to say which premise drives the LTV test it reports.
- Validity of an existing appraisal turns on facts, not a calendar: passage of time, local market volatility, natural disasters, and similar changes. In a market where CBRE's H1 2026 survey (published 2026-08-12) reports the all-property average cap rate essentially flat with the 10-year Treasury peaking at 4.67 percent in mid-May and near 4.6 percent in mid-July, the relevant test is whether the appraisal's rate and absorption assumptions still hold, not how many months old the report is. Directional and time-sensitive; re-verify before relying on it.

### Rating, exceptions, and what happens after approval

- The OCC does not advocate a particular rating system but expects every system to address both the ability and willingness of the obligor to repay and the support provided by structure and collateral, whether through a single rating or dual obligor and facility ratings. Obligor ratings support deal structuring and administration; facility ratings support allowance and capital estimates.
- Policy exception review is a named examination step: examiners assess the frequency and nature of exceptions and whether they were adequately documented, approved, reported, and appropriate in light of relevant credit considerations, and treat an excessive or significantly increasing number of exceptions as a finding in itself.
- SR 20-13 sets the independence expectation for the review that will later re-read the memo, and SR 20-12 (revised 2023-04-21) governs the ASC Topic 326 allowance the credit will feed. The 2023 CRE workout policy statement sets the content bar if the same credit later needs an accommodation: updated and comprehensive borrower, project, guarantor, and sponsor financials; current collateral valuations supporting the loan and the workout plan; global debt service coverage with realistic cash flow projections and the availability, continuity, and accessibility of repayment sources; demonstrated willingness and ability to monitor; an internal rating system that accurately reflects the risk in the workout; and a GAAP allowance methodology.
- Market frame as of this research: the July 2026 SLOOS (released 2026-08-03) reported moderate and modest net shares of banks easing standards on nonfarm nonresidential and multifamily in Q2 2026 with construction and land development basically unchanged and demand mixed by bank size; the OCC's Spring 2026 Semiannual Risk Perspective (released 2026-05-07) flagged CRE credit conditions and refinancing risk in certain segments for ongoing monitoring; the FDIC's Q2 2026 Quarterly Banking Profile (2026-08-25) reported industry ROA of 1.37 percent and aggregate net income of $90.1 billion. All time-sensitive and directional.

## Benchmark and Formula Decisions

Suitable as repo defaults because they are quoted from primary regulator sources:

- The three-tier repayment waterfall with primary = property or occupying-business cash flow, secondary = guarantor and sponsor, tertiary = collateral value, and the rule that collateral only becomes rating-relevant once the first two are inadequate or questionable.
- The examiner file-read sequence as the memo's section order.
- The named stress variables: interest rates, capitalization rates, vacancy, operating expenses, absorption, and DSCR, plus a covenant-breach scenario.
- The covenant menu: debt yield, DSCR, LTV, LTC, and guarantor net worth or liquidity, with the requirement that documented income and expense definitions be reconciled to the underwriting definition.
- Supervisory LTV limits, the 100 percent and 30 percent of total capital exception baskets, full-balance counting, and the rule that value-driven LTV drift is not an exception.
- The HVCRE 15 percent as-completed contributed-capital test, its pre-advance timing, its contractual lock-in, and the reclassification conditions.
- The IO sizing mitigant: size to the balance the equivalent amortizing loan would reach at maturity.
- The requirement that the appraisal or evaluation be reviewed before the final credit decision, and that the memo state which value premise drives the LTV.
- Guarantor sufficiency attributes: capacity and ability to support through payments, curtailments, or re-margining; adequacy in whole or in part over the remaining term; written and legally enforceable; total guarantees to all lenders tallied; liquidity verified.

Case-by-case only, because no primary source publishes a number:

- Minimum DSCR, debt yield, hard equity percentage, sponsor liquidity multiples, and net worth covenant levels. These are institution-set by board-approved policy. See [Lender Credit Policy Benchmarks](knowledge/lender-credit-policy-benchmarks.md) for the frames that do exist.
- Stress magnitudes (how many basis points of rate shock, how many points of vacancy). The regulators name the variables, not the shocks. The skill therefore requires the analyst to state the shock and its basis rather than supplying one.
- Appraisal re-order intervals. Validity is a facts test.

No CRE formulas are restated in the skill. Definitions and mechanics for DSCR, debt yield, LTV, LTC, and NOI come from [Underwriting Calculations](knowledge/underwriting-calc.md).

## Conflicting Source Resolution

- Supervisory ceilings versus market averages. The interagency SLTV limits are ceilings and screening criteria; CBRE and lender filings report realized averages well below them. Where the two could be read as competing targets, the primary regulator source controls the limit and the market data is used only to judge whether a proposed structure is an outlier. The skill states leverage as a binding-test outcome, never as a target drawn from either.
- Guarantee credit. The OCC handbook allows a financially responsible guarantee to preclude or reduce classification severity; FDIC Section 3.2 is stricter about sponsor support, requiring documented history, incentive, capacity, and stated intent, and warning that even those may not suffice without a documented commitment. The skill adopts the stricter FDIC posture for sponsor comfort that is not a written guarantee, and the OCC posture where a legally enforceable written guarantee exists.
- Bank framework versus non-bank lenders. The interagency classification and SLTV vocabulary does not bind debt funds, life companies, or CMBS originators. Where the user is not a depository, the skill keeps the memo structure and drops the supervisory limit and classification language rather than misapplying it.
- Age of source versus currency of rule. "Rating Credit Risk" dates to 2001 with later updates and the appraisal guidelines to 2010; both remain the operative supervisory statements and are cited as current. The 2018 appraisal threshold change and the 2023 workout policy statement supersede older numbers where they overlap, and the knowledge base already carries those.

## Edge Cases and Red Flags

- Owner-occupied property. Primary repayment is the occupying business, not the real estate. A memo that underwrites an owner-occupied building as though it were leased misstates the primary source. The handbook also sets the 50 percent test: where 50 percent or more of the primary source of repayment comes from third-party unaffiliated income, treat the property as non-owner-occupied.
- Renewals and re-amortizations. A five-year loan on a 20-year amortization has a built-in restructuring trigger. Re-amortizing the balance over the original period diverts cash flow to the borrower and the rationale has to be written into the approval document. An IO renewal, refinance, or extension can itself indicate a troubled loan.
- Construction and ADC. The memo must carry the budget, in-balance test, draw controls tied to verified improvements, interest reserve adequacy against construction progress, the takeout source, and preleasing or presale requirements. Sizing off an as-stabilized value with no funded leasing capital is the classic failure.
- Sponsor comfort without a guarantee. Comfort letters and verbal assurances are named in FDIC guidance as lower-grade evidence than a guarantee. Do not carry them into the rating as if they were recourse.
- Non-depository lenders. Debt funds, life companies, CMBS originators, and mortgage REITs use internal or rating-agency grades. Applying "special mention" or "substandard" to their facilities is a category error.
- Concentration at the individual approval. A credit that is fine standalone can still be the wrong marginal exposure. Route that question to [CRE Portfolio Concentration and Stress Tester](skills/lender-credit/cre-portfolio-concentration-and-stress-tester.md) rather than resolving it in the memo.
- Time-sensitive market figures. Cap rates, Treasury levels, spreads, and survey readings move. Every such number in the skill carries a date and a source and is labeled directional.

## Open Questions

- No interagency source publishes minimum CRE DSCR, debt yield, or hard-equity percentages, so the skill cannot supply a defensible numeric floor. Institution policy fills the gap.
- Freddie Mac Optigo and Fannie Mae Form 4660 program grids could not be retrieved during this session (404 and 403 respectively); the agency sizing frames in [Lender Credit Policy Benchmarks](knowledge/lender-credit-policy-benchmarks.md) carry them and are cited there rather than restated here.
- The FDIC Quarterly Banking Profile landing page carries headline earnings figures only; CRE-specific past-due and nonaccrual detail sits in the full QBP tables, which were not opened this session. The classification-side figures in [Regulatory Risk Rating and Classification](knowledge/regulatory-risk-rating-and-classification.md) remain the reference.
- Whether an institution's own credit memo template already encodes these sections varies widely; the skill is written to produce a standalone memo and to be reconcilable to a house template, not to replace one.
