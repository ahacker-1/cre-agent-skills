# CRE Portfolio Concentration and Stress Tester Research

## Purpose

- Supports `skills/lender-credit/cre-portfolio-concentration-and-stress-tester.md`
- Pairs with the pack knowledge bases `knowledge/cre-concentration-and-stress-testing.md` and `knowledge/regulatory-risk-rating-and-classification.md`, and does not restate the cash-flow math in `knowledge/underwriting-calc.md`
- Intended users: CRE portfolio managers, credit officers, credit analysts, loan review, and risk staff at U.S. banks and credit unions who have to segment a CRE book, measure it against the supervisory screening criteria, stress it, and present the result to a board or ALCO

## U.S.-Only Assumptions

- Geography: United States
- Institution type: U.S. federally insured depository institutions and their holding companies. The concentration ratios, the supervisory loan-to-value framework, the HVCRE capital treatment, and the stress testing expectations described here come from the OCC / Federal Reserve / FDIC interagency framework. Credit unions are supervised by NCUA under a separate rule set that is not covered. Debt funds, life companies, mortgage REITs, and CMBS originators are not subject to this framework, though many borrow its vocabulary for internal limits.
- Portfolio scope: income-producing CRE plus construction, land development, and other land loans held on balance sheet, including participations purchased and unfunded commitments where the institution's policy counts them
- Accounting: ASC 326 (CECL) is the operative credit loss standard; institutions that have not adopted it use the prior allowance framework
- This is educational decision support, not legal, tax, accounting, regulatory, investment, or financing advice. Ratio construction, call report classification, allowance conclusions, and capital planning belong to the institution, its board, and its primary federal regulator.

## Key Findings

### The screening criteria are ratios with a specific numerator, denominator, and growth condition

- The 2006 guidance sets two criteria for further supervisory analysis: total reported construction, land development, and other land loans at 100 percent or more of total capital; or total CRE loans as defined in the guidance at 300 percent or more of total capital **and** an outstanding CRE balance that has increased 50 percent or more during the prior 36 months. Only the second criterion carries the growth condition.
- The guidance says plainly that the criteria "do not constitute limits on an institution's lending activity but rather serve as high-level indicators," and that they do not constitute a safe harbor "if other risk indicators are present, regardless of their measurements under (1) and (2)." OCC Bulletin 2006-46 repeats that the guidance sets no numerical limit.
- The 2006 footnotes define total capital as total risk-based capital from call report schedule RC-R. OCC Bulletin 2020-29 replaced that for supervisory concentration measurement effective March 31, 2020 with tier 1 capital plus ALLL or ACL as applicable. The CRE Lending handbook confirms examiners now calculate the ratios that way. The Concentrations of Credit handbook adds a technical correction: for a bank using the 2019 or 2020 CECL capital transition rule, the portion of ACL included in tier 1 capital is subtracted from tier 1 to avoid double counting.
- Numerator scope is definitional, not collateral-code driven. CRE in the guidance means land development and construction loans (including 1-4 family and commercial construction) and other land loans, plus multifamily and nonfarm nonresidential loans where 50 percent or more of repayment comes from third-party, nonaffiliated rental income or from sale, refinancing, or permanent financing proceeds. Loans to REITs and unsecured loans to developers count when performance is closely linked to CRE markets. Loans on nonfarm nonresidential property repaid from the operations of the owner-occupant or its affiliate are excluded.

### Segmentation is prescribed by name, and slicing to evade the ratio is called out

- Encouraged stratifications: property type, geographic market, tenant concentrations, tenant industries, developer concentrations, and risk rating. Other useful ones named: loan structure (fixed or adjustable), loan purpose (construction, short-term, or permanent), loan-to-value limits, debt service coverage, policy exceptions on newly underwritten credit facilities, and affiliated loans such as loans to tenants. The institution should also aggregate exposure to a borrower, including derivative credit exposure.
- Stratification "should be reasonable and supportable" and the portfolio "should not be divided into multiple segments simply to avoid the appearance of concentration risk."
- Management reporting should clearly indicate changes in the portfolio's risk profile "including risk-rating migrations," and should include a defined process for reviewing concentration reports plus ad hoc analysis in response to market events.

### Limits, sublimits, and a second definition of "concentration"

- The Concentrations of Credit handbook defines a concentration as direct, indirect, or contingent obligations exceeding 25 percent of tier 1 capital plus ALLL or ACL. That is a different and lower trigger than the 100 / 300 percent CRE screening criteria and applies to any pool, so a single property-type or single-MSA segment can be a reportable concentration long before the CRE ratio moves.
- The handbook expects limits to be set from the pool's risk characteristics, not its size alone: broad pools such as CRE carry necessarily higher limits than narrow subsegments such as acquisition, development, and construction, and "when a bank sets higher concentration limits for broadly defined pools - especially when those limits are more than 100 percent of capital - sound concentration risk management typically includes setting appropriate sublimits for material groups of segmented exposures."
- It also frames limits in loss terms rather than balance terms: pools whose estimated losses would equal a set percentage of earnings, would reduce the ACL by a set percentage, would equal a set percentage of capital or move the bank to a lower prompt-corrective-action category, would force a dividend cut, or would cause a ratings downgrade. A common practical construction combines a size trigger below 25 percent of capital (10, 15, or 20 percent) with a credit-quality trigger such as special mention plus classified exceeding a set percentage of the pool, in either static or dynamic (rate-of-change) form.
- Correlation is the reason segmentation alone is not enough: pools with weak performance correlation in a benign environment can correlate strongly in a deteriorating one, and stress testing is named as the tool for identifying correlated pools and quantifying the impact on earnings and capital adequacy.

### A separate, hard-numbered concentration limit already exists for LTV exceptions

- The CRE Lending handbook reproduces the Interagency Guidelines for Real Estate Lending supervisory LTV limits: raw land 65 percent; land development or improved lots 75 percent; commercial, multifamily, and other nonresidential construction 80 percent; 1-4 family residential construction 85 percent; improved commercial, multifamily, and other nonresidential property 85 percent; owner-occupied 1-4 family and home equity 90 percent with credit enhancement at or above 90 percent.
- The aggregate of all loans exceeding the supervisory LTV limits at origination should not exceed 100 percent of total capital, and within that basket, loans on commercial, agricultural, multifamily, or other non-1-4 family residential properties should not exceed 30 percent of total capital. Supervisory scrutiny increases as the totals approach those levels. The entire outstanding balance counts toward the basket, not just the portion above the limit. Loans that conformed at origination and later fell out of conformity because collateral declined are not exceptions and do not count.
- Where a loan funds multiple phases, the applicable limit is the one for the final phase funded.

### HVCRE is a capital charge, not a concentration ratio, and it has its own segmentation

- An HVCRE exposure is a credit facility secured by land or improved real property that primarily finances or refinances acquisition, development, or construction; provides financing to acquire, develop, or improve the property into income-producing real property; and depends on future income, sales proceeds, or refinancing. HVCRE carries a 150 percent risk weight under the standardized approach.
- The December 2019 revision aligned the definition with the statutory HVCRE ADC loan in section 214 of the Economic Growth, Regulatory Relief, and Consumer Protection Act of 2018 and added exemptions: 1-4 family residential ADC including condominium and cooperative construction; community development and agricultural land; facilities financing income-producing real property whose cash flow services debt and expenses under the institution's permanent-financing underwriting criteria; facilities financing improvements to existing income-producing real property meeting similar criteria; and projects where the borrower contributed capital equal to 15 percent of the "as completed" appraised value. ADC loans made before January 1, 2015 are exempt.
- The 15 percent contribution must be cash, unencumbered readily marketable assets, paid development expenses out of pocket, or contributed real property or improvements; must be in place before any advance; and must contractually remain until reclassification. Reclassification to non-HVCRE requires substantial completion and cash flow sufficient to support debt service and expenses under the bank's permanent-financing criteria.
- Consequence for a portfolio report: the HVCRE bucket is a capital-consuming segment that overlaps but is not identical to the construction and land development bucket used for the 100 percent criterion.

### Stress testing expectations, method choices, and the named shock variables

- The 2006 guidance requires portfolio-level stress tests or sensitivity analysis quantifying the impact of changing economic conditions on asset quality, earnings, and capital, with sophistication consistent with the size, complexity, and risk of the CRE book. It says a sophisticated portfolio model may not be necessary and that testing "may be as simple as analyzing the potential effect of stressed loss rates on the CRE portfolio, capital, and earnings," and directs the analysis to "the more vulnerable segments." It contrasts well-margined seasoned performing multifamily, which normally needs significantly less robust testing, with most acquisition, development, and construction lending.
- OCC Bulletin 2012-33 separates transaction-level testing (a borrower's ability to service debt under changed conditions, useful for early problem identification) from portfolio-level testing, and portfolio-level into bottom-up (aggregating individual transaction results) and top-down (applying estimated stress loss rates to pools with common risk characteristics). Scenario analysis applies a coherent narrative across multiple variables; sensitivity analysis moves specific variables in isolation. Testing should occur at least annually, and results should drive mitigation - adjusted underwriting, portfolio rebalancing, capital raising - with the allowance increasing commensurate with portfolio stress.
- FDIC examination procedure 33 lists the MIS inputs stress testing needs: stratified portfolios, recent interest and capitalization rates, net operating incomes, average loan-to-value and debt-service ratios, and current client / tenant operating incomes.
- Procedure 35 sets sample selection on vulnerable segments, with pooling factors of purpose, source of repayment, low DSC, high LTV, geographic location, property type, and industry type, plus loans exposed to negative current or future market trends. Procedure 36 requires the sample to reflect the pool's characteristics.
- Procedure 37 lists the shock variables by name - increases in interest rates, overall changes in property values, changes in property vacancy or absorption rates, declines in NOI, and changes in capitalization or discount rates - and says the resulting impact on DSC and LTV ratios is typically evaluated.
- Procedure 38 lists the destinations for results: CRE strategies, policies, staffing and managerial needs, capital planning, funding requirements, underwriting criteria, loan pricing, and risk limits. Procedure 39 requires periodic comprehensive independent validation of the model or methodology. Procedure 40 asks whether the methodology considers volatility in supply and demand for lots, retail and office space, and multifamily units across business cycles. Procedures 41 and 42 tie the results into capital planning (including anticipated charge-offs) and the ACL analysis.

### Structure features that decide which stress bites

- The CRE Lending handbook states that interest-only terms or long amortization can raise debt service coverage and lower payment-default likelihood while increasing loss given default and balloon or full-repayment risk at maturity. Even where terms permit interest-only payments, the property should meet the bank's coverage requirement as though the loan were amortizing, and LTV and as-if-amortizing coverage requirements for interest-only loans are generally more conservative than for amortizing loans. Interest-only tenors are typically three to five years maximum.
- A renewal, refinancing, or extension on an interest-only basis can indicate a troubled loan; absent strong LTV and as-if-amortizing coverage, interest-only periods should generally be limited to construction or stabilization periods.
- Amortization: no regulatory maximum, but 30 years is generally the prudent ceiling for income-producing CRE, with a 15-to-30-year range in most cases - stabilized multifamily at the high end, hotels generally not more than 20 years, office, retail, and industrial generally 25 years.
- Borrowers who can meet payment obligations may still be unable to refinance a balloon at maturity because of declines in property value. That is the mechanism behind a refinance test and the reason a book can look current and still fail.

### Current market and portfolio context (time-sensitive, directional)

- FDIC Quarterly Banking Profile for Q2 2026, released August 25, 2026: industry past-due and nonaccrual fell 9 basis points to 1.44 percent, and the largest quarterly PDNA decline was in nonfarm nonresidential CRE, down $2.4 billion or 14 basis points to 1.52 percent; industry net charge-off rate fell 2 basis points to 0.57 percent. Community banks showed PDNA of 1.34 percent with reserve coverage of 145.6 percent, and their nonfarm nonresidential balances were up 6.6 percent year over year.
- Federal Reserve July 2026 SLOOS: standards eased on net for nonfarm nonresidential (moderate net share) and multifamily (modest net share) in Q2 2026, while construction and land development standards were basically unchanged; CLD demand was weaker, and NFNR and multifamily demand was unchanged overall with large banks reporting stronger and smaller banks weaker demand.
- Trepp data reported July 27, 2026: June 2026 CMBS delinquency 7.35 percent overall, office 11.57 percent, multifamily 7.23 percent, retail 6.91 percent, lodging 5.22 percent, industrial 1.20 percent; the rate rises to 9.53 percent when performing matured balloons are included, and non-performing matured balloons were 65 percent of newly delinquent balances. Different population and definitions from the FDIC bank data; not comparable.
- Federal Reserve 2026 supervisory stress test, released June 24, 2026: severely adverse scenario used a 39 percent CRE price decline, a 30 percent house price decline, and a 10 percent unemployment peak, producing roughly $75 billion of CRE loan losses across the 32 tested banks with an aggregate capital decline of 1.6 percentage points and all banks above minimums. This is a large-bank supervisory scenario, useful only as a labeled directional upper bound for a smaller institution.

Case-by-case only, never hard-coded:

- Shock severity. No source sets a required decline for an individual institution. Severity is a board decision anchored to the institution's own loss experience, current market data by MSA and property type, and the post-stress capital floor. The Federal Reserve's 39 percent CRE price decline may be quoted only as a labeled borrowed upper bound from a large-bank supervisory scenario.
- Internal concentration limits and sublimits by property type, MSA, sponsor, or tenant industry. These are board-approved and institution-specific; the guidance sets none.
- Post-stress capital floors, migration-to-classification mappings, and stressed loss rates by segment.
- Rating migration percentages. No regulator publishes a stressed migration matrix; the institution's own vintage and segment history is the only defensible input.
- Refinance-test constants and stressed cap rates. Derived from current market quotes at the time of the test and stated as of a date.

Formulas the skill uses, all built from the sources above and from `knowledge/underwriting-calc.md` for the underlying cash-flow math:

```
Construction Ratio   = Construction, Land Development and Other Land / (Tier 1 + ACL)
Total CRE Ratio      = Total Non-Owner-Occupied CRE (guidance definition) / (Tier 1 + ACL)
36-Month CRE Growth  = (Current CRE Balance / CRE Balance 36 Months Prior) - 1
Segment Ratio        = Segment Balance / (Tier 1 + ACL)          [25 percent general trigger]
Stressed Value       = Stressed NOI / Stressed Cap Rate
Refinance Gap        = Loan Balance at Maturity - min(Proceeds by stressed LTV, Proceeds by stressed DSCR, Proceeds by stressed debt yield)
Post-Stress Capital  = (Capital - Stressed Losses net of tax and pre-provision earnings) / Stressed Risk-Weighted Assets
```

## Conflicting Source Resolution

- **Denominator.** The 2006 guidance footnotes say total risk-based capital; OCC Bulletin 2020-29 and the CRE Lending handbook say tier 1 plus ALLL / ACL for supervisory concentration measurement. The later, purpose-specific joint statement controls. Any ratio must name its denominator, because the two produce different numbers on the same book.
- **What counts as a "concentration."** The 2006 CRE criteria (100 / 300 percent) and the Concentrations of Credit handbook trigger (25 percent of tier 1 plus ALLL / ACL for any pool) are different instruments, not competing thresholds. The skill reports both rather than choosing one.
- **Bank asset quality vs CMBS delinquency.** FDIC's Q2 2026 nonfarm nonresidential PDNA of 1.52 percent and Trepp's June 2026 CMBS delinquency of 7.35 percent overall and 11.57 percent office are frequently quoted against each other. They cover different populations under different definitions and are not comparable; the skill labels each.
- **Stress severity.** The 2006 guidance permits a simple stressed-loss-rate approach; the Federal Reserve's supervisory stress test uses a detailed multi-variable scenario. Both are legitimate at their own scale. Primary regulator guidance for the institution's own size class takes priority, and a borrowed large-bank scenario value must be labeled as borrowed.
- **Direction of travel.** Q2 2026 bank asset quality improved and standards eased on net for NFNR and multifamily, while CMBS matured-balloon defaults kept climbing. The skill treats improving delinquency as a lagging indicator and does not let it override a failing refinance test.

## Edge Cases and Red Flags

- **Owner-occupied classification.** A nonfarm nonresidential loan repaid by the occupying business is out of the concentration numerator but not out of portfolio risk analysis. Institutions that classify by collateral code rather than by repayment source will misstate the ratio in both directions.
- **REIT loans, unsecured developer lines, participations, and unfunded construction commitments.** In scope under the guidance definition when performance tracks CRE markets, and routinely missing from a collateral-driven extract.
- **HVCRE overlap.** The HVCRE bucket is not the construction and land development bucket. Reporting one as the other misstates both the 100 percent criterion and the capital impact.
- **Segment gaming.** Slicing a book into narrow segments to keep any one below a trigger is specifically identified in the guidance as improper.
- **Missing loan-level data.** Where NOI, DSC, LTV, maturity date, rate type, or property type are unreliable, a bottom-up test is not available. Run a labeled top-down test on stressed loss rates instead and report the data gap as a finding, not a footnote.
- **Stale valuations.** A cap-rate or value shock applied to an appraisal that is years old compounds two errors. Where the valuation is stale or its assumptions no longer hold, route to [Appraisal and Valuation Reviewer](skills/lender-credit/appraisal-and-valuation-reviewer.md) before treating the stressed value as usable.
- **Classification consequences.** A stressed rating migration is a planning output, not a rating action. Actual downgrades, nonaccrual, and charge-off decisions follow the interagency definitions and the institution's policy. The 2023 workouts policy statement is explicit that a performing loan is not adversely classified solely because collateral value fell below the balance.
- **Contingency plans that assume the exit.** Portfolio liquidity is listed as a mitigating factor in the 2006 guidance, but a plan to sell or securitize in the same stress that would close those markets is not a mitigant.
- **Credit unions and non-banks.** NCUA-supervised credit unions operate under a different rule set, and debt funds, life companies, and CMBS originators are outside the interagency framework entirely. Applying bank ratio vocabulary to them without saying so is a reporting error.
- **Growth outrunning infrastructure.** Rapid growth in a segment the institution cannot underwrite, monitor, or work out is a finding independent of any ratio, and FDIC's 2023 advisory names workout infrastructure as one of the six actions.

## Open Questions

- NCUA's parallel commercial lending and concentration framework for credit unions is not covered here and would need its own research note.
- No interagency source prescribes a stressed rating migration matrix or stressed loss rates by CRE property type, so those remain institution-specific and were not defaulted.
- Practices for stressing floating-rate loans with interest rate caps, and for treating cap expiry inside the stress horizon, vary by institution and were not standardized here.
- Treatment of unfunded construction commitments in the concentration numerator differs across institutions and was left to board-approved policy.
- Climate, insurance-cost, and property-tax reassessment shocks are increasingly run in practice but are not named in the shock-variable list in the examination module, so they are offered as optional supplemental sensitivities rather than defaults.
