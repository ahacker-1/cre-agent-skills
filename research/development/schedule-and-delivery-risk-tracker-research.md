# Schedule and Delivery Risk Tracker Research

## Purpose

- Supports `skills/development/schedule-and-delivery-risk-tracker.md`
- Supports the schedule and lease-up duration sections of `knowledge/development-benchmarks.md` and the completion, change, and draw-control sections of `knowledge/construction-contracts-and-draw-controls.md`
- Intended users: developers, development managers, owner representatives, construction lenders and their inspectors, and equity partners tracking a project between notice to proceed and stabilization

## U.S.-Only Assumptions

- Geography: United States
- Deal type: ground-up development and heavy redevelopment across multifamily, industrial, retail, office, and mixed-use, financed with a construction loan and delivered under an owner-contractor agreement
- Legal and market assumptions: schedule, delay, and time-extension mechanics come from the executed contract, which is usually an AIA form modified by supplementary conditions; permitting, inspection, and certificate-of-occupancy authority is local; federal schedule specifications and guides are used here as published, non-anonymous methodology anchors, not as private-market requirements. Nothing here is legal, tax, investment, accounting, or financing advice.

## Key Findings

**What makes a schedule usable as a control document.** GAO-16-89G sets four characteristics of a reliable schedule (comprehensive, well-constructed, credible, controlled) and ten best practices, including confirming that the critical path is valid, ensuring reasonable total float, conducting a schedule risk analysis, updating with actual progress and logic, and maintaining a baseline. GSA's P-120 adopts those characteristics directly for federal building projects and adds owner-side mechanics: a frozen baseline, periodic statused updates compared against it, rebaselining only with stakeholder agreement and archiving of prior baselines, a written explanation for schedule variances over 15%, and earned value management on projects over $20 million.

**Critical path and float.** UFGS-01 32 01.00 10 (August 2026) defines the critical path as the longest path and requires all critical activities to be shown. Both that specification and the A201 package reviewed state that float belongs to the project rather than to any party: the A201 text says unused time "shall belong to the Project," is not owned or subject to the exclusive use of any participant, and that no claim may be made for loss of float. GAO adds the interpretive rules: activities on the critical path have the least total float; unreasonably high total float indicates missing or invalid logic; large negative float values usually indicate missing logic or unrealistic sequencing; every activity with negative float should be questioned, and if the negative float cannot be mitigated the milestone should forecast a slip. GAO also warns explicitly against adhering to a single target float value such as a maximum of two working periods or ten percent of program duration, and describes near-critical paths as those within a float band tailored to the project, giving 5 days on a brief schedule and 2 to 3 months on a multiyear program as illustrations.

**Schedule contingency.** GAO treats schedule contingency as a reserve of time between the last activity's finish and the committed finish milestone, calculated by running a schedule risk analysis and comparing the deterministic date to the simulation result at a chosen confidence level. It notes an 80 percent chance of finishing on time or earlier as a conservative promise date, and that the 55th and 65th percentiles are less certain because schedule distributions are right skewed. Contingency is held by the project manager, released only through formal change control, and may sit as a single activity before the finish milestone or be dispersed before key milestones.

**Delay, extension, and recovery mechanics.** A201 8.1.1 defines Contract Time as the period allotted for Substantial Completion, and 8.2.1 states that the contract time already takes into account average weather and usual industrial conditions in the locality. Section 8.3.1 lists the grounds for an extension: acts or neglect of the owner, architect, or a separate contractor; changes ordered in the work; labor disputes, fire, unusual delay in deliveries, unavoidable casualties, or documented adverse weather; and other causes the owner's representative determines justify delay. Section 15.1.6.2 sets the proof standard for a weather claim: data showing conditions were abnormal for the period, could not have been reasonably anticipated, and adversely affected the scheduled construction. UFGS requires anticipated adverse weather to be built into the schedule, preferably through a weather calendar following AACE 84R-13, prospective time impact analysis following AACE 52R-06 using a fragnet tied into the last approved schedule, and retrospective analysis following AACE 29R-03 within the principles of ASCE 67-17. Critically, it grants a time extension only when the delay consumes all available project float and pushes the finish milestone past the contract completion date. When progress falls behind for non-excusable reasons the contractor must submit a recovery plan showing added crews, hours, or workdays, and improving progress artificially by revising logic, adding constraints, shortening durations, or changing calendars is prohibited. FAR 52.236-15 gives the same pattern in a shorter form, and FAR 52.249-14 supplies the standard list of excusable causes including unusually severe weather.

**Who eats a delay.** The A201 package reviewed carries the mutual waiver of consequential damages at 15.1.7: the owner waives claims for rental expenses, loss of use, income, profit, and financing. That waiver is why liquidated damages, not a general damages claim, are usually the owner's only monetary schedule remedy against the contractor, and it is why owner-side delay cost has to be quantified and managed rather than assumed recoverable.

**Lender consequences of a slip.** The OCC handbook requires an interest reserve sized to pay interest through anticipated completion and lease-up, sale, or occupancy; states that the presence of a reserve may not reflect ability to pay; directs that during lease-up project cash flow is applied to interest before reserve draws and that draws stop once cash flow covers interest; and treats repacking a depleted reserve with new debt as a red flag of credit deterioration that warrants a new appraisal and a fresh feasibility review. It also states that a bank's extension options should be consistent with expected construction time plus the projected absorption period, and that renewals or extensions on an interest-only basis can indicate a troubled loan. The FDIC examination procedures ask whether the building and loan agreement sets a completion date, whether increases or extensions of interest reserves rest on a realistic assessment of the completion timeframe and guarantor capacity, and whether take-out agreements auto-extend the completion date for delays beyond the builder's control.

**Occupancy is the last dependency.** Under the Section 111 code text reviewed, a building may not be used or occupied until the building official issues a certificate of occupancy; a temporary certificate may be issued before completion of the entire permitted work for portions that can be occupied safely; and a certificate may be suspended or revoked if issued in error or on incorrect information. No certificate means no legal occupancy, which means no rent commencement regardless of construction status.

**Duration anchors are thin and dated.** The NAHB-NMHC regulation survey, fielded April 2022, uses Census Survey of Construction averages for 2001 through 2016 of 1.94 months from authorization to start and 13.21 months from start to completion for buildings of 10 or more units, and 1.95 and 11.64 months for 5 to 9 unit buildings. The same survey found neighborhood opposition adds an average of 7.4 months of delay when present. AvalonBay's 10-K organizes its development table around construction start, initial projected or actual occupancy, estimated completion, and estimated stabilized operations, and defines stabilized operations as the earlier of 90 percent physical occupancy or the one-year anniversary of completion.

**Current pressure points.** Turner reported its Building Cost Index at 1552 in Q2 2026, up 1.44 percent for the quarter and 5.15 percent year over year, and named availability of skilled mechanical and electrical labor as the industry's biggest challenge while emphasizing early procurement. Cumming Group reported construction labor costs up 3.7 percent year over year as of June 2025, with scarce electricians and plumbers and shortages expected to produce longer project timelines. CBRE reported Q2 2026 multifamily completions of 77,700 units, down 14 percent year over year, against 167,000 units of net absorption, with vacancy at 4.3 percent and rent growth of 0.5 percent year over year, so a delivery pushed by a few quarters lands in a measurably different supply and rent environment.

## Benchmark and Formula Decisions

Suitable as repo defaults:

- Milestone spine for a development schedule: entitlement approval, permit issuance, notice to proceed, foundation complete, structure topped out or dry-in, mechanical, electrical and plumbing rough-in complete, life-safety and elevator acceptance, temporary certificate of occupancy, certificate of occupancy, first unit or suite delivery, substantial completion, final completion, stabilization.
- Critical path is the longest path; report total float per milestone against the current data date; treat float as belonging to the project and not to a party.
- Every activity or milestone showing negative float is questioned, and if the negative float cannot be mitigated the forecast milestone date is moved to eliminate it rather than left constrained.
- Report both a deterministic date and a risk-adjusted date. Where a schedule risk analysis exists, state the percentile used and prefer the 80th percentile as the committed date; where none exists, say so and treat the deterministic date as unvalidated.
- Schedule variance greater than 15 percent against the accepted baseline requires a written explanation, following GSA P-120.
- Time extension test: a delay event earns contract time only when it consumes all remaining project float and pushes the finish milestone past the contract completion date.
- Weather test: an adverse weather claim requires data showing the weather was abnormal for the period, was not reasonably anticipable, and actually affected scheduled work, and the schedule should already carry a weather calendar or equivalent allowance.
- Interest reserve runway in months equals remaining funded interest reserve divided by projected monthly interest at the current rate and projected balance. Compare that runway to remaining construction months plus the months to the coverage or debt yield test that unlocks an extension, not to construction alone.
- Monthly delay cost equals interest carry on the projected average outstanding balance, plus real estate taxes and insurance, plus site general conditions and owner staffing, plus contractor extended general conditions if owed, plus escalation on unbought scope, plus foregone net operating income from delayed rent commencement, plus any extension fee or rate-cap replacement cost amortized over the delay, less liquidated damages actually collectible.
- Recovery plans must show the resource, sequence, or scope change that produces the recovery. A revised schedule that recovers time by changing logic, shortening durations, adding constraints, or changing calendars is treated as an unsupported recovery, not a recovery.

Case-by-case only, no repo default:

- Any specific near-critical float band, target float value, or float-as-a-percentage-of-duration rule. GAO explicitly rejects a universal value; the band must be set for the project and stated in the output.
- Procurement lead times for switchgear, transformers, generators, elevators, glazing, and rooftop equipment. No published, non-anonymous national lead-time table was located in this research, so lead times must come from the actual purchase orders, vendor confirmations, and submittal log on the project.
- Liquidated damages rates, extended general conditions rates, and acceleration pricing.
- Absolute entitlement or permit durations by jurisdiction, which belong to [Entitlement and Site Risk](knowledge/entitlement-and-site-risk.md) and the local record.

## Conflicting Source Resolution

- Federal schedule specifications and guides versus private practice: GAO, GSA, and the UFGS specification are the only sources located that publish schedule method requirements openly and attributably, so they are used for method, definitions, and tests. They are not evidence of private-market contract terms, and the skill says so. Where a private contract differs, the executed contract governs.
- Float ownership: the UFGS specification and the A201 package reviewed agree that float belongs to the project. Many negotiated private agreements assign float to a party or make it available on a first-use basis. The research adopts project-owned float as the reference position and directs the user to read the executed schedule specification, because the answer changes who gets paid for a delay.
- Contract-form text: the A201 copy retrieved is a state university system package containing supplementary modifications alongside the AIA base text, so section numbers are reliable as vocabulary while the wording of any specific project's form must be read from the signed document. This is consistent with the caution already carried in [Construction Contracts and Draw Controls](knowledge/construction-contracts-and-draw-controls.md).
- Duration data: the Census-derived lags reported through the NAHB-NMHC survey average 2001 through 2016 and are far shorter than the institutional development intervals recorded in [Development Benchmarks](knowledge/development-benchmarks.md), which show a median of 10 quarters from start to completion for institutional multifamily. The difference is product form and definition, not error. The skill uses project-specific durations and treats both figures as directional context only.
- Confidence percentile: GAO presents the 80th percentile as conservative and the 55th and 65th as used by some organizations. The higher standard is adopted because a construction loan maturity is a hard date and a right-skewed distribution punishes optimism.

## Edge Cases and Red Flags

- Phased or multi-building projects where a partial certificate of occupancy for one building starts revenue while the balance is incomplete, and where the loan's completion covenant may still measure the whole project.
- Owner-caused delay running concurrently with contractor-caused delay. Concurrency is a legal question and a forensic scheduling question at once; the skill flags it and stops rather than allocating fault.
- A temporary certificate of occupancy used as the delivery date in the lease-up plan when it carries conditions, an expiration, or open life-safety items, and when it can be revoked.
- Long-lead equipment that has been ordered but not confirmed, or that was value-engineered into a substitution after the original order, resetting the clock.
- Tenant delivery obligations with outside dates that give the tenant a rent abatement or termination right; those dates can bind before the loan does.
- A slip that crosses a seasonal boundary, converting a schedule question into a weather and escalation question.
- A GC schedule update that shows the same finish date every month while activities slip, which usually means float is being consumed silently or logic is being adjusted.
- Interest reserve refunded or repacked with loan proceeds, which the OCC handbook treats as a credit-deterioration signal rather than a budget amendment.
- Any output that assumes delay damages are recoverable from the contractor without checking the consequential damages waiver and the liquidated damages clause.

## Open Questions

- No published, attributable national table of construction equipment procurement lead times was located during this research. Until one is, lead times remain a project-record input.
- AACE Recommended Practices 29R-03, 52R-06, and 84R-13 and ASCE 67-17 are cited by name in the federal specification but sit behind membership or purchase walls, so their content is not reproduced here and their methods are referenced only as named by the specification.
- Ground-up lease-up durations for retail, office, and mixed-use remain unbenchmarked in public filings, matching the gap already recorded in [Development Benchmarks](knowledge/development-benchmarks.md).
- State-by-state treatment of concurrent delay, no-damages-for-delay clauses, and the enforceability of liquidated damages is a counsel question and was not researched here.
