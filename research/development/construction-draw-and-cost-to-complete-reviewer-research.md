# Construction Draw and Cost-to-Complete Reviewer Research

## Purpose

- Supports `skills/development/construction-draw-and-cost-to-complete-reviewer.md`
- Pairs with the existing pack knowledge bases [Construction Contracts and Draw Controls](knowledge/construction-contracts-and-draw-controls.md) and [Construction Lending Criteria](knowledge/construction-lending-criteria.md); this note supplies the source basis for the monthly draw review procedure rather than restating those references
- Intended users: developers and development managers assembling a draw, owner representatives and construction consultants certifying one, construction lenders and loan administrators funding one, and equity partners monitoring cost to complete

## U.S.-Only Assumptions

- Geography: United States. Lien law, retainage limits, prompt-payment deadlines, and title endorsement practice are state-specific and vary materially; nothing here is a national rule.
- Deal type: ground-up construction and heavy redevelopment of income-producing commercial and multifamily property financed by a construction loan with periodic advances, built under an owner-contractor agreement with a schedule of values.
- Legal and market assumptions: the executed loan agreement, the executed construction contract, and the governing state's lien statute control. Federal regulatory material cited here binds U.S. banks and savings institutions and FHA-insured programs; it does not bind debt funds, private lenders, or equity partners, who nonetheless commonly copy the same controls. Federal acquisition rules apply to federal construction contracts and are used here as a documented model of progress-payment mechanics, not as private-market law.
- This is educational decision support, not legal, tax, investment, accounting, or financing advice.

## Key Findings

### The draw is a reconciliation, not an approval queue

The OCC handbook frames disbursement as a control process rather than a payment step: funds should not be advanced unless they are used solely for the financed project and as stipulated in the draw request and the loan agreement, and banks confirm the budget remains in balance with sufficient funds available to fund completion. The handbook names the specific abuses to guard against by funding only what is stipulated: rebuilding to undisclosed specification changes, starting a new project, paying subcontractors for work performed elsewhere, and paying the developer's general corporate overhead.

### Percent complete must be verified independently, not asserted

The OCC handbook states that banks typically require architect or engineering inspection reports with each draw to verify work is done according to specification, that a bank representative typically conducts periodic site inspections, and that inspection reports state compliance with plans and specifications and support disbursements typically based on percentage complete. It adds that construction credit administration should be independent of the loan origination function when possible, with compensating controls and segregation of duties where it is not, and that banks often retain an independent construction consulting firm when they lack in-house engineering, architectural, and construction expertise.

HUD's program runs the same logic on a documented form set: the amount of construction funds approved and advanced must be consistent with construction progress as approved by the HUD Inspector, interim advances are processed and approved by the lender on that basis, each inspection is recorded on form HUD-95379 (HUD Representative's Trip Report), the contractor requisitions on form HUD-92448, and the advance is applied for on form HUD-92403. Other mortgageable items must be adequately documented with bills or receipts before funds are approved.

### Front loading is the named failure mode

The OCC handbook defines front loading as the practice whereby a builder deliberately overstates the cost of the work to be completed in the early stages of construction, and states that if the bank does not detect front loading in the early stages there will almost certainly be insufficient loan funds to complete construction if there is a default. This is the reason a schedule of values review belongs at the front of a draw review and is repeated whenever the SOV is revised.

### Retainage is set by statute and program, not habit

Three retained-funds regimes appear in the sources and they do not agree, which is exactly the point:

- OCC (private commercial practice, descriptive): under a progress payment plan the bank normally retains 10% to 20% of each payment to cover cost overruns or outstanding bills.
- California (private work, prescriptive as of 2026-01-01): Civil Code 8811 caps retention at 5% of the payment and 5% of the contract price, flows the cap down to every tier, is non-waivable, and carries a mandatory attorney fee award. Narrow exceptions exist for non-mixed-use residential of four stories or fewer and where a subcontractor fails to provide required bonds after proper notice.
- Texas (owner-side, prescriptive): Property Code 53.101 requires the owner to reserve 10% of the contract price, or 10% of the value of work measured by proportion completed, during the work and for 30 days after completion. Section 53.103 gives a claimant a lien on those reserved funds if the claimant sends the required notices and files an affidavit within 30 days of the earliest of completion, termination, or abandonment.
- HUD FHA-insured multifamily: 10% holdback on construction items until 50% completion, then 5% until 75% completion, then 2.5% until Final Endorsement, available only where the contractor's identity of interest in the ownership entity is not greater than 5%, prior written surety consent is attached to the reduction request, and there are no questions about quality, contract compliance, or change orders.
- Federal construction contracts: retainage is discretionary and capped, not routine. FAR 32.103 caps retainage at 10% of the approved estimated amount and states it should complement rather than substitute for contract management; FAR 52.232-5 permits the contracting officer to retain a maximum of 10% until satisfactory progress is achieved.

### Lien waiver sequencing is a legal mechanism, not paperwork

California's statutory forms make the sequencing rule explicit and are the clearest available model. Under Civil Code 8132 a conditional waiver on progress payment is "effective only on the claimant's receipt of payment." Under Civil Code 8134 an unconditional waiver carries the statutory notice "THIS DOCUMENT IS ENFORCEABLE AGAINST YOU IF YOU SIGN IT, EVEN IF YOU HAVE NOT BEEN PAID," and directs a claimant who has not been paid to use the conditional form instead. Both forms preserve retention, unpaid extras, and breach claims. The operational consequence: conditional waivers travel with the current request, unconditional waivers arrive as proof the prior advance funded, and a package containing unconditional waivers for work not yet paid is a control failure regardless of what the loan agreement says.

The OCC handbook confirms the same sequencing at the lender level: the draw request should include waivers from subcontractors and suppliers indicating that payment has been received for work completed, and before releasing the final draw and the holdback the bank confirms the borrower has obtained all waivers of liens or releases from contractors, subcontractors, and suppliers, reviews the final inspection report confirming completion to building specifications, and confirms a certificate of occupancy.

### Title priority is preserved draw by draw

The OCC handbook states the lender's title policy should be updated with each draw so the title company confirms there are no outstanding liens, notes mechanics liens are the most common form, and observes that in some jurisdictions mechanics liens can take priority over the bank lien, which is precisely why the update matters in those jurisdictions. ALTA's form library carries the matching instruments: the Endorsement 32 series (32, 32.1 Direct Payment, 32.2 Insured's Direct Payment, current 2021 v.01.01 effective 2021-07-01) for construction loan coverage and Endorsement 33 (Disbursement, adopted 2011-02-03) issued with subsequent advances. A missing or out-of-sequence date-down leaves the advance uncovered for lien rights that accrued in the gap.

### Stored materials are the softest line in any application

HUD's rules are the most explicit U.S. standard retrieved. Only "building components" qualify for insured advances when stored offsite, defined as manufactured or pre-assembled elements impractical to store on site by reason of bulk, size, weight, weather vulnerability, or lack of space, with examples of precast concrete panels, assembled bath or kitchen core units, and fully fabricated structural steel. Kitchen appliances, carpeting, and wood roof trusses are expressly not eligible. Payments are limited to the invoice value of the components. The lender must file UCC-1 financing statements perfecting a first-lien security interest and make additional filings as needed until the components are incorporated, components must be stored at a location approved by the lender, a bill of sale evidencing the borrower's title plus an itemized invoice must accompany each request, and the construction contract must carry the offsite amendment rider and be secured by a 100% performance and payment bond.

### Contingency, change orders, and who pays

The OCC handbook distinguishes the two cases directly: construction budget contingency is intended to cover reasonable but unexpected cost increases such as material price increases, overtime from shipment delays, or adverse weather; where a cost overrun results from poor projections or management, the increased cost would ordinarily be covered by the borrower rather than by a draw on the budgeted contingency. It also requires that changes in construction plans be reviewed by competent staff or a construction consulting firm, approved and documented by the bank and any take-out lender, and reflected in the project's budget, and warns that a significant number of change orders could indicate poor planning or design or problems in construction.

AIA's guidance supplies the sizing frame and the ownership rule: construction contingency of 5% to 10% is common based on risk, difficulty, and complexity; design contingency runs 5% to 10% of overall construction costs and should be held separately by the owner; and any remaining construction contingency is the owner's, not the contractor's. AIA also directs that a construction change directive be priced as if it were a proposed change order and recorded in the change log, which is the discipline that keeps unpriced directives from hiding in the cost to complete.

### Interest reserve burn is a diagnostic, not a budget line

The OCC handbook is unusually direct here. An appropriate interest reserve provides sufficient funds to pay interest through anticipated completion and lease-up, sale, or occupancy, but "the presence of an interest reserve may not accurately reflect a borrower's ability to pay," and inappropriately administered reserves can mask a poorly performing project. During lease-up, project cash flow is ordinarily applied to interest before the reserve is drawn, and once cash flow is sufficient to cover interest no further reserve draws should be permitted. Extended lease-up can deplete the reserve prematurely and render the construction budget inadequate. The decision to revise the budget and repack the interest reserve with debt is described as "a red flag indicating possible credit deterioration," properly supported only by a new appraisal or evaluation and a re-evaluation of feasibility in the current market.

### Cost to complete is a required monthly output

The OCC handbook lists the monitoring package explicitly: monthly reports of work completed, costs-to-date, costs-to-complete, construction deadlines, and loan funds remaining, plus monthly leasing reports with rent rolls during lease-up compared against the underwriting pro forma. Its examination procedures direct examiners to evaluate the basis of disbursements including costs incurred, percentage of completion, and cost to complete, and to determine whether material changes have been made to the plans and to the construction budget and why. A draw package that cannot produce a current cost to complete is not reviewable.

## Benchmark and Formula Decisions

Suitable as repo defaults because they are traceable to a named source:

- **In-balance test**, carried forward unchanged from [Construction Lending Criteria](knowledge/construction-lending-criteria.md): remaining loan commitment plus unfunded borrower equity plus remaining contingency must be at least cost to complete plus remaining interest and carry. The OCC handbook supports the underlying expectation that banks confirm the budget remains in balance with sufficient funds available to fund completion; the arithmetic form is the repo's standardization of that expectation, not an OCC formula.
- **Cost to complete** = revised budget by line, less cost incurred to date (completed and stored), less amounts committed but not yet incurred where those are tracked separately, plus the priced value of pending change orders and open construction change directives, plus remaining interest and carry. Sourced to the OCC monitoring list (work completed, costs-to-date, costs-to-complete, loan funds remaining) plus AIA's instruction to price CCDs as if they were proposed change orders.
- **Percent complete reconciliation tolerance is a decision rule, not a number.** The repo default is that any line where the application's percent complete exceeds the independent inspector's observation is trimmed to the inspector's figure for funding purposes, and the aggregate variance is disclosed. HUD's rule that the advance must be consistent with inspector-approved progress and the OCC's rule that disbursements are typically based on inspection-supported percentage complete both point the same way. No source supports a blanket "within 5%" tolerance, so none is asserted.
- **Retainage** is stated as a range with the controlling authority named, never as a single default: 10%-20% of each payment as descriptive U.S. commercial bank practice (OCC); 5% cap on California private work under contracts entered on or after 2026-01-01 (Civil Code 8811); 10% owner reserve in Texas (Property Code 53.101); 10% / 5% / 2.5% step-down at 50% and 75% completion on FHA-insured multifamily (HUD MAP Guide); 10% maximum on federal contracts and only for unsatisfactory progress (FAR 32.103 and 52.232-5).
- **Contingency** 5%-10% construction and 5%-10% of construction cost for design, per AIA, as a directional starting point for judging whether remaining contingency is adequate against remaining scope. Unused construction contingency reverts to the owner.
- **Developer fee** typically does not exceed 4% of project cost, per the OCC handbook, and is often deferred or disbursed on percentage of completion. Useful when a draw seeks a disproportionate fee advance early.
- **Waiver sequencing rule**: conditional waivers with the current request, unconditional waivers for the prior funded advance, on the state's prescribed form where one exists. Sourced to California Civil Code 8132 and 8134 as the clearest statutory model, plus the OCC's requirement that waivers indicate payment has been received.
- **Final advance gate**: all lien waivers or releases, final inspection confirming completion to specification, and a certificate of occupancy before the holdback is released (OCC), with HUD adding contractor cost certification and surety consent on insured deals.

Case-by-case only, deliberately not defaulted:

- A universal retainage percentage, a universal percent-complete variance tolerance, a universal stored-materials cap as a share of the draw, a universal soft-cost backup dollar threshold, and any assumed number of days a draw should take. Each is set by the loan agreement, the construction contract, the state statute, or the lender's own policy.
- Whether pending change orders belong in cost to complete at the contractor's requested value, at the owner representative's independent estimate, or at zero. The repo shows both the requested and the independently estimated figure rather than picking one.
- Interest reserve adequacy in dollars, which depends on the rate assumption, draw curve, and absorption assumption specific to the deal.

## Conflicting Source Resolution

- **Retainage percentage.** The OCC's descriptive 10%-20% range conflicts with California's 5% statutory cap for private work and with HUD's programmatic step-down. Resolution: statute controls over descriptive supervisory commentary in the state where the work sits, and program rules control on program-insured deals. The skill therefore asks which state and which program before it evaluates retainage at all, and never carries a single national default. The OCC range survives only as a description of common private commercial practice where no statute limits it.
- **Retainage as routine versus retainage for cause.** Federal contracting treats retainage as discretionary and only for unsatisfactory progress (FAR 32.103, 52.232-5), while private construction lending treats it as a standing holdback (OCC). Resolution: these are different regimes, not a disagreement. The skill applies the private-lending framing and cites federal practice only where a project is federally contracted.
- **Contingency ownership.** AIA states unused construction contingency is the owner's, while many executed GMP agreements provide for a savings split. Resolution: the executed contract controls; AIA's position is the default the skill uses when the contract is silent, and the skill flags the question rather than assuming.
- **Cost overrun funding.** The OCC says overruns from poor projections or management are ordinarily borrower-funded rather than drawn from budget contingency, while developers routinely treat contingency as available for any overrun. Resolution: follow the OCC framing for lender-side review and the executed loan agreement for what is actually permitted; the skill distinguishes the cause of the overrun before it recommends the funding source.
- **Whether an interest reserve is coverage.** The presence of a funded reserve reads as strength in a borrower narrative and as a warning in supervisory guidance. Resolution: the OCC's explicit statement that the presence of a reserve may not accurately reflect ability to pay controls, and the skill treats reserve burn rate against remaining schedule as the diagnostic rather than treating reserve balance as comfort.

## Edge Cases and Red Flags

- **Identity of interest between owner and contractor.** HUD's retainage reduction is unavailable where the contractor's identity of interest in the ownership entity exceeds 5%, and retainage generally may not be released until Final Endorsement for such a contractor. On private deals the same relationship should defeat any assumption that pricing, buyout, and retainage release were arm's length.
- **Bonded work.** A retainage reduction on bonded work requires prior written surety consent under HUD's rule, and private sureties raise the same objection. Reducing retainage without consent can impair the bond.
- **States with prescribed statutory waiver forms.** A waiver that does not substantially follow the prescribed form can be void. Advance waivers signed before the work are void in California and treated differently elsewhere, so any waiver language embedded in a subcontract is a counsel question rather than a review finding.
- **Mechanics lien priority jurisdictions.** Where liens can prime the mortgage, the OCC treats the per-draw title update as essential rather than optional; a skipped ALTA 33 in such a state is a materially different finding than in a state where it is not.
- **Offsite stored materials that are not eligible components.** Under HUD's definition, appliances, carpeting, and wood roof trusses do not qualify, and payments are limited to invoice value. Private lenders vary, but a request to fund commodity materials stored offsite with no bill of sale, no approved storage location, no UCC filing, and no insurance should not be funded on the strength of an invoice alone.
- **Open construction change directives.** Work proceeds under a directive before price is agreed, so the contract sum, the cost to complete, and the schedule can all be stale at once. AIA's instruction to price a CCD as if it were a proposed change order is the practical fix.
- **Interest reserve repack.** The OCC treats replenishing a depleted reserve with new loan proceeds as a credit-deterioration signal requiring a new appraisal or evaluation and a fresh feasibility review, not a routine budget amendment. When a draw review reaches this point, FDIC FIL-64-2023 and Federal Reserve SR 23-5 describe the supervisory posture the lender is operating in.
- **Prompt-payment exposure.** Federal construction contracts carry hard deadlines and automatic interest penalties (14 days for a proper progress payment request, 30 days after approval for retainage release, 7 days to flow payment down to subcontractors). Most states have private prompt-payment statutes with their own deadlines and penalties, so a hold recommendation should be paired with a written statement of what is disputed and why, on the timeline the statute allows.
- **Do not hard-code.** The number of days a draw cycle should take, the acceptable percent-complete variance, the share of a draw that may be stored materials, the soft-cost backup threshold, retainage percentage, and whether a specific waiver form is sufficient are all local, contractual, or lender-policy questions.
