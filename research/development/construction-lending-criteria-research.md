# Construction Lending Criteria Research

## Purpose

- Supports `knowledge/construction-lending-criteria.md`
- Also supports the Development and Construction pack skills that size or test construction debt, including `skills/development/construction-loan-sizing-and-structure.md`, `skills/development/construction-draw-and-cost-to-complete-reviewer.md`, and `skills/development/development-ic-memo-writer.md`
- Intended users: developers, development managers, owner representatives, construction lenders, and equity partners evaluating U.S. ground-up and heavy redevelopment debt

## U.S.-Only Assumptions

- Geography: United States
- Lender universe: U.S. banks and savings institutions supervised by the OCC, Federal Reserve, and FDIC; FHA/HUD-insured multifamily lenders; life companies, debt funds, and other non-bank construction lenders
- Deal type: ground-up construction, land development, and substantial rehabilitation of income-producing commercial and multifamily real estate; excludes stabilized-asset permanent financing, which the multifamily, industrial, office, and retail packs cover
- Legal and market assumptions: bank supervisory guidance and the regulatory capital rule set the constraint floor for regulated construction lenders; loan documents, lien law, and mechanics lien priority are state-specific and require counsel
- Educational decision support only; not legal, tax, investment, accounting, or financing advice

## Key Findings

### Sizing constraints for regulated construction lenders

- The Interagency Guidelines for Real Estate Lending Policies set supervisory LTV limits applied to the collateral: raw land 65%, land development 75%, construction of commercial, multifamily, and other nonresidential property 80%, 1-4 family residential construction 85%, and improved property 85%. Owner-occupied 1-4 family and home equity have no stated limit, but loans at or above 90% LTV at origination should carry mortgage insurance or readily marketable collateral.
- For a loan funding multiple phases of one project, the applicable limit is the limit for the final phase funded by the loan.
- Loans exceeding the supervisory LTV limits are not prohibited, but the aggregate of all such loans should not exceed 100% of total capital, and the portion for commercial, agricultural, multifamily, and other non-1-4 family property should not exceed 30% of total capital.
- The OCC handbook directs banks to evaluate loan-to-cost alongside LTV, because LTC is what confirms the borrower has contributed enough equity to absorb cost overruns and leasing or sales shortfalls.
- OCC Bulletin 2006-46 sets two concentration screens for further supervisory analysis: construction, land development, and other land loans at or above 100% of total risk-based capital, and total non-owner-occupied CRE at or above 300% of total risk-based capital combined with 50% or greater growth over the prior 36 months. These are screens, not caps, but they explain why bank construction appetite is often constrained by the lender's balance sheet rather than the project.

### Equity, contributed capital, and HVCRE

- The OCC handbook states that bank policy should require equity to be contributed before construction loan disbursements begin, and that deferred developer profit, unearned developer fees, incurred overhead, and interest or holding fees paid or accrued on contributed land are generally not equity.
- Acceptable equity examples cited include cash, marketable securities, land purchased with cash, and initial soft costs paid up front by the developer such as architect, engineering, and permit fees.
- Under the December 2019 HVCRE final rule, effective April 1, 2020, an ADC credit facility carries a 150% risk weight under the standardized approach unless it fits an exclusion. The commercial real property exclusion requires four things: LTV at or below the applicable interagency supervisory LTV ratio; borrower-contributed capital of at least 15% of the property's appraised "as completed" value; that capital contributed before the bank advances funds, other than nominal sums securing the lien; and a contractual requirement that the capital remain in the project until the exposure can be reclassified as non-HVCRE.
- Contributed capital may be cash, unencumbered readily marketable assets, development expenses paid out of pocket before the bank advances funds, and contributed real property or improvements valued at appraised value under FIRREA section 1110 standards, reduced by liens securing the exposure. Borrowed funds may count only if they are not derived from, related to, or encumbering the project or contributed collateral. Contributed real property must be directly related to the project; unrelated real estate does not count.
- For a multi-phase project, each financed phase generally needs its own "as completed" appraisal or evaluation to be treated as a separate project for the 15% test.
- Reclassification to non-HVCRE requires substantial completion of the development or construction and cash flow sufficient to support debt service and expenses under the bank's permanent-financing underwriting criteria. Material modifications, including removing part of the 15% capital, can put a loan back into HVCRE.
- ADC loans originated before January 1, 2015 are not HVCRE exposures.

### Interest reserve and carry

- The OCC handbook treats the interest reserve as a budget line item that should fund interest through anticipated completion and lease-up, sale, or occupancy, and warns that "the presence of an interest reserve may not accurately reflect a borrower's ability to pay."
- Sizing considerations named: reasonableness of development assumptions including interest-rate changes, draw and paydown timing, and completion and absorption time; whether the borrower has enough equity for the bank to fund interest within LTC and LTV limits if the borrower does not; and that interest reserves are generally not appropriate for stabilized properties or speculative raw land.
- During lease-up, project cash flow should pay interest before the reserve is drawn, and once cash flow covers interest, further reserve draws should stop.
- Replenishing or "repacking" a depleted reserve with additional debt is called out as a red flag for credit deterioration; supporting it appropriately calls for a new appraisal or evaluation and a fresh feasibility assessment.
- The FDIC examination module asks whether extensions or increases in interest reserves are supported by a realistic assessment of continued feasibility, completion timeframe, and guarantor capacity, and whether controls prevent unrelated lines of credit to the developer from being used to keep construction loans current.
- Practitioner commentary distinguishes the interest reserve from a separate carry reserve covering operating expenses, taxes, and insurance during lease-up.

### Guaranties and recourse posture

- The OCC handbook notes guaranties may be limited to interest only, construction completion only, partial principal, stepped-down amounts, or released during the term as conditions are met, and that the bank should monitor achievement of those conditions before release. A guarantor's unpledged assets are not a substitute for project equity, and guarantor liquidity should be verified.
- Non-recourse loans typically carry carve-out guaranties covering fraud, misrepresentation, voluntary bankruptcy, environmental issues, unapproved liens, waste, prohibited transfers, and diversion of funds, with some carve-outs triggering full recourse.
- Paul, Weiss describes five common guaranty types: non-recourse carve-out, environmental indemnity, repayment, carry, and completion. A completion guaranty covers lien-free completion by an outside date substantially per lender-approved plans, with guarantor liability capped at the amount needed to complete less undisbursed loan proceeds and balancing reserves, and is generally released when the project is complete and the statutory mechanics lien filing period has expired. A carry guaranty covers operating and maintenance costs during construction and lease-up and usually releases when the property meets a stipulated debt service coverage or debt yield test. Repayment guaranties commonly burn off or reduce as the property hits agreed performance thresholds such as a DSCR.
- Practitioner commentary indicates non-recourse construction execution is available at materially lower leverage and higher pricing than recourse bank execution.

### In-balance, draws, retainage, and cost to complete

- The OCC handbook expects banks to confirm the budget "remains in balance with sufficient funds available to fund completion" and that construction conforms to schedule, with architect or engineer inspection reports supporting each draw and periodic bank or third-party site inspections.
- Monthly monitoring should cover work completed, costs to date, costs to complete, construction deadlines, and remaining loan funds. Change orders should be reviewed, approved, documented, and reflected in the budget; a high change-order count signals planning, design, or construction problems.
- Contingency allowances typically run 5% to 10% of the overall budget, and overruns from poor projections or management are ordinarily covered by the borrower rather than by drawing contingency.
- Developer fee, distinct from developer profit, typically does not exceed 4% of project cost and is often deferred or paid on percentage of completion. General conditions should not be fully funded up front.
- Commercial construction loans generally use a progress payment plan under which the bank retains 10% to 20% of each payment to cover overruns or unpaid suppliers and subcontractors. The final draw releases the holdback only after lien waivers or releases from contractors, subcontractors, and suppliers, a final inspection confirming completion to specification, and a certificate of occupancy.
- The lender's title policy should be updated with each draw to confirm no outstanding liens, which matters most in jurisdictions where mechanics liens can prime the bank lien.
- The FDIC module expects disbursements advanced on a prearranged plan, made only after review of written inspection reports, lien searches, and title updates, checked against original cost estimates and prior disbursements, supported by receipted bills, and reviewed by someone who did not grant the loan. It also flags "front loading," where a builder overstates early-stage cost, as a control failure that leaves insufficient funds to complete on default.
- Payment and performance bonds and title insurance are named mitigants; a fixed-price contract with the contractor mitigates overrun risk, and where borrower and contractor are related the handbook says the contract should be cost plus a fee with a guaranteed maximum price.

### Tenor, extension, mini-perm, and takeout

- The OCC handbook says the appropriate construction tenor is based on the time needed for construction plus stabilization or sale, and would not be shorter than that required for completion. Extension option length should be consistent with expected construction time plus projected absorption. A bank may structure construction financing that converts to bridge financing for the stabilization period; bridge loans are usually written for up to three years.
- Take-out commitments may be standby or forward. The handbook cautions that such commitments "may mitigate little of the risk" the construction lender assumes, because they require completion and are usually conditioned on performance criteria such as lease-up to break even or better at minimum rental rates, and that underwriting should analyze the risk that the take-out is not funded.
- The FDIC module expects the construction lender to test take-out validity through counsel review, review of the permanent lender's financial statements, a tri-party buy and sell agreement executed before the construction loan closes, and an automatic completion-date extension clause for delays beyond the builder's control.
- HUD Mortgagee Letter 2025-03 sets FHA Section 221(d)(4) new construction and substantial rehabilitation sizing at the lesser of the requested amount, statutory limits, the amount supportable by DSCR, and the amount supportable by the loan ratio, with market rate at 87% LTV/LTC and 1.15 DSCR (7% vacancy factor), affordable LIHTC with rent advantage at 90% and 1.11 (5% vacancy), and properties with 90% or greater rental assistance at 90% and 1.11 (3% vacancy).

### Dated market context

- FDIC Quarterly Banking Profile, Q2 2026: bank construction and development loan balances were $453.5 billion, down 3.4% from Q2 2025; the C&D noncurrent rate was 0.99% and 30-89 day past due was 0.35%.
- Federal Reserve July 2026 SLOOS (Q2 2026 reference quarter): standards for construction and land development loans were "basically unchanged on net," a moderate net share of banks reported weaker CLD demand, and a significant net share reported standards at the tighter end of their historical range.
- CBRE, August 3, 2026: the Lending Momentum Index was 1.0 in Q2 2026, down from a five-year high of 1.5 in Q1 2026 and above 1.3 a year earlier; average commercial spreads were 204 bps and multifamily 162 bps; average LTV was 59.6% commercial and 63.3% multifamily; among non-agency closings, alternative lenders held 38%, banks 30%, life companies 21%, and CMBS 11%.
- MBA, August 6, 2026: Q2 2026 commercial and multifamily originations rose 16% year over year and 12% from Q1 2026, with depository volume up 61% year over year.

## Benchmark and Formula Decisions

Suitable as repo defaults because they are traceable to primary regulation or supervisory handbooks:

- Supervisory LTV limits by collateral category (65% raw land, 75% land development, 80% commercial/multifamily/nonresidential construction, 85% 1-4 family construction, 85% improved property), plus the 100%-of-capital and 30%-of-capital aggregate excess-LTV limits.
- The HVCRE exclusion test: LTV at or below the applicable supervisory limit, 15% contributed capital measured against appraised as-completed value, contributed before first advance, contractually locked in until reclassification.
- Retainage of 10% to 20% of each progress payment on commercial construction loans, released at final draw against lien waivers, final inspection, and certificate of occupancy.
- Construction contingency of 5% to 10% of the overall budget, and developer fee generally not exceeding 4% of project cost.
- Interest reserve sized to fund interest through anticipated completion and lease-up, with project cash flow applied to interest before reserve draws once lease-up begins.
- In-balance / cost-to-complete as a continuing condition of each draw, tested from monthly costs to date, costs to complete, and remaining loan funds.
- FHA Section 221(d)(4) sizing criteria as published in Mortgagee Letter 2025-03, cited as a program standard rather than a market norm.

Case-by-case only, stated as such wherever used:

- Actual LTC caps, stabilized DSCR and debt yield thresholds, and stabilized-LTV takeout tests. No primary source reviewed publishes a universal number; the OCC handbook requires banks to set these in policy but does not prescribe levels. Practitioner sources circulating figures such as a 1.25x stabilized DSCR or a 10%-plus stabilized debt yield were not corroborated by any primary or institutional source in this session and are not adopted as defaults.
- Coupon, spread, fee, and extension-fee levels. Directional market context only, dated to the source.
- Recourse percentages and burn-off thresholds. These are negotiated and lender-specific; the sources support the structures and release triggers, not the levels.
- Preleasing and presale minimums. The OCC handbook and FDIC module both expect a policy minimum but neither prescribes one, and the handbook warns presales may not reliably predict actual sales.

Formulas carried into the knowledge base:

- Yield on cost equals stabilized net operating income divided by total project cost; the spread between that and the market exit capitalization rate is the development margin. The OCC handbook supports the underlying discipline by requiring cost and NOI-supported value to be considered together and treating cost approaching or exceeding expected value as a feasibility failure, but the specific spread convention is a market practice, not a regulatory threshold.
- In-balance test: remaining loan commitment plus unfunded borrower equity plus remaining contingency must be at least cost to complete plus remaining interest and carry.
- Loan amount is the lowest of the LTC test, the as-complete LTV test, the as-stabilized LTV test, the stabilized DSCR test, and the stabilized debt yield test.

## Conflicting Source Resolution

- Practitioner articles and lender marketing pages state specific construction LTC and stabilized DSCR levels. Regulatory and supervisory sources do not. Primary sources took priority: the knowledge base carries the regulatory constraints as defaults and treats leverage and coverage levels as lender-specific and quote-driven.
- An initial automated read of the HVCRE final rule reported a fixed 65% LTV requirement and a January 1, 2015 effective date. The rule text itself says the exclusion requires LTV at or below "the applicable supervisory loan-to-value ratio in the interagency real estate lending guidelines," which varies by collateral category, and sets the final rule's effective date at April 1, 2020, with pre-January 1, 2015 loans exempt. The rule text controls.
- The pre-2018 HVCRE regime restricted counting appreciated land value as contributed capital. The 2019 final rule permits contributed real property or improvements at appraised value under FIRREA standards, net of liens, provided the property is directly related to the project. The final rule controls for loans subject to it; older loans follow the regime in place when made.
- CBRE reports average LTV falling while noting lenders competing on price rather than leverage; MBA reports origination volume rising sharply. These are not in conflict: volume recovery and leverage discipline coexist. Both are reported as dated market context, not as sizing inputs.

## Edge Cases and Red Flags

- Non-bank construction lenders (debt funds, life companies, private credit) are not bound by the supervisory LTV limits or HVCRE capital treatment. Applying bank constraints to a debt-fund quote will misstate available proceeds; applying debt-fund leverage to a bank quote will misstate feasibility.
- Mechanics lien priority is state-specific. In jurisdictions where mechanics liens can prime the mortgage, title endorsement at each draw and disciplined lien-waiver collection matter more, and the analysis should say so rather than assume a national rule.
- Land contributed at appreciated value can satisfy the HVCRE 15% test while representing little cash at risk. Read cash-equity and contributed-capital tests separately.
- Phased projects: a phase without its own as-completed appraisal or evaluation may not qualify as a separate project for the 15% test, which can pull an otherwise conforming phase loan into HVCRE.
- A depleted interest reserve refunded with new loan proceeds is a credit-deterioration signal in the OCC handbook, not a routine budget amendment.
- Front-loaded draw schedules leave insufficient funds to complete on default; percentage-complete verification by an independent inspector is the control that catches it.
- Take-out and forward commitments carry conditions. A commitment that will not fund because a lease-up or rent condition fails is not a takeout.
- Bank concentration position can be the binding constraint. A sponsor with a strong project can still be declined because the lender is near the 100% construction or 300% total CRE screen.
- Guaranty burn-off conditions that are never formally tested leave guarantors liable and sponsors mispricing their exposure; the OCC handbook expects the bank to monitor and assess the conditions before releasing a guarantor.
- Loan modifications that increase the loan amount, change project size or scope, or release part of the 15% capital can re-trigger HVCRE treatment.
