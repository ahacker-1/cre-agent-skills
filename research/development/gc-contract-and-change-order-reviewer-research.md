# GC Contract and Change Order Reviewer Research

## Purpose

- Supports `skills/development/gc-contract-and-change-order-reviewer.md`
- Pairs with the existing knowledge base `knowledge/construction-contracts-and-draw-controls.md` and reuses its terminology and thresholds without restating them
- Intended users: developers, development managers, owner representatives, construction lenders and loan administrators, and equity partners who must read an executed or proposed general contractor or construction manager agreement, or price and dispose of an individual change order

## U.S.-Only Assumptions

- Geography: United States. Retainage caps, prompt-payment rules, lien and waiver formalities, and enforceability of liquidated damages are state law questions, and federal projects add a Miller Act and FAR overlay that private projects do not carry.
- Deal type: U.S. ground-up development and heavy redevelopment built under an owner-contractor or owner-construction manager agreement, usually financed with a construction loan and administered through monthly applications for payment.
- Form assumptions: the AIA A-series is the most common U.S. private-project family and is used here as the reference vocabulary (A101 stipulated sum, A102 cost plus fee with a GMP, A133 construction manager as constructor with a GMP, A201 general conditions). ConsensusDocs, EJCDC, DBIA, and owner-drafted forms allocate the same risks under different numbering. Critically, almost every AIA form in the field is modified: the primary A201 copy read for this research has entire subsections deleted, replaced, or rewritten, so section numbers locate an issue but never settle it.
- Legal assumptions: this is educational issue spotting only. Contract interpretation, lien and waiver sufficiency, enforceability of liquidated damages and indemnity, and insurance adequacy require counsel and a licensed broker in the project state. Nothing here is legal, tax, accounting, investment, or financing advice.

## Key Findings

### The form tells you where the risk sits, the modifications tell you where it moved

- A101 fixes a stipulated sum; A102 and A133 reimburse the cost of the work plus a fee up to a guaranteed maximum. AIA's GMP guidance states that where cost of the work plus fee exceeds the GMP and the overrun is not covered by an approved change or contract adjustment, the contractor is generally responsible for the additional cost.
- A133-2019 splits the engagement into a preconstruction phase and a construction phase that may overlap for fast-tracking. The GMP Amendment (Exhibit A) is the operative document: per AIA's instructions it must state the GMP amount, an itemized breakdown, the assumptions and clarifications the GMP rests on, a schedule of values with unit prices and quantity limitations, the alternates included and the conditions for accepting alternates later, and the substantial completion date. The assumptions and clarifications page is the single highest-yield page in a GMP review because it is where scope that looks priced is quietly excluded.
- Reading the primary A201 copy alongside its own marked-up "changes" pages shows the practical point directly: this owner deleted A201 7.3.4 and 7.3.5 entirely, deleted the standard 13.2.2 lender-assignment provision and substituted an antitrust assignment, rewrote 15.1.5 to waive extended and unabsorbed overhead, and added a 15% total markup ceiling on change orders. Section numbers are a map, not an answer.

### Money mechanics

- Allowances (A201 3.8): the contractor includes stated allowances in the contract sum; the allowance itself covers the cost of materials and equipment delivered at the site plus required taxes less trade discounts, while unloading, handling, labor, installation, overhead, and profit sit in the contract sum and not in the allowance. When actual cost differs from the allowance the contract sum is adjusted by change order reflecting both the materials delta and the change in the contractor's other costs. Section 3.8.3 obliges the owner to select allowance items with reasonable promptness, which makes owner indecision a schedule and cost exposure rather than a neutral act.
- Contingency: AIA's guidance defines contingency as a predetermined amount or percentage held for unpredictable change, cites 5%-10% for construction contingency and 5%-10% of construction cost for design contingency, and states that in CM/GC delivery any remaining contractor contingency is the owner's. It also states that contingency should not be used to accomplish original scope or to substitute for proper initial budgeting.
- Unit prices: A133's GMP Amendment carries unit prices together with the quantity limitations they assume. A unit price without a stated quantity band is a rate the owner cannot control, because the exposure is the quantity, not the rate.
- Change order pricing: A201 7.2.5 in the copy reviewed limits recoverable change cost to actual labor at pre-approved composite rates, actual substantiated material cost, rental of tools and equipment above a $1,000 initial-cost threshold, actual bond cost, and subcontractor cost, with all other change-processing effort (estimating, coordination, layout, as-builts, small tools, general expenses) absorbed into overhead and profit. That structure is an owner drafting model worth copying; the specific percentages in it (15% combined markup ceiling, 7.5% cap on the GC's markup of subcontracted change work, a declining scale negotiated above $30,000, credit overhead and profit negotiated on deducts above $15,000) are that owner's supplementary conditions, not AIA defaults and not a market standard.
- A201 7.3.8 requires the credit for a deletion to be actual net cost, and where a change involves both additions and credits the overhead and profit allowance is computed on the net increase. Without that sentence, bundled adds and deducts let markup ride on the gross.

### Changes: entitlement, pricing, and time are three separate questions

- Instruments: a change order under 7.2.1 requires agreement of owner, contractor, and architect on the change, the sum adjustment, and the time adjustment (AIA form G701). A construction change directive under 7.3.1 is signed by owner and architect only and directs the change before agreement on sum or time (AIA form G714). Under 7.3.6 the contractor must promptly proceed on receipt, and under 7.3.9 may request interim payment while total cost is still being determined, with the architect making an interim determination that adjusts the contract sum subject to either party's right to assert a claim. Section 7.4 covers minor changes with no adjustment; if the contractor performs a minor change without first giving notice that it affects sum or time, the contractor waives the adjustment. Section 7.1.4 bars changes without prior authorization except in emergency.
- Notice is the entitlement gate. Under 15.1.3.1 as modified in the copy reviewed, contractor claims must be initiated within 21 days after the event or after the claimant first recognizes the condition, whichever is later. Under 15.1.5 notice must be given before executing the work that is the subject of a cost claim, except for an emergency under 10.4. A time claim under 15.1.6.1 must include an estimate of cost and of the probable effect of the delay, and a weather claim under 15.1.6.2 must be documented with data showing conditions were abnormal, could not reasonably have been anticipated, and adversely affected the schedule.
- HUD's MAP Guide supplies a disciplined entitlement taxonomy that translates cleanly to private deals: changes are approved only when they are necessary, a betterment, or an equivalent. Necessary changes arise from latent conditions differing from the construction documents, code changes after closing or firm commitment, the architect's errors or omissions, or damage to completed construction. Betterment changes must increase net income, reduce long-term operating or maintenance expense, or otherwise enhance the security. Equivalent changes substitute an unavailable item at equal or better utility, or reduce price at equal or better utility. The face of the change order must show its classification, whether it qualifies for payment from contingency, and whether it results from error, omission, or negligence of the architect, contractor, or borrower.
- HUD requires all changes to be approved in writing by lender and HUD before they are made, and treats work performed in anticipation of a future change order as non-compliant, with future insured advances potentially affected until corrected. It also requires written surety approval before approving any change order granting a time extension, except where completion assurance is cash or a letter of credit, and requires the borrower to escrow funds for additive change orders once aggregated additive changes reach a $5,000 increase in the contract price.

### Time, completion, and delay damages

- Liquidated damages enforceability turns on a two-prong test: damages were difficult to ascertain when the contract was signed, and the stipulated amount is a reasonable estimate of probable damages or reasonably proportionate to actual damages. Courts give substantial weight to the parties' own stipulation that damages were hard to estimate, and a challenger must show the amount is shockingly disproportionate. The commentary reviewed reports courts upholding liquidated damages at twice actual damages.
- The concurrent-delay trap is the more common failure. Under North Carolina's non-apportionment rule as described, liquidated damages are unenforceable if the party benefiting from them was itself responsible for part of the delay, unless the contract contains an apportionment clause. The drafting response is explicit: stipulate difficulty of ascertainment, document the good-faith estimate, and include an apportionment mechanism.
- A201 15.1.7 waives consequential damages mutually, including the owner's rental expense, loss of use, income, profit, financing, business, and reputation, and expressly preserves liquidated damages. For a development deal that is exactly backwards from the owner's intuition: the delay damages the owner actually fears are the ones waived, so the liquidated damages rate is not a cap on a remedy, it is the entire remedy.

### Payment, retainage, and closeout

- A201 9.5.1 gives seven grounds to withhold certification: defective work not remedied; third-party claims filed or reasonable evidence of probable filing absent acceptable security; failure to pay subcontractors or suppliers; reasonable evidence that the work cannot be completed for the unpaid balance; damage to the owner or a separate contractor; reasonable evidence the work will not finish within the contract time and the unpaid balance would not cover actual or liquidated damages for the anticipated delay; and failure to carry out the work per the contract documents. Ground four and ground six are the cost-to-complete and schedule tests written into the payment article.
- Retainage is state law before it is contract drafting. California Civil Code 8811, for contracts entered into on or after 2026-01-01, caps retention at 5% of the payment and 5% of the contract price on a private work, with a bond-failure exception and an exception for residential projects that are not mixed-use and do not exceed four stories, and awards attorney fees to the prevailing party in an enforcement action. Texas Property Code 53.101 runs the other way, requiring the owner to reserve 10% of the contract price or of the value of work done during the work and for 30 days after completion. HUD requires 10% holdback until 50% completion, then permits 5% to 75% and 2.5% to Final Endorsement, conditioned on no identity of interest above a 5% equity interest, prior written surety consent, and no open questions about performance.
- HUD's final-advance rules are a useful closeout checklist for any owner: before releasing the contractor's retainage, hold 150% of the cost estimate for items of delayed completion, any owed or contested lien amounts, the lesser of liquidated or actual damages computed at cost certification, and the net effect of all change orders where that effect is negative.
- Lien waivers are statutory in a number of states. California prescribes four forms (Civil Code 8132 conditional on progress payment, 8134 unconditional on progress payment, 8136 conditional on final payment, 8138 unconditional on final payment); a conditional waiver binds only on actual payment while an unconditional waiver binds on signature.

### Risk transfer: bonds, insurance, warranty, termination, lender provisions

- Bonds: FAR 28.102-1 requires performance and payment bonds on federal construction contracts above $150,000 and two or more alternative payment protections above $35,000 up to $150,000, furnished before notice to proceed. On private work bonding is a negotiated credit decision. HUD requires the general contractor to provide assurance of completion as a combined performance and payment bond, cash, or a letter of credit in a HUD-approved amount, which is the cleanest statement of the three-way choice an owner actually has.
- Insurance: the A201-2017 family moved detailed insurance requirements out of the general conditions and into an exhibit to the owner-contractor agreement, so a review that reads only A201 Article 11 will miss the limits. In the copy reviewed the owner purchases builder's risk in at least the amount of the initial contract sum, off-site and transit coverage is added only on contractor request with the owner's written approval, and the contractor bears loss within the deductible up to a stated amount. A wrap-up consolidates general liability and workers compensation for all enrolled contractors: an OCIP when the owner sponsors it, a CCIP when the contractor does, with a PCIP variant jointly sponsored. Under a wrap, contractors remain responsible for off-site work, excluded operations, and deductibles or retentions, which is where enrollment disputes start.
- Warranty and correction: A201 12.2.2.1 gives a one-year correction period running from substantial completion, extended for work first performed after that date and not extended by corrective work. A201 9.8.6 assigns warranties and guarantees to the owner at substantial completion. The one-year correction period is not the warranty and not the statute of limitations; 12.2.5 says so expressly.
- Termination and dispute resolution: A201 14.1 lets the contractor terminate after a 30-day stoppage in defined circumstances on seven days' notice; 14.2 covers owner termination for cause; 14.3 suspension for convenience with sum and time adjustment; 14.4 termination for owner's convenience with payment for work performed. Article 15 routes claims through an initial decision maker who under 1.1.8 must be impartial; the AIA summary notes 30 days to demand mediation and 30 days to file, with 15.3.3 allowing a party to demand binding dispute resolution if mediation stalls.
- Lender provisions: standard A201-2017 section 13.2.2 permits the owner to assign the contract to a lender providing construction financing without the contractor's consent, provided the lender assumes the owner's rights and obligations, and obliges the contractor to execute the consents reasonably required. The owner-modified copy reviewed deleted that provision. Section 5.4 gives the owner a contingent assignment of subcontracts effective only after termination for cause and only for subcontracts the owner accepts, subject to the prior rights of the surety. Together these are the two provisions a construction lender needs and the two most often edited away.

## Benchmark and Formula Decisions

Suitable as repo defaults:

- Structural review order: form and delivery method, then price and what is excluded, then time and delay damages, then payment and retainage, then risk transfer, then lender provisions. Price cannot be assessed before the exclusions are read.
- The three-question change order test: entitlement (is this a compensable change under the contract, and did the contractor give the notice the contract requires), pricing (is it priced by the contract's own cost and markup rules), and time (is a time extension requested, substantiated by schedule analysis, and is it compensable or excusable-only).
- Entitlement classification adapted from HUD: necessary, betterment, or equivalent, plus a fourth private-deal category the AIA framework supplies - contractor-risk items (scope already within the contract documents, coordination failures, unauthorized work under 7.1.4, and changes performed without the notice that 7.4 and 15.1.3 require).
- Contingency benchmarks: 5%-10% construction contingency and 5%-10% of construction cost for design contingency, cited to AIA, treated as directional starting points.
- Contract-based withholding grounds: use the seven grounds in A201 9.5.1 as the checklist rather than an ad hoc list.
- Closeout holdback checklist adapted from HUD: 150% of the estimate for delayed-completion items, contested lien amounts, the lesser of liquidated or actual damages, and the net of negative change orders.

Case-by-case only, never a repo default:

- Contractor fee percentage, general conditions as a percentage of cost, change order markup caps, savings-split percentages, and liquidated damages dollar rates. These are negotiated, market-specific, and project-specific. The 15% and 7.5% markup ceilings and the $30,000 and $15,000 thresholds found in the primary A201 copy are one public owner's supplementary conditions and must be presented that way.
- Retainage percentage. Governed by state statute in both directions (5% cap in California for post-2026 private contracts, 10% owner reserve in Texas) and by lender program rules where they apply. No national default.
- Bond requirement on private work. Federal thresholds do not transfer.

## Conflicting Source Resolution

- Retainage: California and Texas point in opposite directions, and HUD's 10% is higher than California's private cap. There is no conflict to resolve because each governs different work; the resolution rule for the skill is that the governing state's statute and the lender program control, and the contract's number is tested against them rather than the reverse.
- Contingency percentages: AIA's 5%-10% ranges are association guidance, not a standard-form requirement, and practitioner sources vary. The skill cites AIA and labels the range directional.
- Change order markup: the primary A201 copy caps markup at percentages that other owners do not use. Where a modified form and a standards-body description conflict, the executed document controls for that project, and the standards-body text is used only to identify what was changed.
- Liquidated damages: enforceability doctrine is state-specific and the non-apportionment rule described is North Carolina's. The skill states the two-prong test and the apportionment problem as issues to route to counsel, not as a national rule.

## Edge Cases and Red Flags

- Modified forms. The most reliable finding in this research is that AIA numbering survives while the text does not. Any review that cites a section number without quoting the executed language is unreliable.
- Design-build, EPC, and owner-drafted forms. The A-series vocabulary maps imperfectly; the risk questions still apply but the section map does not.
- Identity of interest between the developer and the contractor. HUD treats any equity interest above 5% as disqualifying for retainage reduction and restricts release until Final Endorsement. On private deals with a related-party GC, pricing, buyout, contingency draws, and retainage release all need arm's-length testing and equity partner disclosure.
- Cost-plus with no GMP (A134-type). There is no cap; the review shifts entirely to cost controls, audit rights, and the definition of the cost of the work.
- GMP set on incomplete design. AIA's guidance notes that setting the GMP too early inflates contingency, which raises the maximum price itself. Design completeness at GMP is a number to state, not a feeling.
- Federal, state, or locally funded work. Miller Act and little Miller Act bonding, Davis-Bacon, and prompt-payment overlays change the analysis and are outside the private-deal defaults.
- Public owner supplementary conditions used as a private-deal template. Waivers of extended and unabsorbed overhead, sole-discretion consent rights, and hard markup caps may not survive negotiation with a private GC and may not be enforceable everywhere.

## Open Questions

- Enforceability of no-damages-for-delay clauses, indemnity, and additional-insured obligations varies materially by state and was not surveyed here; each is a counsel referral.
- Contractor default insurance (subguard) as an alternative to subcontractor bonding was not researched in this pass and is deferred.
- No current national dataset on change order frequency or magnitude by product type was retrieved this session, so the skill publishes no benchmark for change order percentage of contract sum.
- Prompt-payment statutes and their interaction with contractual pay-when-paid and pay-if-paid clauses were not surveyed and are deferred to a future pass.
