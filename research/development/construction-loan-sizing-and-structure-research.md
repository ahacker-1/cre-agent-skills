# Construction Loan Sizing and Structure Research

## Purpose

- Supports `skills/development/construction-loan-sizing-and-structure.md`
- Pairs with the pack knowledge bases `knowledge/construction-lending-criteria.md` and `knowledge/development-benchmarks.md`, and shares source ground with `research/development/construction-lending-criteria-research.md`
- Intended users: developers, development managers, owner representatives, construction lenders, and equity partners sizing and structuring U.S. ground-up and heavy redevelopment debt

## U.S.-Only Assumptions

- Geography: United States
- Deal type: ground-up construction, land development, and substantial rehabilitation of income-producing commercial and multifamily real estate. Stabilized-asset permanent financing is out of scope and belongs to the multifamily, industrial, office, and retail packs
- Lender universe: U.S. banks and savings institutions supervised by the OCC, Federal Reserve, and FDIC; FHA/HUD-insured multifamily lenders under the MAP program; life insurance companies, debt funds, and other non-bank construction lenders
- Legal and market assumptions: bank supervisory guidance and the regulatory capital rule set the constraint floor for regulated lenders only. Non-bank lenders are not bound by supervisory LTV limits or HVCRE capital treatment. Loan documents, guaranty enforceability, mechanics lien priority, and lien waiver form are state-specific and require counsel
- Educational decision support only; not legal, tax, investment, accounting, or financing advice

## Key Findings

### Proceeds are set by the tightest of several tests

- The OCC handbook directs banks to establish loan limits as a maximum percentage of cost (LTC) as well as market value (LTV), because "Evaluating LTC in addition to LTV helps ensure that the borrower contributes sufficient equity," which provides both continued economic interest and cushion for cost overruns and leasing or sales shortfalls. The handbook prescribes no LTC level; the cap is bank policy and quote-driven.
- The Interagency Guidelines set supervisory LTV limits applied to the collateral: raw land 65%, land development 75%, construction of commercial, multifamily, and other nonresidential property 80%, 1-4 family residential construction 85%, improved property 85%. Owner-occupied 1-4 family and home equity carry no stated limit, but loans at or above 90% LTV at origination should have mortgage insurance or readily marketable collateral.
- Loans above the supervisory limits are permitted but the aggregate of all such loans should not exceed 100% of total capital, and the portion for commercial, agricultural, multifamily, and other non-1-4 family property should not exceed 30% of total capital. For a loan funding multiple phases, the applicable limit is the limit for the final phase funded by the loan, and disbursements should not exceed actual development or construction outlays.
- The handbook defines DSCR as NOI divided by annual debt service and debt yield as NOI divided by the loan amount, noting that debt yield "provides a measurement of risk that is independent of the interest rate, amortization period, and capitalization rate" and that debt yields reflecting normalized or higher-rate levels can be used to establish stressed loan amounts. Neither carries a prescribed threshold; both are named as appropriate financial covenants alongside LTV and LTC.
- The handbook requires construction appraisals to include an as-is current market value and a prospective market value, and defines the two prospective values used in sizing: the "as complete" value as of expected completion, and the "as stabilized" value as of the date the property is projected to achieve stabilized occupancy. Market values for proposed construction, partially leased or vacant buildings, and nonmarket lease terms must include analysis for appropriate deductions and discounts.

### HUD Section 221(d)(4) as the published reference lane

- Mortgagee Letter 2025-03 (2025-01-08) revised MAP Guide criteria. For 221(d)(4) new construction and substantial rehabilitation: market rate or LIHTC without rent advantage at 87% LTV/LTC and 1.15 DSCR with a 7% vacancy factor; affordable LIHTC with rent advantage to market at 90% and 1.11 with 5%; properties with 90% or greater units carrying rental assistance at 90% and 1.11 with 3%.
- The maximum loan is the lesser of the requested mortgage amount, the statutory limit, the amount supportable by the applicable DSCR, and the amount supportable by the applicable loan ratios. This lesser-of structure is the same logic conventional lenders apply, published.
- MAP Guide mechanics worth knowing as a structural reference point: the general contractor provides assurance of completion as a combined performance and payment bond, cash, or a letter of credit in a HUD-approved amount; the working capital escrow for new construction is 4% of the mortgage amount, split 2% construction contingency for overruns and change orders and 2% for excess soft costs and cost of putting the project in operation, and 2% for substantial rehabilitation; an initial operating deficit escrow is required on all new construction applications, is not mortgageable, and unused portions return to the borrower; the absorption period used to estimate market demand should not exceed 18 months from delivery of the first units; standard sustaining occupancy is 6 months; and BSPRA, where an identity of interest exists between borrower and general contractor and no paid builder's profit is in the mortgage, is 10% of specified cost lines exclusive of land and is credited against the borrower's required equity contribution.

### Equity, contributed capital, and HVCRE

- The OCC handbook states bank policy should require equity to be contributed before construction loan disbursements begin, and that allowing deferred equity "can significantly increase completion risk"; where deferred, the bank should be assured the equity is and will remain available. Deferred developer profit, unearned developer fees, incurred overhead, and interest or holding fees paid or accrued on contributed land "do not contribute to the value of the project and are generally not considered equity." Common acceptable equity examples are cash, marketable securities, land purchased with cash, and initial costs paid up front such as architect and engineering fees and permits. A guarantor's unpledged assets "should not be considered a substitute for project equity," and guarantor liquidity should be verified.
- The HVCRE final rule carries a 150% risk weight for an ADC exposure that does not qualify for an exclusion. The commercial real property exclusion has four conditions: LTV less than or equal to the applicable supervisory LTV ratio in the interagency guidelines; borrower-contributed capital of at least 15% of the real property's appraised "as completed" value; that capital contributed prior to the bank advancing funds, except nominal sums securing the lien; and the 15% contractually required to remain in the project until the loan can be reclassified.
- Reclassification to non-HVCRE requires substantial completion of the development or construction plus cash flow generated by the property covering debt service and expenses in accordance with the bank's loan underwriting standards for permanent financings; the agencies expect those standards to be "prudent, clear, and measurable."
- Contributed capital forms include cash, unencumbered readily marketable assets, development expenses paid out of pocket, and contributed real property or improvements.

### Interest reserve and carry

- The OCC handbook: an appropriate interest reserve "provides sufficient funds to pay interest through the project's anticipated completion and lease-up, sale, or occupancy," and "the presence of an interest reserve may not accurately reflect a borrower's ability to pay."
- Sizing considerations named: reasonableness of development assumptions including potential interest-rate changes, timing of expected disbursements and paydowns, and time required for completion and sale or lease-up; whether there is sufficient equity for the bank to fund interest within LTC and LTV limits if necessary, even where the borrower intends to pay interest from its own funds; and that interest reserves are generally not appropriate for stabilized properties or speculative raw land purchases.
- During lease-up, project cash flow is ordinarily applied to interest before the reserve, and once cash flow covers interest "no further draws on the reserve should be permitted to prevent the diversion of income that should be used to support the project."
- Repacking a depleted reserve with additional debt "is a red flag indicating possible credit deterioration"; supporting it appropriately calls for a new appraisal or evaluation and re-evaluation of project feasibility in the current market. FDIC examiners separately test whether extensions or increases in interest reserves are supported by a realistic assessment of continued feasibility, the completion timeframe, and guarantor capacity, and whether controls prevent unrelated borrowing facilities from being used to keep the loan current.
- The carry reserve is a distinct item. The practitioner source describes lender-established upfront reserves within the loan budget for interest and for day-to-day operations, taxes, and insurance, which may be reduced on stabilization if coverage thresholds are met.

### Guaranty package and recourse

- Paul Weiss identifies five commonly required guaranties: non-recourse carve-out, environmental indemnity, repayment, carry, and completion.
- Completion guaranty: guaranty of completion by a negotiated outside date, free and clear of liens, substantially per lender-approved plans. Remedies are lender completion at guarantor expense, guarantor completion, or liquidated damages. In all cases guarantor liability "is limited to the amount required ... to complete the project in accordance with the approved plans, less any undisbursed loan amounts (including any construction or 'loan balancing' reserves held by the lender)." Release comes when the project is complete and the statutory mechanics lien filing period has expired, and also on payment in full.
- Repayment guaranty: used particularly for construction loans and other non-stabilized assets, for all or a specified portion of the debt. Many provide for reduction or burn-off, or full release, on the property or borrower achieving specified thresholds such as an agreed DSCR, because "the need for a repayment guaranty diminishes as cash flow from the encumbered property improves." Foreclosure or deed in lieu does not release it.
- Carry guaranty: covers operating and maintenance costs such as insurance premiums, utility charges, and real estate taxes while cash flow is insufficient. Release on full repayment, and usually on the property reaching a stipulated performance level such as a DSCR or debt yield test; release on foreclosure or deed in lieu often carries a tail of thirty days to one year.
- Non-recourse carve-out guaranty: bifurcates acts into full-recourse triggers (such as unauthorized transfers and voluntary or collusive bankruptcy filings, with no damages proof required) and loss-recourse triggers (such as physical waste and misapplication of funds). Effectively performed on repayment in full; prospective release on completed foreclosure or acceptance of a deed in lieu, with continuing liability for facts existing on or before that date.
- Environmental indemnity: repayment or foreclosure does not generally release it; lenders often agree to a sunset one to three years after repayment or transfer, conditioned on a current clean environmental report and no outstanding claim.
- The OCC handbook expects the bank to monitor and assess achievement of stepped-down or release conditions before releasing a guarantor, and describes carve-out structures for nonrecourse loans in the same terms. The practitioner source frames the leverage tradeoff directionally: a recourse lender might offer 70% loan to cost where a non-recourse lender caps at 50%, at higher pricing. That is a 2018 characterization, not a current market quote.

### In-balance, draw controls, retainage, and the budget

- The OCC handbook: banks "typically confirm that the budget remains in balance with sufficient funds available to fund completion and construction conforms to the agreed schedule," and monitor so that "the borrower's request for funds is appropriate for the particular stage of development with adequate funds remaining for completion."
- Under a progress payment plan, normally used for commercial projects, "the bank normally retains, or holds back, 10 to 20 percent of each payment to cover project cost overruns or outstanding bills from suppliers or subcontractors." Draw requests should include waivers from subcontractors and suppliers; the bank disburses after independently confirming progress, less the holdback. Before the final draw and holdback release: all lien waivers or releases obtained, a final inspection confirming completion to building specifications, and a certificate of occupancy.
- Contingency allowances "vary based on the project's size or complexity but usually range between 5 and 10 percent of the overall budget." Cost overruns resulting from poor projections or management "would ordinarily be covered by the borrower rather than by a draw-down on the loan amount budgeted for contingencies."
- A developer fee, distinct from developer profit, compensates for project management and project-specific overhead, may be deferred or disbursed on percentage of completion, and "typically does not exceed 4 percent of the project cost." Interest or preferred returns to equity partners or subordinated debt holders, developer general corporate overhead, and selling costs funded from sales proceeds should not be in the construction budget. Developer profit should generally be funded by sales, by loan funds upon completion and lease-up, or by subsequent term financing.
- Cost overrun mitigation named: a fixed-price contract with the contractor, and where borrower and contractor are the same or related, "the contract should specify cost plus a fee with a guaranteed maximum price." Payment and performance bonds and title insurance are named mitigants; a contractor related to the borrower cannot generally be bonded.
- Front loading, where a builder deliberately overstates the cost of early-stage work, is the specific failure mode the controls exist to catch; undetected, "there will almost certainly be insufficient loan funds to complete construction if there is a default."
- FDIC examiner procedures add the operational detail: disbursements advanced on a prearranged plan, only after management reviews written inspection reports, requisite lien searches, and title updates; written authorization from contractor, borrower, inspector, and lending officer; reviewed by an employee with no part in granting the loan; compared to original cost estimates and checked against previous disbursements; made directly to subcontractors; supported by receipted bills. Inspections should be conducted on an irregular schedule and before draw requests, by qualified individuals rotated or independent of the lending function, with spot checks of the inspectors' work.

### Tenor, extension, mini-perm, and takeout

- The OCC handbook: appropriate tenor "is generally based on the time needed for construction and stabilization or sale and would not be shorter than that required for completion." A bank "may wish to provide construction financing that covers the expected construction period with the facility converting to bridge financing for the expected stabilization period," and extension option length "should be consistent with the expected construction time plus the projected absorption period." Bridge loans are usually written for a period of up to three years.
- Take-out commitments come as standby (back-up financing, with fee structure and rate often intended to dissuade exercise) or forward (a commitment to refinance on completion and, almost always, lease-up, frequently from a life insurance company, usually allowing a rate lock in advance of funding). The handbook's warning is direct: these commitments "may mitigate little of the risk that the lender assumes in making the construction loan," because they require completion of construction and are usually subject to performance criteria such as lease-up to break even or better at minimum rental rates. Underwriting "would ordinarily include analysis of the risk should the take-out commitment not be funded."
- FDIC examiners test take-out validity through counsel review for acceptability, review of the permanent lender's financial statements for capacity, completion of a tri-party buy and sell agreement before the construction loan closes, and whether the take-out agreement includes a clause providing an automatic extension of the completion date for delays beyond the builder's control.
- Preleasing or presale minimums are a common condition of commitment or funding, but the handbook cautions that presales may not be a reliable indicator because purchase commitments may not result in sales if values decline; banks analyze whether they are bona fide and whether meaningful deposits were collected.

## Benchmark and Formula Decisions

Suitable as repo defaults because they are published and traceable:

- Loan sizing formula: loan equals the minimum of the LTC result, as-complete LTV result, as-stabilized LTV result, stabilized DSCR result, and stabilized debt yield result. Sourced to the OCC handbook's LTC-plus-LTV instruction, its DSCR and debt yield definitions, and HUD's published lesser-of criteria.
- Supervisory LTV limits for regulated banks: 65% raw land, 75% land development, 80% commercial/multifamily/nonresidential construction, 85% 1-4 family residential construction, 85% improved property; multi-phase loans take the final-phase limit.
- HVCRE test: 15% of appraised as-completed value, contributed before first advance, contractually locked until reclassification, with LTV at or below the supervisory limit; 150% risk weight otherwise.
- HUD 221(d)(4) reference grid: 87% / 1.15 / 7% market rate; 90% / 1.11 / 5% LIHTC with rent advantage; 90% / 1.11 / 3% with 90% or greater rental assistance.
- In-balance test: remaining loan commitment plus unfunded borrower equity plus remaining contingency must be at least cost to complete plus remaining interest and carry.
- Retainage 10% to 20% of each progress payment; contingency 5% to 10% of the overall budget; developer fee typically not exceeding 4% of project cost.
- Guaranty structure: completion capped at cost to complete less undisbursed proceeds and balancing reserves, released on completion plus expiry of the mechanics lien filing period; repayment burn-off on agreed DSCR thresholds; carry release on a stipulated DSCR or debt yield with a possible post-foreclosure tail; environmental indemnity sunset one to three years post-repayment.
- Extension option length consistent with expected construction time plus projected absorption; bridge/mini-perm tenor up to three years.

Case-by-case only, not repo defaults:

- Any LTC percentage. No regulator publishes one for conventional construction debt; it is a lender policy number. The 70% recourse versus 50% non-recourse framing is one practitioner's 2018 characterization and is directional only.
- Any DSCR or debt yield threshold for conventional construction debt. Only the HUD program minimums are published.
- Interest reserve as a percent of loan. Size it by modeling the draw curve against the schedule, not by ratio.
- Guaranty burn-off percentages and step-down levels. The structures and triggers are sourced; the numbers are negotiated.
- Pricing, spreads, and fees, which move continuously.

## Conflicting Source Resolution

- Where supervisory guidance and practitioner commentary disagree on what a construction lender "typically" does, the supervisory source governs what a regulated lender must be able to defend, and the practitioner source is used only to describe negotiated market practice. Recourse posture is the clearest example: the OCC handbook does not require recourse, and the leverage-versus-recourse tradeoff comes only from practitioner commentary.
- The OCC handbook and the HVCRE final rule both address borrower equity but answer different questions. The handbook's equity discussion is an underwriting standard about completion risk and cost-overrun cushion; the HVCRE 15% test is a capital-treatment condition measured against as-completed appraised value. A deal can satisfy the HVCRE test through contributed real property while holding little new cash at risk, so the skill tests both separately rather than collapsing them.
- CBRE's Lending Momentum Index shows Q2 2026 origination momentum easing from Q1 while MBA reports Q2 2026 volume up 16% year over year. These are not in conflict: one is a quarter-over-quarter index of CBRE-brokered non-agency closings, the other a year-over-year volume survey. Both are cited with their basis rather than reconciled into a single narrative.
- Interest reserve treatment differs between the OCC handbook (a budget line item sized to carry the project, with warnings about masking) and the FDIC examination module (a set of tests applied to extensions and increases). They are complementary and both are used: the handbook for sizing, the module for what happens when the reserve runs short.

## Edge Cases and Red Flags

- Non-bank lenders. Debt funds, mortgage REITs, and other private credit lenders are not subject to supervisory LTV limits or HVCRE capital treatment. Do not apply those thresholds as constraints to a non-bank quote; apply them only to explain a bank's behavior and to compare lanes.
- Land contributed at appreciated value. It can satisfy the HVCRE 15% test while representing little new cash. Always show cash equity separately from total contributed capital.
- Phased projects. Each financed phase generally needs its own as-completed appraisal or evaluation to be treated separately for the 15% test, and the supervisory LTV limit follows the final phase funded by the loan. Phasing changes both tests at once.
- Related-party contractor. Where borrower and contractor are the same or affiliated, the OCC handbook expects cost plus a fee with a guaranteed maximum price rather than a fixed price, and such a contractor generally cannot be bonded. Route the contract terms to [GC Contract and Change Order Reviewer](skills/development/gc-contract-and-change-order-reviewer.md).
- Mechanics lien priority. Whether a mechanics lien can prime the mortgage is state-specific and drives how much weight the title-update and lien-waiver controls carry. Counsel item, always.
- Guaranty enforceability, replacement guarantor standards, and joint versus several liability among multiple guarantors are negotiated and state-specific. The skill lists what to look for; it does not opine on enforceability.
- Interest reserve on a stabilized or raw-land component. Generally not appropriate per the OCC handbook. Flag any budget that carries one.
- Take-out treated as certain. A standby commitment is priced to discourage use and a forward commitment is conditioned on completion and lease-up. Underwrite the no-takeout case.
- Loan modifications that increase the amount, change scope, or release part of the 15% contributed capital can re-trigger HVCRE treatment on a loan that had been conforming.

## Open Questions

- No public, non-anonymous source was located that publishes current market LTC caps, DSCR minimums, or debt yield floors for conventional U.S. construction debt. Those remain quote-driven and the skill refuses to default them.
- Construction loan pricing, extension fee levels, and guaranty burn-off percentages are not disclosed in any of the sources retrieved and are treated as negotiated terms to be captured from the term sheet.
- Life company construction-to-permanent program terms are described only generically in the OCC handbook's forward-commitment discussion. No individual life company program document was retrieved; the lane description is therefore structural, not quantitative.
- Whether the C&D contraction reported in the Q2 2026 QBP reflects tightening supply, weaker demand, or paydowns cannot be separated from the QBP alone; the SLOOS points to demand, but the two are not directly reconcilable at this level.
