# Construction Contracts and Draw Controls Research

## Purpose

- Supports `knowledge/construction-contracts-and-draw-controls.md`
- Also supports the Development and Construction pack skills that touch contract structure and draw mechanics, including `skills/development/gc-contract-and-change-order-reviewer.md` and `skills/development/construction-draw-and-cost-to-complete-reviewer.md`
- Intended users: developers, development managers, owner representatives, construction lenders and loan administrators, and equity partners reviewing a general contractor agreement or a monthly draw package

## U.S.-Only Assumptions

- Geography: United States. Lien law, retainage law, prompt-payment law, and bond requirements are state-specific, and federal projects add the Miller Act and FAR overlay.
- Deal type: U.S. ground-up development and heavy redevelopment financed with a construction loan, built under an owner-contractor agreement, with monthly draws funded against verified progress.
- Legal assumptions: this research supports educational issue spotting only. Contract interpretation, lien perfection, waiver sufficiency, and enforceability of liquidated damages require counsel licensed in the project state. Nothing here is legal, tax, accounting, or financing advice.
- Form assumptions: AIA forms are the most common U.S. private-project family and are used here as the reference vocabulary. ConsensusDocs, EJCDC, DBIA, and owner-drafted forms allocate the same risks under different numbering, and nearly every AIA form in the field is modified by supplementary conditions.

## Key Findings

### Contract families and what each allocates

- The A-series owner-contractor agreements set the basis of payment; A201-2017 supplies the general conditions that the agreement adopts by reference. The AIA summary describes A201 as the keystone document that allocates responsibilities among owner, contractor, and architect for medium and large projects.
- A101 (stipulated sum) fixes a lump sum that does not move with actual cost. Cost overrun risk sits with the contractor; savings stay with the contractor. It requires a fully designed, tightly defined scope.
- A102 (cost of the work plus a fee with a GMP) reimburses documented cost up to a ceiling. Per AIA's GMP guidance, if the cost of the work plus fee exceeds the GMP, the contractor pays the excess from its own funds. The owner gets open-book visibility and a cost cap but not a fixed price.
- A133-2019 is the construction manager as constructor form. AIA's instructions describe a two-phase structure: a preconstruction phase where the CM advises the owner, then a construction phase that begins when the owner accepts a GMP set out in the Guaranteed Maximum Price Amendment (Exhibit A), after which the CM is bound to complete at or below the GMP. Phases may run concurrently to fast-track.
- A134 is the same CMc relationship without a GMP. B101 is the owner-architect agreement and is where design scope, phases, and construction-phase administration duties live, so it determines who certifies pay applications and inspects for substantial completion.
- CMAA's owner guide separates the delivery method (design-bid-build, CM at risk, design-build, and variants) from the contracting format (fixed price, guaranteed maximum price, cost plus). The two choices are independent, and treating them as one is a frequent source of confusion in development files.

### Money mechanics inside the contract

- Allowances: A201 section 3.8 requires the contractor to include stated allowances in the contract sum, with the allowance covering materials and equipment delivered and related costs, and other contractor costs carried in the contract sum rather than the allowance. Section 3.8.3 requires the owner to select allowance items with reasonable promptness. Allowances are placeholders for undecided scope, not a contingency.
- Contingency: AIA's contingency guidance defines contingency as a predetermined amount or percentage held for unpredictable change, cites 5%-10% as common for construction contingency and 5%-10% of construction cost for design contingency, states that the contractor controls the contractor's contingency, and states that unused contingency reverts to the owner. It also warns that contingency is for unforeseen conditions, not for scope expansion or for patching an incomplete budget.
- Unit prices: A201 addresses unit pricing at 9.1.2 and in the Construction Change Directive machinery at 7.3, giving a pre-agreed rate for quantities that cannot be fixed at signing.
- Savings and buyout: AIA's GMP guidance notes some agreements return savings entirely to the owner and others split them per negotiated provisions. Common negotiated splits reported in practitioner commentary run from 50/50 to 70/30 in the owner's favor; that range is practitioner-reported, not a standard-form default.

### Changes

- A201 section 7.1.2 provides that a Change Order rests on agreement among owner, contractor, and architect, while a Construction Change Directive requires agreement of owner and architect and may or may not be agreed by the contractor.
- Section 7.3.1 defines the Construction Change Directive as a written order directing a change before the parties agree on the adjustment to contract sum or time; 7.3.2 confirms it is used in the absence of total agreement on Change Order terms; 7.3.6 requires the contractor to proceed promptly on receipt; 7.3.9 governs interim payment while the total cost is still being determined.
- Section 7.4 covers minor changes in the work, ordered by the architect without change to contract sum or time.
- Section 7.1.4 bars the contractor from making changes without prior authorization except in emergencies, which is the hook for the "unauthorized work is at contractor risk" position.

### Completion

- Section 9.8.1 defines Substantial Completion as the stage when the work or a designated portion is sufficiently complete that the owner can occupy or use it for its intended purpose. The contractor submits a punch list (9.8.2), the architect inspects (9.8.3), and the architect issues a Certificate of Substantial Completion that fixes the date, allocates responsibility for security, maintenance, heat, utilities, damage, and insurance, and sets the time to finish punch items (9.8.4).
- Section 9.9 allows partial occupancy or use before completion of the whole, with a joint inspection immediately prior, and confirms that partial occupancy does not by itself constitute acceptance.
- Section 9.10 conditions final payment and release of remaining retainage on final inspection and on certification and evidence that debts and claims are paid or satisfied and liens released. Section 9.10.4 provides that making final payment waives owner claims except stated carve-outs, and 9.10.5 provides that accepting final payment waives claims by the contractor, subcontractor, or supplier except those previously identified.
- Section 12.2.2.1 sets the one-year correction period running from Substantial Completion, requires prompt owner notice, and provides that failure to notify within the period waives the right to require correction. Section 12.2.2.2 extends the period for work first performed after Substantial Completion, and 12.2.5 confirms the correction period is not a statute of limitations.

### Payment applications and stored materials

- A201 section 9.2 requires a schedule of values, approved by the architect, that becomes the basis for applications for payment. Section 9.3.1 requires an itemized application prepared per the schedule of values, supported by the data the owner or architect requires, including releases and waivers of lien from subcontractors and suppliers, and reflecting retainage.
- Section 9.3.2 permits payment for materials and equipment delivered and suitably stored at the site, and for materials properly stored off site only if the owner approves in advance at an agreed location, conditioned on clear title, evidence of transfer of title free of liens, and evidence of insurance.
- Section 9.4 gives the architect seven days to certify, certify in part, or withhold; section 9.5 lists the grounds to withhold certification.
- G702 is the one-page application and certificate: the contractor applies and the architect certifies. G703 is the continuation sheet that carries each schedule of values line with work completed this period, previously completed, stored materials, retainage, and balance to finish. AIA publishes G702GMP and G703CW variants for cost-of-the-work GMP contracts.

### Retainage law

- Retainage percentages are statutory in many states and are moving. California SB 61 added Civil Code section 8811, capping retention on private construction contracts at 5% effective January 1, 2026 for contracts entered on or after that date, requiring negotiated reductions to flow down to subcontractors, exempting non-mixed-use residential projects of four stories or less and certain bonded-subcontract failures, and making the cap non-waivable with a mandatory fee award to the prevailing party.
- Texas takes the opposite approach on the owner side: Property Code section 53.101 requires the owner to reserve 10% of the contract price or of the value of work completed during the work and for 30 days after completion, as a fund for lien claimants.
- HUD's MAP Guide, Chapter 12, requires a 10% holdback on construction items until 50% completion for FHA-insured multifamily construction loans, and permits a stepped reduction after 50% completion to 5% until 75%, then to 2.5% until Final Endorsement, only where the contractor has no identity of interest greater than 5%, the surety consents in writing, and there are no questions about performance.

### Lien waivers

- Twelve states prescribe statutory waiver forms; California is the clearest model. The CSLB confirms four forms under Civil Code sections 8132 (conditional on progress payment), 8134 (unconditional on progress payment), 8136 (conditional on final payment), and 8138 (unconditional on final payment), and requires substantial compliance with the statutory form to be effective.
- Civil Code section 8132 provides that a conditional progress waiver is effective only on the claimant's actual receipt of payment, and voids non-conforming waivers. An unconditional waiver states that the claimant has been paid and is enforceable on signature even if payment never arrives, which is why unconditional waivers should trail funded payment rather than lead it.
- Rabbet's draw package standard reports that missing or incomplete lien waivers are the leading cause of draw rejection across the industry. That is a vendor observation, directional only, but it matches the sequencing logic above.

### Bonds and insurance

- On federal work, FAR 28.102-1 requires performance and payment bonds on construction contracts over $150,000 under 40 U.S.C. chapter 31, subchapter III (the Miller Act), and requires the contracting officer to select two or more alternative payment protections for contracts from $35,001 to $150,000. Private projects have no comparable federal mandate; bonding is a negotiated credit decision, though many state and local public-work statutes impose their own "little Miller Act" requirements.
- A201-2017 covers insurance and bonds in Article 11, but the AIA summary confirms the 2017 family moved most detailed insurance requirements into exhibits to the owner-contractor agreement (the A101 Exhibit A Insurance and Bonds), leaving waiver of subrogation and related fiduciary obligations in A201. Builder's risk is typically an owner-purchased property policy on the work in place plus, when negotiated, off-site and transit coverage.
- Wrap-up programs consolidate general liability and workers compensation for the whole project under one policy: an OCIP when the owner sponsors it, a CCIP when the general contractor does. Builder's risk, pollution, and professional coverage are commonly purchased alongside a wrap rather than inside it. This is broker and practitioner commentary, not a standard, and wrap economics only work at scale.

### Title and lender draw controls

- ALTA's 2021 Claims Guide chapter on construction liens explains that ALTA Endorsement 32 (with 32.1 and 32.2 variants keyed to who disburses) provides mechanic's lien priority coverage to the construction lender in lieu of the policy's Covered Risk 11(a), and that ALTA Endorsement 33 is the date-down issued with each subsequent advance, moving the Date of Coverage forward and increasing coverage for that advance. Each ALTA 33 protects only against lien rights accrued as of its Date of Coverage, which is why skipping date-downs on a draw creates a priority gap.
- HUD's MAP Guide illustrates the government version of lender draw control: interim advances are approved by the lender based on the HUD inspector's approval of the construction amount, using form HUD-92403 (Application for Insurance of Advance of Mortgage Proceeds), with the inspector recording percentage completion on the HUD Representative's Trip Report, form HUD-95379. HUD will not authorize interim advances exceeding documented percentage of completion less applicable retainage.
- Rabbet's practitioner standard describes a complete commercial draw package as roughly ten items: cover letter and funding request, index, sources and uses draw summary, pay application (G702/G703) or sworn statement, invoice summary, supporting invoices above the lender threshold, change order log, stored materials documentation, lien waivers, and certificates of insurance plus inspection and title updates. Sworn statement formats are required in Illinois, Michigan, and on HUD/FHA loans.

### Liquidated damages

- Ward and Smith's analysis of North Carolina law states the common two-prong test: damages must have been difficult to ascertain when the contract was made, and the stipulated amount must be a reasonable estimate of probable damages. Failing either prong makes the clause an unenforceable penalty, and the challenger must show damages were easy to measure or the amount shockingly excessive.
- The same source describes a rigid non-apportionment rule in that jurisdiction: an owner that bears any responsibility for delay cannot recover liquidated damages unless the contract expressly permits apportionment. Apportionment language is therefore a drafting issue, not a background rule.
- Practitioner commentary retrieved in the same search notes that liquidated damages are usually tied to substantial completion and that some jurisdictions allow them to run to final completion; this varies and is not a safe default.

## Benchmark and Formula Decisions

Suitable as repo defaults, each traceable above:

- Construction contingency of 5%-10% of hard cost and design contingency of 5%-10% of construction cost, cited as common ranges by AIA contingency guidance, presented as ranges and not as a required number.
- Retainage of 5% or 10% depending on state law and contract, with California private work capped at 5% for contracts on or after 2026-01-01 (Civ. Code 8811) and Texas requiring a 10% owner reserve during work and 30 days after (Tex. Prop. Code 53.101).
- HUD-insured multifamily construction: 10% holdback to 50% completion, with a permitted step-down to 5% at 50%, 2.5% at 75%, held to Final Endorsement, subject to the identity-of-interest, surety consent, and performance conditions in MAP Guide 12.15.5.
- Federal construction bonding threshold of contracts over $150,000 for performance and payment bonds, with alternative protections from $35,001 to $150,000 (FAR 28.102-1).
- One-year correction period from Substantial Completion as the A201-2017 default (12.2.2.1), extended for work first performed later (12.2.2.2), and expressly not a limitations period (12.2.5).
- Architect action on a payment application within seven days under A201 9.4.1.
- Draw package contents: schedule of values, G702/G703 or sworn statement, invoice detail, change order log, stored materials support, lien waivers, insurance certificates, inspector report, and title date-down. Supported jointly by A201 9.3, the Rabbet draw standard, HUD MAP Chapter 12, and the ALTA claims guide.
- Sequencing rule: conditional waivers accompany the current draw request, unconditional waivers follow proof of funded payment for the prior draw. Derived from Civ. Code 8132 and 8134 as described by CSLB and the statute.

Case-by-case only, stated as such in the knowledge base:

- Savings-sharing percentages on GMP contracts. The 50/50 to 70/30 range is practitioner-reported and negotiated; the AIA forms do not prescribe a split.
- Liquidated damages dollar amounts per day, and whether they run to substantial or final completion. Both are jurisdiction and project specific.
- Contractor fee percentages, general conditions as a percentage of cost, and preconstruction fees. No reliable single-number source was retrieved this session, so these stay a market-check item.
- Whether unconditional waivers are enforceable without payment, lien filing and notice deadlines, and whether a statutory waiver form is mandatory. All state-specific.
- Wrap-up (OCIP/CCIP) versus traditional insurance. Economics turn on project size, subcontractor rates, and claims history.

## Conflicting Source Resolution

- Retainage percentage: sources disagree because the law differs by state and by project type. A 10% default (Texas, HUD-insured multifamily) and a 5% cap (California private work on or after 2026-01-01, and similar recent New York and Washington legislation reported in the same search) are both correct in their own jurisdictions. Resolution: state the governing law as the controlling input, and present 5% and 10% as the two common poles rather than picking one.
- Federal bonding threshold: the Miller Act statute reads at more than $100,000 while FAR implements at over $150,000. Resolution: cite the regulation actually applied in procurement (FAR 28.102-1) and note that the statutory figure differs.
- Insurance location in the AIA family: the A201 copy read for this note carries detailed owner-modified insurance limits in Article 11, while the AIA summary states the 2017 family moved detailed insurance requirements into the owner-contractor agreement exhibits. Resolution: the AIA publisher's description of the unmodified form controls for the general rule, and the read copy is an example of how owners modify it. The practical instruction is to check both the general conditions and the agreement exhibit.
- Liquidated damages running past substantial completion: sources split by jurisdiction. Resolution: no default; flag as a counsel question.
- Contingency percentages: the AIA source gives 5%-10% for construction contingency and a worked 6% owner-contingency example from a single public project. Resolution: carry the range, not the single project figure.

## Edge Cases and Red Flags

- Owner-modified AIA forms. Almost every executed A201 in the field carries supplementary conditions. Section numbers alone are unsafe; read the actual executed document, including any "intentionally deleted" sections.
- Design-build and integrated project delivery reallocate design responsibility and do not map cleanly to the A101/A102/A133 vocabulary.
- Identity-of-interest general contractors. HUD restricts retainage release where the contractor holds more than a 5% equity interest in the ownership entity; private lenders and equity partners raise similar concerns about arm's-length pricing.
- Off-site stored materials. A201 9.3.2 permits payment only with advance owner approval, agreed location, clear title, lien-free transfer evidence, and insurance. Many lenders go further and refuse off-site stored material funding, or require bonding, UCC filings, and separate inspection.
- Advance lien waivers. California voids waivers signed before work is performed; other states differ. Do not treat a contract-embedded advance waiver as effective.
- Unconditional waivers delivered before funds clear. This is a documented pattern that leaves the payer holding a released claim with no proof of payment.
- Construction Change Directives that sit unpriced for months. The work proceeds, cost accrues, and the contract sum and time have not been adjusted, so the cost-to-complete and the schedule float in the draw package are both stale.
- Contingency drawn as a change-order line. If contingency is spent through change orders that also increase the GMP, the cap is not doing its job. Check whether contingency draws are inside or outside the GMP.
- Retainage reduced at 50% by rider without surety consent. In bonded work this can impair the bond. HUD requires prior written surety consent as a condition.
- Skipped title date-downs. Each ALTA 33 covers only lien rights accrued through its Date of Coverage. A missed date-down or an out-of-sequence disbursement can create an uninsured priority gap.
- Public work. Miller Act, little Miller Acts, prevailing wage certifications, and public prompt-payment statutes add requirements not present on private work.
