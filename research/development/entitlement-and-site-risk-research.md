# Entitlement and Site Risk Research

## Purpose

- Supports `knowledge/entitlement-and-site-risk.md`
- Also supports the Development and Construction pack skills that depend on entitlement status and site condition, principally `skills/development/site-and-entitlement-screen.md`, `skills/development/schedule-and-delivery-risk-tracker.md`, and `skills/development/development-ic-memo-writer.md`
- Intended users: developers, development managers, owner representatives, construction lenders, and equity partners screening U.S. ground-up and heavy redevelopment sites

## U.S.-Only Assumptions

- Geography: United States, including states with additional state-level environmental review regimes such as California and New York
- Deal type: ground-up development and heavy redevelopment across multifamily, industrial, retail, office, and mixed-use
- Legal assumptions: land use is governed locally under state enabling statutes, so procedures, hearing calendars, notice periods, and fee schedules vary jurisdiction by jurisdiction. Federal overlays (Clean Water Act, CERCLA, Endangered Species Act, National Historic Preservation Act, NFIP) apply nationally but are triggered by project-specific facts
- This is educational decision support, not legal, environmental, engineering, or investment advice. Local land use counsel and licensed environmental and geotechnical professionals control

## Key Findings

### Zoning and land use frame

- Approvals fall on a spectrum from ministerial to discretionary. By-right and administrative site plan approval carry schedule and cost risk; rezonings, PUDs, variances, and conditional use permits add political risk because a legislative or quasi-judicial body can say no on the record.
- APA PAS Report 545 describes three PUD approval routes: the base zone alternative, the rezoning alternative, and approval as a conditional use, typically with a two-stage preliminary and final plan review. Negotiation and discretion are structural features of the PUD, not defects in a particular jurisdiction.
- Site plan review is a statutory creature in many states. New York Town Law 274-a is a concrete example: the planning board may impose reasonable conditions directly related to the site plan, notice runs 10 days to the applicant and 5 days by publication, and the board must decide within 62 days of application or of the close of hearing, extendable only by consent. Numbers like these are jurisdiction-specific and must be read from the local code and state enabling statute.
- Density bonus and inclusionary programs are simultaneously an entitlement lever and a cost. California's State Density Bonus Law (HCD fact sheet, April 2026) requires only 5% very low-income, 10% low-income, or 10% for-sale moderate-income units to unlock a 20% to 50% density increase on a sliding scale, an 80% bonus for qualifying 100% affordable projects, one to five concessions or incentives by affordability tier, waivers of standards that physically preclude the project, and capped parking ratios (generally 1 space for studio/1-bedroom, 1.5 for 2-3 bedroom, 2.5 for 4-plus bedroom, with deeper reductions near major transit). No other state's program can be assumed to mirror it.

### Entitlement timeline and community process risk

- NAHB and NMHC found that community opposition, separate from regulation itself, added an average of 5.6% to total multifamily development cost and 7.4 months to delivery in their April 2022 survey. Sample size was 49 usable responses, so treat this as directional.
- The same study found 47.9% of multifamily developers avoid jurisdictions with policies such as inclusionary zoning and 87.5% avoid jurisdictions with rent control. Political climate shows up as site selection, not just as schedule.
- State environmental review regimes create their own clock. NYSDEC's SEQR process contemplates 30 days to designate a lead agency, 20 days for a determination of significance, 60 days to a final EIS scope, a 45-day adequacy review of the draft EIS, a minimum 30-day comment period, and findings 30 days after the final EIS. These are statutory floors on a positive declaration path, not a schedule.

### Impact, linkage, and exaction fees

- Sheetz v. County of El Dorado (decided April 12, 2024, unanimous) held that the Takings Clause does not distinguish between legislative and administrative permit conditions, so legislatively adopted monetary exactions are subject to the Nollan essential nexus and Dolan rough proportionality tests. The Court expressly did not decide whether a condition imposed on a class of properties must be individualized to the same degree as one aimed at a single development.
- Fee magnitude is material. NAHB reported (June 9, 2026) that regulation adds $131,734 to the price of an average new single-family home, 26.4% of the $499,500 average price, split $46,795 (9.4%) at the land development stage and $84,939 (17.0%) during construction.
- For multifamily, the 2022 NAHB/NMHC survey attributed 40.6% of total development cost to regulation, with fees and required studies at the start of site work averaging 8.7% of total development cost when present (reported by 98.0% of respondents), fees at building permit averaging 4.6%, land dedicated to government or left unbuilt averaging 4.7% when present (51.0% of respondents), and inclusionary mandates averaging 6.9% when present (38.8% of respondents), with those respondents reporting a 7.6% average rent increase to absorb them.

### Environmental site review

- A Phase I Environmental Site Assessment is the standard entry point. EPA recognizes ASTM E1527-21 (and ASTM E2247-23 for forestland or rural property) as satisfying All Appropriate Inquiries under 40 CFR Part 312. AAI supports the bona fide prospective purchaser, innocent landowner, and contiguous property owner defenses under CERCLA.
- Report viability matters for closing schedules: AAI must be conducted or updated within one year before acquisition, and the interviews, records review, visual inspection, and lien and title search components must occur within 180 days of acquisition.
- E1527-21 adds emphasis on emerging contaminants such as PFAS as non-scope considerations that may still warrant inclusion where a state regulates them.
- Phase II subsurface investigation is not standardized to a single national trigger; it follows from recognized environmental conditions identified in the Phase I and from the environmental professional's judgment. Keep it case-by-case.
- Wetlands and waters: Section 404 permits are required for discharge of dredged or fill material into waters of the United States including wetlands. USACE issues permits, EPA sets the environmental criteria and retains veto authority, and the applicant must show avoidance, then minimization, then compensation for unavoidable impacts. Nationwide Permit 39 covers commercial and institutional development but caps loss of non-tidal waters at 1/2 acre and stream bed loss at 300 linear feet, and requires a pre-construction notification. The 2026 NWPs were finalized January 8, 2026, take effect March 15, 2026, and expire March 15, 2031.
- Flood: 44 CFR 60.3 requires participating communities to permit all development in the Special Flood Hazard Area, elevate the lowest floor of new residential construction to or above base flood elevation, allow non-residential structures to elevate or dry floodproof to that level, and prohibit floodway encroachment unless an analysis shows no increase in flood elevation. Many communities adopt freeboard above the federal minimum; that is local.
- Endangered species: a non-federal project with potential take of a listed species can require an ESA Section 10 incidental take permit supported by a Habitat Conservation Plan, which itself triggers NEPA and public review. Timelines are project-specific and long; this research did not find a reliable published median.
- Construction stormwater: an NPDES permit is required for construction disturbing 1 acre or more, or less than 1 acre if part of a larger common plan of development or sale that will ultimately disturb 1 acre or more. A SWPPP must be prepared before the Notice of Intent is filed. Most states are authorized to run their own NPDES program, so the operative permit is usually a state general permit rather than EPA's.

### Utilities, offsite obligations, and geotechnical

- Will-serve letters are the practical evidence that water, sewer, power, and gas capacity exists and is committed. Practitioner commentary (Area Development, Q2 2024) treats obtaining them early, along with right-of-way acquisition, as core to controlling site risk. Search results indicate many planning agencies will not accept an entitlement application without them; that is a jurisdiction-specific requirement, not a national rule.
- Offsite obligations (road widening, signals, sewer upsizing, lift stations, dedications) are usually documented in a development agreement or subdivision improvement agreement with bonding or letter-of-credit security. Treat them as hard cost with a schedule, not as a contingency line.
- Geotechnical: IBC Section 1803 requires a geotechnical investigation with defined scope (soil classification, slope stability, bearing capacity, moisture variation, compressibility, liquefaction potential, expansiveness), and permits the building official to waive it only in narrow cases such as satisfactory adjacent data or unoccupied structures. A soil is expansive if it has a plasticity index of 15 or greater per ASTM D4318, more than 10% passing a No. 200 sieve, and more than 10% of particles under 5 micrometers, or alternatively an expansion index above 20 per ASTM D4829.

### Historic, design, and state review regimes

- Section 106 of the National Historic Preservation Act is triggered by a federal undertaking, which includes projects that receive federal financial assistance, permits, licenses, or approvals. The process is set out at 36 CFR Part 800 and is administered by the ACHP with the State Historic Preservation Office. A privately funded project with no federal nexus is not subject to Section 106, but may still face local landmark or design review.
- California's AB 130, signed and effective June 30, 2025, created a statutory CEQA exemption (Public Resources Code Section 21080.66) for housing-rich infill: at least two-thirds residential square footage, sites up to 20 acres inside incorporated municipalities or Census urban areas, previously developed or surrounded by urban uses, consistent with zoning and general plan, a Phase I ESA required, no demolition of a historic-register structure, and a prevailing wage trigger above 85 feet. SB 131 narrows CEQA review for near-miss projects to the single condition that defeated the exemption.
- New York's SEQRA sorts actions into Type I (presumed more likely to have significant impact, full EAF, mandatory coordinated review), Type II (no further review), and Unlisted (short or full EAF, determination of significance required).

### Political and litigation risk

- CEQA litigation exposure is genuinely contested. The Rose Foundation-commissioned study (May 2023) found a 1.9% litigation rate across projects with a CEQA review document for 2013-2021, 508 cases filed 2019-2021, of which 121 challenged actual proposed housing units, and an average of 192 lawsuits per year since 2002. Holland and Knight's Hernandez research (August 2022) reported 47,999 housing units targeted by CEQA lawsuits filed in 2020 and framed that as roughly half of California's annual housing production.
- Both can be true. Litigation is rare per project and concentrated on large projects, which is precisely the population a developer with a large infill site belongs to.

## Benchmark and Formula Decisions

Suitable as repo defaults because they are fixed federal or state legal thresholds:

- Phase I / AAI viability: 1 year overall, 180 days for the interview, records, visual inspection, and lien search components (EPA)
- NPDES construction stormwater threshold: 1 acre disturbed, or under 1 acre within a larger common plan of development or sale (EPA)
- NWP 39 caps: 1/2 acre non-tidal waters loss, 300 linear feet stream bed, PCN required; 2026 NWPs effective 2026-03-15 to 2031-03-15 (USACE)
- NFIP minimums: permit for all SFHA development, lowest floor at or above BFE for new residential, elevate-or-dry-floodproof for non-residential, floodway no-rise (44 CFR 60.3)
- IBC expansive soil criteria: PI 15 or greater, plus the No. 200 sieve and 5-micrometer fractions, or expansion index above 20 (IBC 1803)
- Sheetz: legislative monetary exactions are subject to Nollan/Dolan nexus and rough proportionality (U.S. Supreme Court, 2024-04-12)

Directional only, must carry a date and source in the text:

- 40.6% of multifamily development cost attributable to regulation, and its components (NAHB/NMHC, 2022, n=49)
- 5.6% cost and 7.4 months of delay attributed to community opposition (NAHB/NMHC, 2022)
- $131,734 and 26.4% of new single-family price attributable to regulation (NAHB, 2026-06-09)
- 1.9% CEQA litigation rate (Rose Foundation, 2023)

Must remain case-by-case with that said explicitly:

- Entitlement duration for any specific approval type. No national dataset in this research supports a default months-to-entitlement figure. SEQR statutory intervals and New York's 62-day site plan clock are examples of how a specific regime is structured, not a national norm.
- Impact fee amounts. These are set by local schedule and vary by jurisdiction, use, and unit or square-foot basis.
- Phase II ESA trigger, scope, and cost. Driven by recognized environmental conditions and professional judgment.
- ESA Section 10 / HCP duration.
- Whether will-serve letters are a filing prerequisite.
- Freeboard above BFE, inclusionary percentages outside California, and design review standards.

## Conflicting Source Resolution

- CEQA litigation exposure: the Rose Foundation study (1.9% of projects litigated) and Holland and Knight's Hernandez research (nearly half of 2020 housing units targeted) use different denominators. The first measures the share of projects sued; the second measures the share of units in sued projects. Both are advocacy-adjacent, from opposite directions. Resolution: present both, and tell the user that per-project litigation frequency is low while unit-weighted exposure on large projects is high, so a big infill project should not underwrite the low per-project rate.
- Impact fee constitutionality after Sheetz: some commentary reads the decision as opening broad challenges to legislative fee schedules; the opinion itself and Justice Kavanaugh's concurrence preserve the ordinary practice of imposing conditions through reasonable formulas or schedules. Resolution: the judicial opinion text controls over the commentary, and the knowledge base states the open question rather than a rule.
- ASTM standard for rural property: one law firm summary referenced E2247-16; the EPA page states E2247-23. Resolution: the agency page controls.
- NWP 39 numeric limits: the direct USACE PDF returned 403 on fetch, so the 1/2-acre and 300-linear-foot limits are taken from the USACE PDF as summarized in search results and corroborated by Sidley's January 2026 client alert, which confirms the half-acre loss limit and PCN requirement survived the 2026 reissuance. Flagged in the source table.

## Edge Cases and Red Flags

- Federal nexus creates review that a private-money project would not face. A project that takes federal funds, a federal loan guarantee, or a Section 404 individual permit can pull in NEPA and Section 106, adding months. Screen for the nexus before assuming none.
- A Phase I that ages past the AAI windows before closing does not just need a cover-letter update. The 180-day components have to be redone.
- Nationwide permit thresholds are cliffs. A design that crosses 1/2 acre of fill drops out of NWP 39 into an individual permit with public notice and a materially longer timeline.
- Floodway is different from floodplain. Development in the SFHA is a design and cost problem; development in the regulatory floodway must show no rise, which can be a project killer.
- Density bonus concessions are not automatic approvals. They are entitlements to specific relief that a jurisdiction can deny on defined findings, and California's structure does not travel to other states.
- Vested rights and entitlement expiration. Approvals lapse, phasing conditions run, and a development agreement may or may not vest fees. Local counsel controls.
- Conditions of approval discovered after closing. Recorded conditions, prior development agreements, and annexation agreements can bind a site independent of current zoning.
- Utility capacity that exists on paper but is allocated. A will-serve letter with an expiration date or a capacity reservation fee is a different asset from a general statement of adequacy.
- Geotechnical surprises in redevelopment: undocumented fill, prior foundations, and buried tanks are common on infill sites and are usually outside a Phase I's scope.
- Any project relying on a legislative approval before a discretionary body should be treated as unentitled until the appeal and referendum periods run.
